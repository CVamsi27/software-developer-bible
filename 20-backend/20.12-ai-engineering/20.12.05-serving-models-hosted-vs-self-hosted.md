# Serving Models — Hosted APIs vs Self-Hosted

>
> **Why it matters:** "We just use the OpenAI SDK" ends interviews; a decision matrix with numbers starts them. This chapter pairs with LLM App Architecture (where the provider abstraction sits) and Safety, Cost & Governance (why residency is a hard constraint, not a preference). The GDPR angle is a live interview topic for EU-bound roles: Article 44 transfers, DPAs, and the right to deletion shape architecture.

## Definition

**Hosted serving**: you consume models through a vendor's API; inference hardware, batching, scaling, and model updates are the vendor's problem; your controls are parameters, prompts, and contractual (DPA, zero-retention flags).

**Self-hosting**: you run open-weight model binaries on your own (or cloud-tenant) GPUs behind an inference server (vLLM, TGI, SGLang); you own capacity planning, quantization choices, cold starts, upgrades, and every byte that touches the model.

```text
HOSTED                                SELF-HOSTED
app ──► api.vendor.com ──► their GPUs app ──► your LB ──► vLLM pods ──► your GPUs
        (data crosses boundary)              (data stays inside VPC/on-prem)
control: prompt + params + contract   control: weights, quantization,
                                       batching, region, everything
```

## First Principles

Serving decisions follow from the ownership-vs-economics trade:

1. **Hosted APIs rent expertise and scale; self-hosting rents you nothing but control.** Hosted: provider-operated inference, integration/governance work, per-token cost, data leaves your boundary. Self-host: data sovereignty, fixed cost at volume, latency control — with a capability/quality profile that must be evaluated for the actual task. The choice is per-use-case (data sensitivity × volume × capability need), not ideological.
2. **Inference cost is physics**: GPU memory bounds batch size; batching bounds throughput; throughput bounds cost-per-token. Self-hosters live in this math (quantization, batching, caching); API users experience it as pricing tiers (batch APIs, prompt caching, → 20.12.01 axiom 1).
3. **The abstraction boundary is the prompt/tool interface** (→ 20.12.02): designing to a provider-agnostic contract (LiteLLM-style gateways) keeps switching possible — the same contract-first discipline as OpenAPI (→ 20.3.1.05), applied to model providers whose pricing and capability change quarterly.

## Why Do We Need It?

1. **Cost curves cross** — hosted tokens look cheap at 10k requests/month and punitive at 50M tokens/day; self-host GPU cost is fixed, so per-token cost falls with utilization. Choosing wrong by one order of magnitude of traffic is a budget incident.
2. **Data residency can be non-negotiable** — Contractual or sector-specific residency rules may require in-region/private processing. GDPR Chapter V does not impose a blanket ban on transfers outside the EEA: transfers need an applicable legal mechanism and appropriate protection, alongside the other processing obligations.
3. **Latency profiles differ structurally** — hosted adds network RTT plus shared-capacity queuing; self-hosted colocates inference next to your services and lets you tune batch windows. Real-time voice/UI workloads feel TTFT deltas of even 200 ms.
4. **Availability is multi-dimensional** — providers have outages, rate limits, and silent model deprecations; a router with fallbacks (and optionally a self-hosted fallback tier) turns vendor incidents into blips.
5. **Model choice and reproducibility** — hosted models change or retire under you (behavior drift without version pins); pinned open weights give reproducible evals and no surprise capability removals.
6. **Negotiation leverage** — teams with a working self-hosted path negotiate hosted pricing from a credible walk-away position.

## How It Works

### Decision matrix

| Dimension | Hosted API | Self-hosted open weights |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Time to first token shipped | Hours | Weeks (infra, evals, guardrails) |
| Quality ceiling | Frontier models first | Task-specific; compare eligible models on your eval suite |
| Unit economics at scale | Linear in usage; discounts at commit | Fixed GPUs; cheap at high utilization |
| Latency p95 | Network + provider queue variance | Tunable; predictable in-region/colo |
| Data residency / GDPR | Deployment regions plus processing/retention/transfer terms to verify | Deployment control; verify telemetry, storage, administration, and dependencies too |
| Control over serving | Almost none (params + prompt) | Quantization, batching, scheduling, caching layers |
| Ops burden | Provider-operated inference; your integration, security, and governance remain | GPUs, drivers, CUDA versions, scaling, upgrades, on-call |
| Model lifecycle | Deprecations forced on you | You choose upgrade timing; pin and re-eval |
| Compliance story | Vendor attestations (SOC2, ISO) | Your attestations — full auditability |

Interview soundbite: *hosted is OPEX for intelligence; self-hosted is CAPEX plus a platform team — pick per workload class, not per company.*

### vLLM basics: PagedAttention and continuous batching

Two ideas make modern open-weight serving economically viable:

- **PagedAttention** (vLLM): the KV cache — every sequence's attention history — is stored in fixed-size blocks (pages) instead of contiguous preallocated buffers sized to `max_seq_len`. Block allocation reduces KV-cache fragmentation and enables sharing/reuse; gains depend on the workload and serving configuration.
- **Continuous batching**: instead of waiting for a whole batch of requests to finish before admitting new ones (static batching), the scheduler admits new requests at every iteration boundary as others finish. GPU utilization stays high under mixed-length traffic.

```text
STATIC BATCHING                    CONTINUOUS BATCHING (vLLM)
t0 [A A A A]                       t0 [A A B C]
t1 [A A _ _]  ← idle slots waste   t1 [A A B C D]   ← D admitted as A/B finish
t2 [A _ _ _]     GPU cycles        t2 [A E F G D]   ← slots refilled each iter
t3 done → admit next batch         ...measure throughput and tail latency under the same workload/SLO
```

Also worth naming: **prefix caching** (reuse KV for eligible identical token prefixes), **chunked prefill**, speculative decoding, and tensor parallelism across GPUs for models larger than single-GPU VRAM.

### Quantization ladder

Weights stored at lower precision = less VRAM, faster memory-bound decode, some quality loss:

| Format | Bits/weight | ~Quality vs fp16 | Typical use |
| ------------------------------ | ----------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------- |
| fp32 | 32 | baseline (training) | Rarely served |
| fp16/bf16 | 16 | reference (bf16 safer numerically) | Default for hosted-class serving with headroom |
| int8 or FP8 (distinct formats) | 8 | Task/model/hardware-dependent; measure it | Throughput bump on H100/L4-class HW |
| int4 GPTQ | 4 | small but real degradation on hard tasks | Single-GPU fits for 30–70B models |
| int4 AWQ | 4 | Activation-aware calibration; quality depends on model/task/kernel | Popular default for 4-bit serving |

Rules of thumb: bf16 → int4 roughly quarters weight memory with modest quality loss on most benchmarks, but run YOUR eval suite before committing — coding/math tasks degrade sooner than chat. Lower precisions exist but require especially careful task/hardware evaluation; there is no universal quality cutoff.

### GPU sizing rough math

```text
VRAM(model)  = params × bytes_per_weight          bf16: ×2,  int8: ×1,  int4: ×0.5
VRAM(KV)     = 2 × layers × kv_heads × head_dim × seq_len × batch × bytes
               (×2 because K and V; fp16 assumed)
Total        = VRAM(model) + KV peak + activations (~5-15%) + CUDA overhead (~1-2 GB)

Worked example — Llama-3.1 70B, 8k context, bf16:
  weights: 70e9 × 2 B            ≈ 140 GB  → needs 2×80GB (tensor parallel) or int4 (≈35GB, 1×A100/H100 80)
  KV cache: 2 × 80 layers × 8 kv_heads × 128 head_dim × 8192 tok × 2 B × batch
          ≈ 2.68 GB (2.5 GiB) per concurrent request (GQA keeps this sane)
  → 20 such requests add about 54 GB KV to about 140 GB weights before overhead; they do not fit in 160 GB. Tensor-parallel sharding, quantization, and measured headroom determine a feasible concurrency.
```

Practical shortcuts interviewers accept: a 7–8B model fits a 24 GB consumer/A10 card at bf16 or a T4 at int8; 70B wants 2×80 GB at bf16 or 1×48 GB at AWQ-int4 with short contexts; KV cache — not weights — is what usually caps concurrency.

### Throughput vs latency vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Why it matters |
| ---------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| TTFT | Time To First Token — queue + prefill latency | Perceived responsiveness; streaming UX target (<500 ms chat, <250 ms voice) |
| TPOT / ITL | Time Per Output Token (inter-token latency) | Reading-speed feel; <50 ms feels instant |
| Prefill | Processing the prompt (compute-bound, parallel) | Dominated by input length; prefix caching cuts it |
| Decode | Generating tokens one-by-one (memory-bandwidth-bound) | Dominated by output length × TPOT |
| Throughput | Tokens/sec aggregate across all users | Cost efficiency metric; batching raises it |
| Goodput | Throughput measured only on requests meeting SLO | What you actually sell; vLLM benchmarks report it |

The tension: larger admitted batches can raise throughput while increasing queueing/decode latency; the curve depends on workload and scheduler — SLO-aware schedulers maximize goodput, not raw throughput.

### Routing and fallback across providers

Production clients treat model providers like any unreliable dependency:

```text
request ──► router ──► policy select ──► primary (hosted-A)
              │            │                │ fail/timeout/429?
              │            │                ▼
              │            │           fallback (hosted-B)
              │            │                │ fail?
              │            │                ▼
              │            │           self-hosted vLLM (degraded-but-alive tier)
              │            ▼
              └──► PII classifier decides tier FIRST (residency policy),
                   availability logic second — never route regulated data
                   to a non-compliant endpoint just because it's up
```

Key mechanics: health-aware pools, hedging (duplicate slow requests), retry budgets, response-schema compatibility checks across models (structured outputs differ!), and cost-aware routing (cheap model first, escalate on judge/confidence signals).

### Self-host operational realities

- **Cold GPUs**: scale-from-zero takes minutes (image pull, weight load from disk/network into VRAM — tens of GB); keep warm floors, use autoscaling on queue depth not CPU, snapshot volumes with weights preloaded.
- **Capacity planning**: KV cache caps concurrency; size for p99 context length, not average; load-test with realistic mixed lengths.
- **Model updates**: new checkpoint ≠ drop-in — rerun evals, shadow-test against current, canary by traffic slice; keep rollback images.
- **Driver/CUDA/version matrix**: pin everything; a CUDA mismatch takes down a fleet at 2 a.m. more reliably than any model bug.
- **Multi-tenancy**: one GPU pool, many models — swap-in-time costs (model reload) vs memory (co-resident LoRA adapters are cheap; full models are not).
- **Observability**: tokens/sec, TTFT/TPOT histograms, KV-cache eviction rate, queue depth, goodput per SLO class.

### Hybrid patterns (the usual ending)

1. **Hosted for general, self-hosted for PII**: classifier routes; regulated payloads go to in-VPC vLLM; everything else enjoys frontier quality. One possible policy, subject to the actual residency/processing contract.
2. **Hosted primary, self-hosted fallback**: open-weight tier keeps you alive through provider outages and rate-limit storms.
3. **Self-hosted bulk, hosted premium**: routine summarization/extraction on your GPUs; gnarly reasoning escalates to the frontier API.
4. **In-region managed endpoints** (Azure OpenAI EU, Bedrock regions, Google EU): hosted convenience with residency attestation — middle path when ops capacity is thin.

## Code Examples

### Provider router with fallback chains and residency tiers

```typescript
// llm/router.ts
type Provider = {
  name: string;
  approvedDataClasses: readonly string[]; // verified policy, not a location nickname               // compliance attribute FIRST
  complete(req: CompletionRequest): Promise<CompletionResponse>;
};

type RoutePolicy = { primary: string; fallbacks: string[] };

const POLICIES: Record<TaskClass, RoutePolicy> = {
  general:    { primary: "openai-eu",  fallbacks: ["anthropic-eu", "vllm-self"] },
  regulated:  { primary: "vllm-self",  fallbacks: ["azure-openai-de"] },  // EU-only endpoints
  background: { primary: "vllm-self",  fallbacks: ["openai-eu"] },        // cheap bulk tier
};

export async function complete(req: CompletionRequest & { taskClass: TaskClass }): Promise<CompletionResponse> {
  const policy = POLICIES[req.taskClass];
  const chain = [policy.primary, ...policy.fallbacks].map(n => {
    const provider = registry[n];
    if (!provider) throw new Error(`unknown provider ${n}`);
    return provider;
  });

  let lastError: unknown;
  for (const provider of chain) {
    if (!provider.approvedDataClasses.includes(req.dataClass)) continue;   // hard residency gate
    const breaker = breakers.for(provider.name);
    if (breaker.isOpen()) continue;
    try {
      const res = await withTimeout(provider.complete(req), req.timeoutMs ?? 30_000);
      breaker.recordSuccess();
      metrics.route(provider.name, req.taskClass);
      return res;
    } catch (e) {
      lastError = e;
      breaker.recordFailure();                                      // 429/5xx/timeouts trip it
      warn("provider_fallback", { provider: provider.name, err: String(e) });
    }
  }
  throw new AllProvidersFailedError(chain.map(p => p.name), lastError);
}
```

Note the ordering: compliance gates filter the chain before availability logic ever runs.

### OpenAI-compatible client pointed at self-hosted vLLM

```typescript
// llm/vllm-client.ts — vLLM speaks the OpenAI protocol, so the SDK just works
import OpenAI from "openai";

const local = new OpenAI({
  baseURL: process.env.VLLM_BASE_URL ?? "http://vllm.internal:8000/v1",
  apiKey: process.env.VLLM_API_KEY ?? "unused",
});

export async function summarizePII(text: string): Promise<string> {
  const completion = await local.chat.completions.create({
    model: process.env.VLLM_SERVED_MODEL!, // advertised server name; pin weights at deployment
    messages: [
      { role: "system", content: "Summarize support tickets in <=3 sentences." },
      { role: "user", content: text },
    ],
    max_tokens: 200,
    temperature: 0.2,
  });
  return completion.choices[0]?.message?.content ?? "";
}
```

Same interface for hosted and self-hosted means the router above composes both without adapter sprawl.

### VRAM sizing calculator

```typescript
// llm/sizing.ts
type SizeInput = {
  paramsB: number;                 // e.g. 70 for 70B
  bitsPerWeight: 16 | 8 | 4;
  layers: number;
  kvHeads: number;                 // after GQA
  headDim: number;
  ctxLen: number;
  concurrency: number;
  gpuVramGiB: number; // all calculated capacities below use binary GiB
};

export function estimateVram(i: SizeInput) {
  const weightsGiB = (i.paramsB * 1e9 * i.bitsPerWeight / 8) / 1024 ** 3;
  const kvPerReqGiB =
    (2 * i.layers * i.kvHeads * i.headDim * i.ctxLen * 2 /*bytes per bf16 element; leading 2 counts K and V*/) /
    (1024 ** 3);
  const kvTotalGiB = kvPerReqGiB * i.concurrency;
  const overheadGiB = 2 + 0.10 * (weightsGiB + kvTotalGiB);          // CUDA + activations
  const totalGiB = weightsGiB + kvTotalGiB + overheadGiB;
  return {
    weightsGiB: round(weightsGiB),
    kvPerRequestGiB: round(kvPerReqGiB),
    totalGiB: round(totalGiB),
    fitsOneGpu: totalGiB <= i.gpuVramGiB,
    idealAggregateLowerBound: Math.ceil(totalGiB / i.gpuVramGiB) // not a deployability guarantee,
  };
}

// estimateVram({ paramsB: 70, bitsPerWeight: 4, layers: 80, kvHeads: 8,
//   headDim: 128, ctxLen: 8192, concurrency: 16, gpuVramGiB: 80 })
// → weights ≈ 33 GiB, KV ≈ 40 GiB, total ≈ 82 GiB → aggregate lower bound 2 GPUs (or cut context/concurrency)
```

### Deploying vLLM (the operational minimum)

```bash
# Set these to an eval-approved image digest, compatible model repository, and commit.
: "${VLLM_IMAGE:?pin vLLM image by digest}"
: "${MODEL_ID:?choose a supported model/checkpoint}"
: "${MODEL_REVISION:?pin the Hugging Face commit}"
: "${VLLM_API_KEY:?configure authentication}"
docker run --gpus all --ipc=host \
  -p 127.0.0.1:8000:8000 \
  -e VLLM_API_KEY \
  "$VLLM_IMAGE" \
  --model "$MODEL_ID" --revision "$MODEL_REVISION" \
  --tensor-parallel-size 2 --max-model-len 16384 \
  --gpu-memory-utilization 0.90 --enable-prefix-caching
```

The image entrypoint/flags, tensor-parallel compatibility, model licensing, tokenizer/chat template, and quantization kernels must be verified for the pinned release. The memory utilization setting allocates a budget; it is not a proof against OOM. Keep the endpoint private and test authentication rather than using an unverified model id or a mutable `latest` tag.

### PII-based routing policy (hybrid pattern glue)

```typescript
// llm/pii-router.ts
export async function route(request: ChatRequest): Promise<CompletionResponse> {
  const scan = await piiScan(request.messagesText());             // detector service
  const taskClass: TaskClass =
    scan.hasRegulatedPii ? "regulated" :                          // never leaves boundary
    request.priority === "quality" ? "general" :                  // frontier tier
    "background";                                                 // self-hosted bulk tier

  const res = await complete({ ...request, taskClass, dataClass: request.authoritativeDataClass });
  audit.log({ taskClass, providers: res.viaChain, piiTypes: scan.types });
  // An interaction log is not the full GDPR Article 30 record of processing activities.
  return res;
}
```

## Real-World Use Cases

1. **EU SaaS helpdesk (GDPR)** — tickets contain names, emails, sometimes health mentions; regulated class routes to in-Frankfurt vLLM serving Llama-70B-AWQ; general queries hit a hosted EU endpoint; DPA + zero-retention documented; an interaction audit complements the separate Article 30 record of processing activities.
2. **High-volume document pipeline** — compare provisioned GPU utilization, operations, and measured task quality against eligible hosted pricing; escalate only where the fallback clears the quality and policy bar.
3. **Real-time voice agent** — TTFT budget 300 ms forces self-hosted (or dedicated provisioned throughput) with small model + speculative decoding; hosted shared queues blow the budget during peaks.
4. **Startup MVP** — 100% hosted with provider abstraction behind one interface (this chapter's router); revisit self-hosting when monthly spend passes roughly the cost of one GPU node plus 0.3 FTE ops — calculate the crossover from current prices, utilization, staff time, and availability requirements.
5. **Fintech on-call copilot** — hybrid #2: hosted primary for reasoning quality; an evaluated self-hosted fallback can reduce provider dependency; test its capacity and quality under the full outage load; circuit breakers shared with the orchestration layer.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Fix |
| ------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Comparing hosted token price to GPU sticker price only | Include ops FTE, idle/cold capacity, eval burden — TCO at YOUR utilization |
| Sizing GPUs by weights alone, ignoring KV cache | `weights + KV(concurrency × p99 ctx)`; KV usually caps concurrency first |
| Serving int4 because a benchmark said "no quality loss" | Run your own eval suite per task class; coding/math degrade before chat does |
| One provider, hardcoded client | Provider-agnostic interface + fallback chain + circuit breakers from day one |
| Routing regulated data to whatever endpoint is healthy | Residency/compliance gate runs BEFORE availability routing |
| Not pinning model revisions (hosted or open) | Pin exact versions everywhere; deprecation notices go to your calendar, not your outage channel |
| Autoscaling GPUs on CPU/RPS | Scale on queue depth / TTFT SLO burn-up; keep warm floor to hide cold-start minutes |
| Treating model update as config change | Shadow + canary + eval gate + rollback image, same discipline as DB migrations |
| Ignoring structured-output differences across fallback models | Schema-validate every model's JSON; test the full fallback chain, not just the primary |
| Max-model-len set to "be safe" huge value | Right-size context; long admitted sequences increase KV demand; configured maximum context and admission policy both need a realistic load test |

## Best Practices

1. **Decide per workload class** (chat, extraction, voice, regulated), never once for the company; the answer is legitimately different inside one codebase.
2. **Keep one internal completion interface** so hosted/self-hosted/fallback tiers compose freely (router example above).
3. **Quantize one step at a time** (fp16 → int8 → int4) with eval gates between; compare compatible quantization methods on the chosen hardware and task.
4. **Budget VRAM explicitly**: weights + KV at p99 context × target concurrency + 10% overhead; write the math down like the calculator above.
5. **Optimize goodput under SLO**, not raw tokens/sec — TTFT/TPOT targets first, then batch size/prefix caching to raise throughput within them.
6. **For EU PII**: verify processing regions, remote access, subprocessors, retention, transfer mechanisms, and deployment controls together; log processing activities and retention per Art. 30 anyway.
7. **Pin model revisions and re-run trajectory evals** on every upgrade — treat weights as dependencies with lockfiles.
8. **Load-test with realistic length mixes**; synthetic uniform prompts flatter static batching and lie about p99 TTFT.
9. **Plan the exit of every provider**: exportable prompts, portable eval sets, a tested fallback tier — leverage and resilience in one move.

## Performance Considerations

- Decode is memory-bandwidth-bound: batch size raises throughput until bandwidth saturates; beyond that, more concurrency only inflates TPOT.
- Prefix caching is free money for system-prompt-heavy apps (agents! RAG!) — enable it hosted-side too where offered (prompt caching APIs).
- Chunked prefill stops long prompts from spiking TPOT for already-streaming requests — keep enabled for mixed interactive/batch fleets.
- Speculative decoding buys 1.5–2.5× decode speed when draft/target models agree often; verify on your domain, acceptance rates vary wildly.
- Watch KV-cache eviction and preempt-recompute rates in vLLM metrics; rising values mean you've oversubscribed memory and p99 TTFT will show it before users complain.

## Summary

- The hosted-vs-self-hosted choice is per-workload, driven by four axes: unit economics at your volume, latency profile, data residency, and control/reproducibility.
- Data policy is workload-specific: GDPR transfer obligations, contractual residency, retention, access, and vendor terms must be assessed together; self-hosting alone is not compliance.
- vLLM's PagedAttention + continuous batching is why self-hosting became viable; prefix caching and quantization (AWQ/GPTQ int4) multiply the effect.
- VRAM planning is arithmetic: `params × bytes` for weights plus KV cache at p99 context × concurrency — the KV term decides how many users fit.
- Speak the vocabulary fluently: TTFT/TPOT, prefill vs decode, throughput vs goodput; they map directly onto user-perceived quality and cost.
- Routers with residency gates, health-aware fallback chains, and pinned revisions turn a single-vendor dependency into a resilient multi-tier serving stack.

# Transactional Email & Notification Delivery

> **Email is not a function call.** `sendEmail()` looks like one, which is why teams ship it inline in a request handler and then discover — in production, during a launch — that the provider was rate-limiting, the domain had no DKIM record, the retry sent a duplicate invoice, and nobody noticed because the failure was swallowed in a `catch`.

## Definition

**Transactional email** is a message whose content is specific to a single recipient and triggered by that recipient's own action or account state — as opposed to **marketing/bulk email**, which is sent to segments for promotion.

The distinction matters operationally, not just semantically:

|  | Transactional | Marketing / bulk |
| -------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Trigger | User action or account event | Campaign schedule |
| Consent | Implied by the transaction (with lawful basis) | Explicit opt-in, one-click unsubscribe |
| Volume | Bounded by traffic | Arbitrary, bursty |
| Failure cost | Broken login, unpaid invoice | Missed campaign |
| Sending domain | Own transactional subdomain | Separate domain/stream |
| Rate shape | Steady, spiky with traffic | Very spiky, needs pacing |

Keep them on **separate subdomains and often separate providers** (e.g. `mail.` vs `news.`): a marketing burst and its complaint rate must not damage the reputation of the channel that delivers password resets.

## First Principles

Email delivery exists under constraints that no API design can remove:

1. **You do not control the receiving server.** Delivery is a *negotiation* between mail servers; the final inbox/ spam decision is made by someone else's reputation system, using signals you can only influence (authentication, complaint rate, engagement).
2. **The failure arrives later, and out of band.** SMTP acceptance is not delivery, and delivery is not read receipt. So the sending call cannot return the truth — which means "email sent" can only ever mean "handed to the provider".
3. **Retries are guaranteed, deduplication is your job.** Any at-least-once pipeline (queue, provider retry, our own retry) will duplicate work; the only defense is an idempotency key derived from the business event, not from the attempt.
4. **A user-visible action must not depend on a mailbox.** If checkout waits on SMTP, provider latency becomes checkout latency and provider downtime becomes lost orders.
5. **Reputation is a shared, slow-moving resource.** One bad send list can poison a domain for months, so suppression and rate limiting are safety systems, not optimizations.

The consequence: split the *decision* to notify (synchronous, in your transaction) from the *act* of delivering (asynchronous, in a worker, idempotent, observable).

## Why it matters

Email is the fallback identity and recovery channel for almost every product: password resets, receipts, invitations, alerts. When it silently fails, users cannot log in and support cannot prove anything was sent — and because email failures rarely throw, the incident is discovered by customers. Doing it properly is a small amount of architecture that converts an invisible failure mode into a queue with retries, a suppression list, and delivery metrics.

## How it works

### 1. The pipeline

```text
 business event                 your app (synchronous)              worker (async)
 ──────────────                 ──────────────────────              ─────────────
 order.paid ──▶ tx { outbox row + domain change } ──▶ relay ──▶ queue ──▶ render ──▶ provider API
                                                         │                        │
                                                         ▼                        ▼
                                            idempotency key          webhook: delivered / bounced /
                                            email:invoice:8421        complained / opened
                                                         │
                                                         ▼
                                              suppression list + metrics
```

Three properties are non-negotiable:

1. **The decision is transactional, the send is not.** Write the outbox row in the same database transaction as the business change (→ [20.6.3.06](../../20.6-microservices/03-data-and-consistency/20.6.3.06-transactional-outbox.md)); otherwise a crash between "order saved" and "email queued" loses the notification forever.
2. **The worker is idempotent.** Provider timeouts are ambiguous — the message may have been accepted. Keyed deduplication (provider idempotency key and/or a `sent_at` guard on the outbox row) is what prevents the duplicate invoice.
3. **Delivery state is webhook-driven.** `202 accepted` is not delivered; only the provider's `delivered`/`bounce`/`complaint` events are.

### 2. Authentication: SPF, DKIM, DMARC

All three are DNS records; all three must be correct before the first send, and none of them are optional in the Gmail/Outlook era.

| Record | What it proves | Failure looks like |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **SPF** (RFC 7208) | The sending IP is authorized for the domain | `550 SPF fail`; mail rejected or spam-foldered |
| **DKIM** (RFC 6376) | The message body/headers were signed by the domain and not altered | Signature verification fails; DMARC cannot pass |
| **DMARC** (RFC 7489) | Publishes policy + alignment and where aggregate reports go | No policy = spoofable; with `p=reject` unaligned mail is rejected |

Roll out DMARC as `p=none` first, read the aggregate (RUA) reports to find every legitimate sender, then move to `quarantine` and `reject`. Skipping the observation step is how teams discover their payroll system was sending as the root domain.

### 3. Deliverability mechanics

- **Send from a subdomain** (`mail.example.com`) so reputation damage is isolated from the root domain.
- **Warm up a new IP/domain** — ramp volume over days; a sudden burst from a cold IP reads as spam.
- **Pace the queue** to respect provider rate limits (429 → back off with jitter → [30.3.2.02](../../../30-architecture/30.3-infra-patterns/02-failure-and-recovery/30.3.2.02-retry-patterns.md)).
- **Bounce taxonomy**: *hard* = permanent (unknown mailbox) → suppress immediately; *soft* = temporary (mailbox full, greylisted) → retry with backoff, escalate to suppression after N attempts.
- **Complaint = unsubscribe.** A complaint rate above ~0.1% is a deliverability emergency, so it drives your alerting, not just reporting.
- **Content hygiene** — one clear purpose, no link shorteners, real `text/plain` alternative, correct `List-Unsubscribe` for anything bulk.

### 4. Idempotency and suppression

Two data structures carry the correctness:

- **Idempotency map**: `send_key -> provider_message_id`, unique index in the database, checked before send and written after. Keys derive from business facts (`password-reset:<userId>:<resetId>`), so a retry of the *same* event is skipped while a *new* event still sends.
- **Suppression list**: emails that must never receive mail again (hard bounce, complaint, manual unsubscribe, legal request). Consult it *before* the provider call; feed it from bounce/complaint webhooks.

## Code Examples

### Provider port: one interface, swappable backends

```ts
// The domain depends on a port, not on Resend/SES/Postmark — so provider
// migration is a config change and tests inject a fake.
export interface EmailProvider {
  send(msg: {
    to: string;
    subject: string;
    html: string;
    text: string;
    idempotencyKey: string;   // provider-side dedupe where supported
    tags?: Record<string, string>;
  }): Promise<{ providerMessageId: string }>;
}
```

### Transactional outbox → queue → worker

```ts
// 1. SYNCHRONOUS, inside the business transaction: the decision to notify.
await prisma.$transaction(async (tx) => {
  const invoice = await tx.invoice.update({ where: { id }, data: { status: 'ISSUED' } });
  await tx.outboxEmail.create({
    data: {
      key: `invoice:${invoice.id}:issued`,   // idempotency key = business event
      to: invoice.customerEmail,
      template: 'invoice-issued',
      payload: { invoiceId: invoice.id, totalCents: invoice.totalCents },
    },
  });
});

// 2. ASYNCHRONOUS: a relay drains the outbox into the queue (BullMQ, SQS, Kafka).
//    Relay is safe to run twice because the outbox row is unique on `key`.
await emailQueue.add('send', { key: row.key }, {
  jobId: row.key,                            // queue-level dedupe
  attempts: 5,
  backoff: { type: 'exponential', delay: 30_000 },
  removeOnComplete: 1000,
});

// 3. WORKER: render, check suppression, send, record. Idempotent by construction.
async function sendEmailJob(job: Job<{ key: string }>) {
  const row = await prisma.outboxEmail.findUnique({ where: { key: job.data.key } });
  if (!row || row.sentAt) return;                             // already delivered
  if (await isSuppressed(row.to)) return markSuppressed(row); // never mail these

  const rendered = renderTemplate(row.template, row.payload);
  const { providerMessageId } = await provider.send({
    to: row.to,
    ...rendered,
    idempotencyKey: row.key,
  });
  await prisma.outboxEmail.update({
    where: { key: row.key },
    data: { sentAt: new Date(), providerMessageId },
  });
}
```

### Bounce and complaint webhooks feed the suppression list

```ts
// POST /webhooks/email — verify the signature, then act. Idempotent per event id.
@Post('webhooks/email')
async handle(@Headers('x-provider-signature') sig: string, @Req() req: RawBodyRequest) {
  verifyWebhookSignature(req.rawBody, sig, process.env.EMAIL_WEBHOOK_SECRET!);
  const event = req.body as ProviderEvent;

  if (await seenEvent(event.id)) return { ok: true };      // replay-safe
  await recordEvent(event.id);

  switch (event.type) {
    case 'bounce':
      if (event.bounce.type === 'hard') await suppress(event.to, 'hard-bounce');
      else await scheduleSoftBounceRetry(event.to, event.messageId);
      break;
    case 'complaint':
      await suppress(event.to, 'complaint');               // treat as unsubscribe
      await metrics.increment('email.complaint');
      break;
    case 'delivered':
      await metrics.timing('email.delivery_latency', event.deliveredAt - event.sentAt);
      break;
  }
  return { ok: true };                                     // fast 2xx, work is done
}
```

### Log the send decision, not just the call

```ts
logger.info({
  event: 'email.sent',
  key: row.key,                 // join key to the business event
  template: row.template,
  providerMessageId,
  latencyMs,
}, 'transactional email dispatched');
// Metrics that matter: queued, sent, delivered, bounced, complained, suppressed.
// Alert on: queue depth growth, bounce rate > 2%, complaint rate > 0.1%, provider 429s.
```

## Real-World Patterns

- **Password reset / magic link** — the highest-stakes transactional mail: never queue-bypass it, keep latency low, and never reveal whether an address exists in the response (→ [20.5.1.01](../01-design-and-contract/20.3.1.01-api-design-patterns.md), enumeration defense).
- **Invoices/receipts** — attach PDFs by generating them in the worker, and key on the invoice number so a retry cannot double-charge a customer's inbox.
- **Invitations and notifications** — batch and digest to keep volume (and thus reputation risk) proportional to value.
- **Alerting from your own product** — route to a provider with a different infrastructure than the one being monitored, or the alert dies with the outage.
- **Multi-tenant SaaS** — per-tenant sending subdomain and per-tenant suppression lists, so one customer's bad list cannot poison another's deliverability (→ [30.3.4.04](../../../30-architecture/30.3-infra-patterns/04-data-at-scale/30.3.4.04-multi-tenant-saas-architecture.md)).
- **Regulated data (PHI/PII)** — keep email bodies free of protected data, use a link to an authenticated view, and treat the recipient address itself as PII in logs.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Sending inline in the request handler** — user latency now includes a third party, and a provider outage fails the user's action.
2. **Treating SMTP/provider acceptance as delivery** — the failure arrives minutes later via webhook; without that handler you are blind.
3. **No idempotency key** — the retry after a timeout sends a second invoice.
4. **Ignoring bounces** — continuing to mail a dead address destroys sender reputation and gets you throttled for all customers.
5. **No DKIM/SPF/DMARC** — mail lands in spam and you blame the provider.
6. **Sharing the domain between marketing and transactional** — one bad campaign breaks password resets.
7. **Logging email bodies or tokens** — reset links in logs are credential leaks.
8. **No pacing against provider rate limits** — a launch burst triggers 429s, retries amplify them, and the queue melts.
9. **Swallowing send errors** — an empty `catch {}` turns a deliverability incident into an unexplained support queue.
10. **Only testing the happy path** — no test for bounced addresses, suppressed recipients, or provider 5xx.

## Best Practices

- Model notification as **an event, not a function call**: outbox row in the transaction, queue for delivery, worker for rendering and sending.
- One **provider port** with at least two implementations (real + fake) so tests never hit the network and provider migration is a config change.
- Derive **idempotency keys from business facts** and enforce uniqueness in the database, not just in the queue.
- Maintain a **suppression list** fed by bounce/complaint webhooks, and check it before every send.
- **Authenticate the domain** (SPF, DKIM, DMARC `p=none` → `quarantine` → `reject`) and separate transactional from marketing subdomains.
- **Observe the pipeline**: queued vs sent vs delivered vs bounced vs complained, plus queue depth and provider error rates; alert on bounce/complaint thresholds.
- **Cap and pace**: max attempts, max age (do not deliver a 3-day-old OTP), rate limit per provider and per tenant.
- Keep bodies **templated and versioned**, with a plain-text alternative generated from the same source.
- Give users a **notification preference** where regulation requires it (and always for anything non-essential).

## Performance Considerations

- Sending is I/O-bound and network-latent (provider APIs are typically 100–500 ms): keep worker concurrency modest and rely on the queue for throughput rather than threads.
- **Queue depth is the metric with the shortest feedback loop**; a growing depth means provider throttling or a worker bug long before customers complain.
- Batch where the provider allows it (SES bulk, Postmark batch), but never batch across idempotency keys.
- Rendered HTML is CPU-cheap but templates with heavy logic are not — render once per message, cache compiled templates.
- Trap "expired" notifications: an OTP or magic link mailed after it expires is wasted volume and a support ticket; drop by max age.
- Keep the webhook handler fast: persist the event, return 2xx, do the work in the same queue infrastructure (→ [20.3.3.05](20.3.3.05-webhooks.md)).

## Summary

- Transactional email is an at-least-once pipeline: decision synchronous in the transaction, delivery asynchronous in a worker, idempotent by business key.
- Deliverability is authentication (SPF, DKIM, DMARC) plus reputation management (subdomains, warm-up, pacing, suppression).
- Bounces and complaints come back as webhooks and must feed a suppression list — that list is the difference between one bad address and a blocked domain.
- "Sent" means handed to the provider; only webhook events tell you delivered, bounced or complained.
- Observability and thresholds (queue depth, bounce rate, complaint rate) are what turn an invisible failure into an alert.

# Saga Pattern

A payment timeout may arrive after the provider has charged the customer. Releasing inventory as if the charge had failed can leave a paid order without stock.

## Definition

The **Saga pattern** manages a multi-service business workflow through a sequence of local transactions. Participants may coordinate through events or commands from an orchestrator. If completion fails, explicit compensating operations address earlier committed work.
A compensation may need retries or manual recovery; it does not erase already observed effects or provide global isolation.

> **Textbook Definition:** A saga coordinates a sequence of committed local transactions with explicit compensating actions when completion fails. Compensation is a new business operation, not a rollback of all previously visible effects or a guarantee of isolation.

## First Principles

Participants commit independently, so earlier effects can be visible before the whole process succeeds. Recovery must act on those effects under business rules. Releasing stock, refunding payment and cancelling dispatch are different operations with different validity windows; none erases the original history.

Network failure also separates outcome from observation. A payment request can commit while its response is lost. Progress must retain that uncertainty rather than translating every exception into a rejected payment.

## 1. Follow a reservation through payment and recovery

```text
reserve stock -> attempt payment with stable identity -> confirm order
                         |                    |
                 known rejection        unknown outcome
                         |                    |
                  release stock      reconcile provider result
                         |                    |
                  cancel order      complete OR compensate
```

The inventory service enforces stock availability atomically under its local transaction. Saga compensation cannot prevent two concurrent reservations from overselling. Reservation expiry and payment uncertainty need a coordinated policy: expiring a hold while a charge is unresolved can leave a paid order without stock.

## 2. Classify the payment result before persisting action intent

This complete JavaScript helper models a small part of the workflow: deciding what to do after the payment attempt. It performs no remote effects and is not a durable engine. Outcomes must come from validated participant/provider evidence, not arbitrary client input.

```javascript
function paymentDecision(state, outcome) {
  if (state !== 'reserved' && state !== 'payment_unknown') {
    throw new Error('invalid payment state');
  }
  if (outcome === 'unknown') return { state: 'payment_unknown', action: 'reconcile' };
  if (outcome === 'confirmed_paid') return { state: 'paid', action: 'confirm_order' };
  if (outcome === 'confirmed_declined') return { state: 'release_pending', action: 'release_stock' };
  throw new Error('unrecognized outcome');
}
module.exports = { paymentDecision };
```

A timeout produces `unknown`, so the next action is reconciliation, not a new charge or immediate release. A verified paid result advances to confirmation. A verified decline advances to a separately tracked release.
Repeated observations must not dispatch fresh duplicate effects: persist versioned state and action intent, then deduplicate the dispatched action at its participant.

A durable coordinator needs workflow/tenant identity, version, step/action identity, accepted outcome, attempt/deadline data and pending recovery. Commit progress and outgoing action intent together where they share a database. Claim work atomically, reject stale owners/versions, and resume after crashes.
A logger or in-memory callback list supplies none of that durability.

## 3. Compensation is another fallible business action

If an order is paid but fulfillment cannot proceed, a refund may be required. Record refund intent and its provider-supported idempotency key, then resolve acceptance and unknown outcomes. Do not mark compensation complete after merely logging its error.
Exhausted retries need visible `manual_review` or another owned recovery state.

Some steps are irreversible, such as a parcel already delivered. Arrange compensatable preparation before the irreversible **pivot** where practical, then use defined retry/recovery behavior afterward. Reverse-order compensation is a useful starting point, but business dependencies can require another order.
Multiple failed compensations may need tracking independently.

An API deadline stops waiting; `Promise.race` does not cancel the work. A late effect can still arrive and must be reconciled against current workflow identity/version. Retry with bounded backoff only when operation identity and participant behavior make it safe.

## 4. Choreography and orchestration own progress differently

| Model | Mechanism | Questions to settle |
| ------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Choreography | Participants consume facts and publish their outcomes | Who owns end-to-end status, deadlines, causal order and recovery? |
| Orchestration | A coordinator persists steps and commands | How does it resume, fence stale work and evolve long-running versions? |

Choreography does not remove coupling: participants depend on event schemas and process meaning. Orchestration does not require placing every domain rule in the coordinator; participants enforce their own invariants. Choose from process visibility, ownership and evolution rather than a fixed service-count threshold.

## 5. Expose intermediate state and operate recovery

The user can see `pending`, `confirmed`, `recovery_pending` or a documented failure. Do not describe a process as globally rolled back if a refund is still unresolved. Record causal/action identity in minimized logs, and monitor oldest pending/unknown step, compensation failure, reservation age and operator backlog.

Test a crash before/after each local commit, lost participant response, duplicate result, concurrent state update, late completion and failed compensation. A container restart or CronJob alone does not recover workflow decisions. Use the [outbox chapter](20.6.3.06-transactional-outbox.md) for publication intent and the [distributed transaction comparison](20.6.3.02-distributed-transactions.md) for atomic-commit alternatives.

## Summary

A saga coordinates visible local commits and explicit recovery. Persist stable step identities and uncertainty, preserve local concurrency invariants, and treat compensation as fallible new work. Choose choreography or orchestration with an owned, observable restart and recovery contract.

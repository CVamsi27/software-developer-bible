# Message Brokers Interview Questions

> **Format:** Question → 30-second answer → follow-up traps. 25 questions covering what senior loops actually ask about Kafka, RabbitMQ, and event-driven systems in production. Pair with the [track files](INDEX.md) for depth.

---

## First Principles

Brokers trade synchronous coupling for temporal decoupling — every question below is a consequence of that trade. Ordering survives only per partition, delivery is at-least-once in practice, and progress is owned by consumers, not the broker. Interviewers rarely want the config value; they want the failure it prevents and the trade-off it re-opens.

---

## Beginner (1–8)

### Q1. "What problem does a message broker solve that HTTP calls don't?"

The producer succeeds when the *log* accepts the record — the consumer can be down, slow, or not yet deployed. You gain temporal decoupling, burst buffering, and fan-out; you give up the synchronous answer. Follow-up traps: "so when is HTTP still right?" (any time you need the reply to continue — checkout stock checks); "what new failure modes appear?" (lag, rebalances, poison, schema skew).

### Q2. "Kafka or RabbitMQ — how do you decide?"

Kafka for replayable streams, per-key ordering at scale, and analytics/CDC (the log model); RabbitMQ for rich routing and per-job work distribution with acks (the queue model). Follow-up traps: "do you need replay?" (Kafka's killer feature); "do you need complex routing?" (RabbitMQ exchanges); "who tracks progress?" (consumers via offsets vs broker via acks).

### Q3. "Explain at-least-once delivery and what it demands from consumers."

Process, *then* ack: a crash between the two means redelivery, so duplicates are normal weather. The consumer must be idempotent — dedup on `event_id`, upsert, or version check. Follow-up traps: "why not exactly-once everywhere?" (it's a narrow pipeline property, not a switch); "where has this bitten you?" (retry after partial write without idempotency).

### Q4. "What is a consumer group?"

A set of consumers sharing a `group.id`; Kafka divides the topic's partitions among them, so each partition is processed by exactly one member — parallelism with per-partition ordering. Follow-up traps: "what happens when a member dies?" (rebalance; partitions redistribute); "why not one group per service?" — no, one group per *purpose*; sharing a group across unrelated services couples their scaling.

### Q5. "What does acks=all mean and why does it matter?"

The producer's write is acked only when replicated to the all current in-sync replicas; `min.insync.replicas` is the acceptance floor, not the number waited for, not merely held by the leader — so a leader death doesn't erase acked writes. Follow-up traps: "what if ISR shrinks below the floor?" (writes rejected — availability traded for durability); "what does acks=1 risk?" (acked-but-not-replicated data loss).

### Q6. "What is consumer lag and why alert on it?"

`log-end-offset − committed-offset`: how far behind the consumer is. It's the honest SLI for async pipelines — throughput can look great while a backlog grows. Follow-up traps: "why not alert on redeliveries?" (redelivery is routine under at-least-once); "what does monotonic lag growth mean?" (a consumer that can't keep up or is stuck).

### Q7. "What is a dead-letter queue for?"

Bounded retries for transient errors; permanent failures go to the DLQ so the partition keeps moving — then the DLQ is *monitored and reviewed*, and reprocessing or discarding is a recorded decision. Follow-up traps: "what if the DLQ itself is unmonitored?" (poison disappears silently — worse than blocking); "who owns it?" (the consumer's team, with an alert).

### Q8. "Why do messages need keys?"

The key chooses the partition, and ordering exists only within a partition — keying by entity id (order-id, user-id) gives you per-entity ordering while keeping parallelism. Follow-up traps: "what happens with no key?" (client partitioning strategy provides no per-entity ordering contract); "what if one key is hot?" (partition skew — discuss salting/sub-partitioning).

## Intermediate (9–17)

### Q9. "Walk me through what happens when a Kafka broker dies."

Its partition leaders move to ISR members (controller-triggered election); with RF=3/min.ISR=2 and two healthy in-sync replicas remaining, producers with `acks=all` can continue; consumers see a brief blip. If ISR is below the floor, writes fail *by design* — unavailability over data loss. Follow-up traps: "what's unclean leader election and why is it off?" (an out-of-sync replica becoming leader would erase acked writes); "what does the consumers' committed offset have to do with it?" (the offset topic is replicated too; recovery depends on its durability configuration, while the data partition's election is a separate mechanism).

### Q10. "Your team sees p99 latency spike after every deploy. Kafka is in the path. Where do you look?"

Rebalance count first: a new consumer instance joining (or a slow first poll exceeding `max.poll.interval.ms`) stalls the group while partitions move. Then handler budgets vs poll interval — evicted members leave in-flight work that double-processes. Fixes: cooperative-sticky assignment, handler timeouts strictly under the poll budget, idempotent consumers to absorb overlap. Follow-up traps: "why not just raise max.poll.interval?" (masks the real handler latency; the storm returns at the new ceiling).

### Q11. "How do you make 'write to the DB and publish an event' atomic?"

The transactional outbox: insert the event into an `outbox` table in the *same transaction* as the write; a relay (poller or CDC) publishes after commit and marks rows sent. At-least-once between relay and broker; consumers dedup on `event_id`. Follow-up traps: "why not a dual write?" (crash between the two = silent divergence); "why not 2PC?" (coordination cost, blocked transactions — the outbox trades an eventually-consistent publish pipe for local atomicity).

### Q12. "A schema change breaks consumers overnight. What was missing?"

A compatibility contract enforced in CI: add-optional-only evolution, version in the envelope, consumers ignore unknown fields, and a registry check that rejects breaking changes before merge. Follow-up traps: "how do you ever *remove* a field?" (deprecate → stop writing → remove after consumers confirm tolerance); "why is version in the envelope, not just the registry?" (consumers route/branch by it at runtime).

### Q13. "Design idempotent consumption."

Three layers, pick per handler: dedup table keyed on `event_id` with TTL; upsert semantics (write the *state*, not the transition); version/condition checks (update where version = X). All three survive redelivery. Follow-up traps: "where does the dedup table live?" (same store as the side effect — one transaction); "why TTL?" (bounded state; the dedup lifetime must cover the longest permitted redelivery, archived replay, and disaster-recovery window).

### Q14. "When would you choose log compaction over time retention?"

When the topic's value is *latest state per key* — entity snapshots, config, `__consumer_offsets` — and new consumers must rebuild state by replaying from offset 0. Time retention answers "keep the history for N days"; compaction alone retains current keyed state while removing superseded values; retention of deletion markers has its own horizon. Follow-up traps: "can you combine them?" (yes — `compact,delete` also time/size-expires segments and may remove the latest value; tombstone expiry is separately configured); "what's a tombstone?" (a null-value record marking deletion, critical for correctness of the rebuilt state).

### Q15. "How do you size partitions?"

Partitions are the unit of consumer parallelism, so size from measured per-partition throughput, required consumer parallelism, key skew, and recovery overhead — not from today's throughput. Too few caps scale; too many multiplies files, fds, replication traffic, and rebalance surface. Follow-up traps: "can you lower the count later?" (painfully — plan growth, don't guess); "does more partitions mean more ordering?" (no — finer ordering scopes, weaker global guarantees).

### Q16. "What does prefetch do in RabbitMQ and what breaks without it?"

Prefetch bounds unacked in-flight deliveries per consumer. Too high: workers drown in redelivered work on crash and memory balloons; absent/unbounded: one slow consumer hoards messages it can't process. It's the queue-model backpressure dial, mirroring consumer lag in Kafka. Follow-up traps: "how do you pick a number?" (desired in-flight concurrency plus measured processing/memory capacity — then tune under load; latency multiplied by concurrency is not a message count); "what happens to unacked messages when the consumer dies?" (redelivered — at-least-once again).

### Q17. "Events vs commands — does the distinction actually change anything?"

Yes: events are facts any number of consumers may react to; commands assign work to exactly one handler. Sharing a topic across the two couples producers to consumers' semantics and breaks fan-out reasoning. Name it in the topic (`orders.events` vs `payments.commands`), route accordingly. Follow-up traps: "is request/reply over a broker a command pattern?" (yes — with correlation ids and reply topics; ask when that's worth it vs plain HTTP).

## Advanced (18–25)

### Q18. "Design the event pipeline for an order lifecycle end to end."

Keyed events (`order.created`, `order.paid`, `order.shipped`) via outbox from the orders service; consumers: fulfillment (commands topic), notifications, analytics. Envelope carries `event_id`/`schema_version`/`trace_id`. Fulfillment is idempotent (order state machine), DLQs per consumer with weekly review, lag SLI per group. Follow-up traps: "what if payment and shipping must be ordered?" (the same key in the same topic under a stable partitioner/partition count maps to one partition; this orders log appends, not business causality across independent producers/topics); "how does a new analytics service get history?" (replay from offset 0 — retention sized for it).

### Q19. "Explain effectively-once and where each piece lives."

At-least-once delivery + idempotent consumers = effectively-once *effects*. Kafka adds transactional producers/consumers for read-process-write loops (consume-transform-produce with offsets committed in the transaction); external sinks dedup via their own keys. Follow-up traps: "is the Kafka transaction exactly-once?" (for the specific pipeline topology it was designed for — not a general teleporter); "what breaks it?" (a non-idempotent side effect outside the transactional boundary — an email, an HTTP call).

### Q20. "A consumer is stuck on one poison message. Walk me through the incident."

Symptom: partition lag growing, no error spikes — the handler keeps throwing, the partition stops advancing. Diagnosis: replay the failing record locally from the DLQ/lag. Fix: bounded retries with backoff → quarantine to DLQ; only advance after its publish succeeds, otherwise reject/retry → alert → review the payload, patch the handler or the data → reprocess the DLQ. Prevention: schema checks at consume, handler budgets, DLQ alerts. Follow-up traps: "why not auto-discard after N retries?" (silent data loss needs a *recorded decision*, not a default); "how did the poison get produced?" (schema skew — Q12's CI gap).

### Q21. "How do you do change data capture into Kafka, and what can go wrong?"

Debezium tails the WAL/binlog into topics (compact for state, retain for history); downstream materializes views by replay. Pitfalls: schema drift at the source, hot rows concentrating on one partition, huge transaction bursts (one commit → thousands of events), and treating tombstones as noise. Follow-up traps: "why CDC instead of dual writes?" (the DB is the source of truth; no dual-write divergence); "how do you handle PII in the log?" (field-level encryption/tokenization before the sink; retention policy is a compliance decision).

### Q22. "You inherit a system where three unrelated services share one consumer group. What's the risk and the migration?"

Risk: partitions are divided among *all* members, so any service's scaling or slowness starves the others; one member's rebalance stalls everyone's processing. Migration: give each purpose its own group id (all groups read the full topic), deploy incrementally, watch lag per group. Follow-up traps: "does triple-reading raise broker load?" (yes — replication/fetch fan-out; size it, then decide if a compacted down-stream topic serves some consumers); "why is this mistake so common?" (group-per-app looks like the sharing-friendly default in tutorials).

### Q23. "Kafka retention is 7 days but finance needs 7 years of order events. Options?"

Tiered storage or sink-to-object-store (iceberg/parquet via connectors) with the broker as the *transport*, not the archive; compacted state topics for current truth; the sink is queryable, the log stays hot. Follow-up traps: "why not just raise retention to 7 years?" (broker disk, recovery times, and rebalance weight all balloon for data nobody reads hot); "how do replays work then?" (re-load from the sink into a topic, or batch-consume the lake — same dedup/idempotency rules apply).

### Q24. "Design multi-region event flow. What do you trade?"

Active-passive MirrorMaker 2 replication: async, so RPO > 0 — accept it, make consumers idempotent across region failover (duplicated replicated events). Active-active needs partition ownership per region or CRDT-style conflict handling — usually not worth it. Follow-up traps: "does failover preserve ordering?" (replication preserves source-partition sequence, but failover/duplicate windows and concurrent regional producers need an explicit ordering/ownership contract); "how do you test it?" (game-day failover with lag/RPO dashboards, not a runbook nobody ran).

### Q25. "What would make you remove a broker from an existing system?"

The honest senior answer: when the temporal decoupling is no longer load-bearing — a single consumer that could just call the producer, rates so low a DB-backed job table wins, or a request/reply pattern fighting the tool. Removing infrastructure is a credibility moment: measure usage per topic, migrate the stragglers, delete topics deliberately. Follow-up traps: "how do you find out the broker is over-provisioned?" (per-topic produce/consume rates, subscriber counts, last-published timestamps); "what's the risk of removal?" (hidden replay/audit consumers — grep your own dashboards and data contracts before deleting).

## Summary

Broker interviews probe whether you can hold the whole contract in your head: decoupling bought, ordering scoped, delivery at-least-once, progress owned by consumers, failures routed through designed paths. Anchor every answer in a failure it prevents and a trade it re-opens — that's the senior signal.

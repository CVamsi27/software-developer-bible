# Message Brokers Interview Questions

> **Format:** Question → 30-second answer → follow-up traps. 25 questions covering what senior loops actually ask about Kafka, RabbitMQ, and event-driven systems in production. Pair with the [track files](INDEX.md) for depth.

---

## First Principles

Brokers trade synchronous coupling for temporal decoupling — every question below is a consequence of that trade. Ordering depends on the partition/queue and handler contract; a common configured delivery mode is at-least-once. Kafka classic groups track committed offsets, Share Groups acknowledge individual records, and RabbitMQ queues track delivery acknowledgments. Interviewers rarely want the config value; they want the failure it prevents and the trade-off it re-opens.

---

## Beginner (1–8)

### Q1. "What problem does a message broker solve that HTTP calls don't?"

The producer succeeds when the configured broker acceptance contract completes — the consumer can be down, slow, or not yet deployed. You gain temporal decoupling, burst buffering, and fan-out; you give up the synchronous answer. Follow-up traps: "so when is HTTP still right?" (any time you need the reply to continue — checkout stock checks); "what new failure modes appear?" (lag, rebalances, poison, schema skew).

### Q2. "Kafka or RabbitMQ — how do you decide?"

Kafka classic consumer groups for replayable partitioned streams and offset-based recovery; Kafka 4.2+ Share Groups for per-record acknowledgment and queue-like parallel work; RabbitMQ for rich routing and queue delivery. Follow-up traps: "do you require per-partition order?" (classic groups); "do you need each record acknowledged independently?" (Share Groups, with a client that exposes the API); "do you need complex routing?" (RabbitMQ exchanges).

### Q3. "Explain at-least-once delivery and what it demands from consumers."

Process, *then* ack: a crash between the two means redelivery, so duplicates are normal weather. The consumer must be idempotent — dedup on `event_id`, upsert, or version check. Follow-up traps: "why not exactly-once everywhere?" (it's a narrow pipeline property, not a switch); "where has this bitten you?" (retry after partial write without idempotency).

### Q4. "What is a consumer group?"

A **classic** set of consumers sharing a `group.id`; Kafka divides the topic's partitions among them, so one member owns each assigned partition at a time. Old in-flight work can overlap reassignment; parallel handlers can complete out of order. Share Groups are a separate Kafka 4.2+ mode with per-record acknowledgment. Follow-up traps: "what happens when a classic-group member dies?" (rebalance; partitions redistribute); "why one group per purpose?" (so each purpose reads the full stream).

### Q5. "What does acks=all mean and why does it matter?"

The producer's write is acked only when replicated to all current in-sync replicas; `min.insync.replicas` is the acceptance floor, not the number waited for, not merely held by the leader — a leader failure is survivable while a suitable acknowledged copy remains. Losing every copy can still lose acknowledged data. Follow-up traps: "what if ISR shrinks below the floor?" (writes rejected — availability traded for durability); "what does acks=1 risk?" (acked-but-not-replicated data loss).

### Q6. "What is consumer lag and why alert on it?"

For a classic group, `log-end-offset − committed-offset` estimates offset distance behind the log. It is not a time measure and does not apply to Share Groups' per-record acknowledgment state. Follow-up traps: "why not alert on redeliveries?" (redelivery is routine under at-least-once); "what else should you measure?" (oldest-unprocessed age and event-to-effect latency).

### Q7. "What is a dead-letter queue for?"

Bounded retries for transient errors; permanent failures go to the DLQ so the partition keeps moving — then the DLQ is *monitored and reviewed*, and reprocessing or discarding is a recorded decision. Follow-up traps: "what if the DLQ itself is unmonitored?" (poison disappears silently — worse than blocking); "who owns it?" (the consumer's team, with an alert).

### Q8. "Why do messages need keys?"

In a classic consumer group, the key normally selects a partition and ordering is defined within that partition. Keying by entity ID (order ID, user ID) keeps an entity's records together, but serial processing is still required to preserve effect order. Share Groups acknowledge records independently and do not provide the same ordered-stream contract. Follow-up traps: "what happens with no key?" (no per-entity partition-order contract); "what if one key is hot?" (partition skew — discuss salting/sub-partitioning and the ordering guarantee it sacrifices).

## Intermediate (9–17)

### Q9. "Walk me through what happens when a Kafka broker dies."

Its partition leaders move to ISR members (controller-triggered election); with RF=3/min.ISR=2 and two healthy in-sync replicas remaining, producers with `acks=all` can continue; consumers see a brief blip. If ISR is below the floor, writes fail *by design* — unavailability over data loss. Follow-up traps: "what's unclean leader election and why is it off?" (an out-of-sync replica becoming leader would erase acked writes); "what does the consumers' committed offset have to do with it?" (the offset topic is replicated too; recovery depends on its durability configuration, while the data partition's election is a separate mechanism).

### Q10. "Your team sees p99 latency spike after every deploy. Kafka is in the path. Where do you look?"

Start with rebalance count: a joining member or a handler exceeding its client progress budget can move partitions and leave effects in flight. Check the client and protocol: cooperative-sticky applies to classic groups; KIP-848 changes assignment and server heartbeat control. Confluent JavaScript manages heartbeats in librdkafka, and its `eachMessage` heartbeat callback is a no-op; it does not extend the handler budget. Bound or move slow work, make effects idempotent, and do not rely on a Promise timeout to stop the underlying operation. Follow-up traps: "why not just raise `max.poll.interval.ms`?" (it may only delay the same failure; the handler still needs a safe progress and cancellation contract).

### Q11. "How do you make 'write to the DB and publish an event' atomic?"

The transactional outbox: insert the event into an `outbox` table in the *same transaction* as the write; a relay (poller or CDC) publishes after commit and marks rows sent. At-least-once between relay and broker; consumers dedup on `event_id`. Follow-up traps: "why not a dual write?" (crash between the two = silent divergence); "why not 2PC?" (coordination cost, blocked transactions — the outbox trades an eventually-consistent publish pipe for local atomicity).

### Q12. "A schema change breaks consumers overnight. What was missing?"

A compatibility contract enforced in CI: format-specific backward/forward compatibility, semantic contract tests, a versioned decoding policy, and registry checks that reject breaking changes before merge. Unknown-field behavior depends on the decoder and validation rules, so test the deployed readers rather than assuming they ignore fields. Follow-up traps: "how do you ever *remove* a field?" (deprecate → stop writing → remove after consumers confirm tolerance); "why is version in the envelope, not just the registry?" (consumers may route/branch by it at runtime).

### Q13. "Design idempotent consumption."

Three layers, pick per handler: dedup table keyed on `event_id` with TTL; upsert semantics (write the *state*, not the transition); version/condition checks (update where version = X). Each needs a defined atomicity/replay boundary. An unconditional upsert of an older event can overwrite newer state; use monotonic entity versions where that matters. Follow-up traps: "where does the dedup table live?" (same store as the side effect — one transaction); "why TTL?" (bounded state; the dedup lifetime must cover the longest permitted redelivery, archived replay, and disaster-recovery window).

### Q14. "When would you choose log compaction over time retention?"

When the topic's value is *latest state per key* — entity snapshots, config, `__consumer_offsets` — and new consumers must rebuild state by replaying from offset 0. Time retention answers "keep the history for N days"; compaction alone retains current keyed state while removing superseded values; retention of deletion markers has its own horizon. Follow-up traps: "can you combine them?" (yes — `compact,delete` also time/size-expires segments and may remove the latest value; tombstone expiry is separately configured); "what's a tombstone?" (a null-value record marking deletion, critical for correctness of the rebuilt state).

### Q15. "How do you size partitions?"

For classic groups, partitions bound group parallelism; size from measured throughput, target parallelism, key skew and recovery overhead — not just current traffic. Too few cap scale; too many multiply files, descriptors, replication traffic and rebalance surface. Share Groups have per-record parallelism and a different scaling model. Follow-up traps: "can you lower the count later?" (not in place — migrate to a new topic; raising the count can also move keyed records); "does more partitions mean more ordering?" (no — finer ordering scopes, weaker global guarantees).

### Q16. "What does prefetch do in RabbitMQ and what breaks without it?"

Prefetch bounds unacked in-flight deliveries per consumer. Too high: workers drown in redelivered work on crash and memory balloons; absent/unbounded: one slow consumer hoards messages it can't process. It's the queue-model backpressure dial, mirroring consumer lag in Kafka. Follow-up traps: "how do you pick a number?" (desired in-flight concurrency plus measured processing/memory capacity — then tune under load; latency multiplied by concurrency is not a message count); "what happens to unacked messages when the consumer dies?" (redelivered — at-least-once again).

### Q17. "Events vs commands — does the distinction actually change anything?"

Yes: events are facts any number of consumers may react to; commands assign work to exactly one handler. Sharing a topic across the two couples producers to consumers' semantics and breaks fan-out reasoning. Name it in the topic (`orders.events` vs `payments.commands`), route accordingly. Follow-up traps: "is request/reply over a broker a command pattern?" (yes — with correlation ids and reply topics; ask when that's worth it vs plain HTTP).

## Advanced (18–25)

### Q18. "Design the event pipeline for an order lifecycle end to end."

Keyed events (`order.created`, `order.paid`, `order.shipped`) via outbox from the orders service; consumers: fulfillment (commands topic), notifications, analytics. Envelope carries `event_id`/`schema_version`/`trace_id`. Fulfillment is idempotent (order state machine), DLQs per consumer with weekly review, lag SLI per group. Follow-up traps: "what if payment and shipping must be ordered?" (the same key in the same topic under a stable partitioner/partition count maps to one partition; this orders log appends, not business causality across independent producers/topics); "how does a new analytics service get history?" (replay from offset 0 — retention sized for it).

### Q19. "Explain effectively-once and where each piece lives."

At-least-once delivery + idempotent consumers = effectively-once *effects*. Kafka adds transactional producers/consumers for read-process-write loops (consume-transform-produce with offsets committed in the transaction and downstream readers using `read_committed`); external sinks dedup via their own keys. Follow-up traps: "is the Kafka transaction exactly-once?" (for the specific pipeline topology it was designed for — not a general teleporter); "what breaks it?" (a non-idempotent side effect outside the transactional boundary — an email, an HTTP call).

### Q20. "A consumer is stuck on one poison message. Walk me through the incident."

Symptom: partition lag growing, no error spikes — the handler keeps throwing, the partition stops advancing. Diagnosis: replay the failing record locally from the DLQ/lag. Fix: bounded retries with backoff → quarantine to DLQ; only advance after its publish succeeds, otherwise reject/retry → alert → review the payload, patch the handler or the data → reprocess the DLQ. Prevention: schema checks at consume, handler budgets, DLQ alerts. Follow-up traps: "why not auto-discard after N retries?" (silent data loss needs a *recorded decision*, not a default); "how did the poison get produced?" (schema skew — Q12's CI gap).

### Q21. "How do you do change data capture into Kafka, and what can go wrong?"

Debezium tails the WAL/binlog into topics (compact for state, retain for history); downstream materializes views by replay. Pitfalls: snapshot-to-log handoff and retained WAL/binlog checkpoints, replication-slot disk growth while capture stalls, schema drift at the source, hot rows concentrating on one partition, huge transaction bursts (one commit → thousands of events), and treating tombstones as noise. Follow-up traps: "why CDC instead of dual writes?" (the DB is the source of truth; no dual-write divergence); "how do you handle PII in the log?" (field-level encryption/tokenization before the sink; retention policy is a compliance decision).

### Q22. "You inherit a system where three unrelated services share one consumer group. What's the risk and the migration?"

Risk: partitions are divided among *all* members, so each purpose receives only a subset of records it may need; one member's rebalance stalls everyone's processing. Migration: give each purpose its own group id (all groups read the full topic), choose the initial/reset offset and deduplication policy before deployment, then watch lag per group. A new group replay can repeat external effects. Follow-up traps: "does triple-reading raise broker load?" (yes — replication/fetch fan-out; size it, then decide if a compacted down-stream topic serves some consumers); "why is this mistake so common?" (group-per-app looks like the sharing-friendly default in tutorials).

### Q23. "Kafka retention is 7 days but finance needs 7 years of order events. Options?"

Tiered storage or sink-to-object-store (iceberg/parquet via connectors) with the broker as the *transport*, not the archive; compacted state topics for current truth; the sink is queryable, the log stays hot. Follow-up traps: "why not just raise retention to 7 years?" (local/tiered storage cost and replay/recovery requirements change for data nobody reads hot); "how do replays work then?" (re-load from the sink into a topic, or batch-consume the lake — same dedup/idempotency rules apply).

### Q24. "Design multi-region event flow. What do you trade?"

Active-passive MirrorMaker 2 replication: asynchronous, so failover may lose the unreplicated tail — measure that window, make consumers idempotent across region failover (duplicated replicated events). Active-active needs partition ownership per region or CRDT-style conflict handling — usually not worth it. Follow-up traps: "does failover preserve ordering?" (replication preserves source-partition sequence, but failover/duplicate windows and concurrent regional producers need an explicit ordering/ownership contract); "how do you test it?" (game-day failover with lag/RPO dashboards, not a runbook nobody ran).

### Q25. "What would make you remove a broker from an existing system?"

The honest senior answer: when the temporal decoupling is no longer load-bearing — a single consumer that could just call the producer, rates so low a DB-backed job table wins, or a request/reply pattern fighting the tool. Removing infrastructure is a credibility moment: measure usage per topic, migrate the stragglers, delete topics deliberately. Follow-up traps: "how do you find out the broker is over-provisioned?" (per-topic produce/consume rates, subscriber counts, last-published timestamps); "what's the risk of removal?" (hidden replay/audit consumers — grep your own dashboards and data contracts before deleting).

## Summary

Broker interviews probe whether you can hold the whole contract in your head: decoupling bought, ordering scoped, delivery at-least-once, progress owned by consumers, failures routed through designed paths. Anchor every answer in a failure it prevents and a trade it re-opens — that's the senior signal.

# Saga Pattern

Flight booking succeeds, but the hotel rejects the reservation. The airline has already committed, so the original request cannot roll back the trip. The workflow must persist that partial result and decide whether to cancel, retry, or escalate:

```text
reserve flight ✓ → reserve hotel ✗ → cancel flight (compensation)
```

## Definition

A **saga** coordinates a business workflow through **local committed transactions** and **compensating actions**. Intermediate results can be visible, and compensation restores an acceptable business outcome rather than making the whole workflow an atomic rollback.

> **Textbook Definition:** In the original database model, a saga is a long-lived transaction split into local transactions that can interleave with other work; compensating transactions amend a partial execution. [Garcia-Molina and Salem, "Sagas" (1987)](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/techreports/1987/070.pdf)

## First Principles

Each participant commits locally, and other work may observe or depend on intermediate state. A later failure cannot undo those commits. The coordinator — or the participants in a choreography — needs durable evidence of requested, completed, and uncertain outcomes plus business-valid recovery actions. A saga has no global isolation.

## How It Works

### 1. Compensate a failed trip step by step

```text
reserve flight → reserve hotel → charge payment → confirm trip
      │                │                │
 cancel flight    cancel hotel     refund payment
```

Suppose the flight reservation succeeds and hotel booking returns a definitive rejection. The workflow records the rejected hotel step, requests flight cancellation, and records its result. Cancellation is a new business transaction: fees or other effects may remain, and a failed cancellation stays compensation-pending for retry and reconciliation.

A hotel timeout is different: the reservation might have completed remotely. Retrying or cancelling without a stable operation identifier risks a duplicate or an orphan. Query the operation's status or retry using the provider's idempotency contract before deciding what to compensate.

### 2. Make the recovery record durable

| State | Evidence needed before advancing |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Step requested | Workflow ID, step ID, stable operation key and input |
| Step completed | Provider result or authoritative local commit identity |
| Step uncertain | Timeout/failure evidence and a reconciliation action |
| Compensation pending | Original outcome and stable compensation identity |
| Workflow finished | Confirmed business outcome, including any unresolved exception policy |

Persist state transitions and outgoing intent atomically where possible, for example using an outbox. A restart should resume from durable evidence. A stack of in-memory callbacks lost on crash is no durable saga implementation. A timeout races with an operation that may continue; `Promise.race` alone never cancels remote work.

Compensations must tolerate retries and races with delayed success. Choose their order from domain dependencies; reversing successful steps is common, but not universal. Classify steps as compensable, a pivot, or retryable. The pivot marks where recovery becomes forward-only; after it succeeds, retryable work should complete rather than compensate earlier steps. Some actions are irreversible or only partly compensable, so define escalation instead of claiming every effect can be undone.

### 3. Place coordination in an orchestrator or choreography

In orchestration, one workflow owner decides the next step from persisted state. Replication and durable execution can prevent that owner from becoming an unrecoverable single point of failure. In choreography, participants react to events and own portions of progress and recovery. They remain coupled through event meaning and ordering even when they never call each other directly.

Choose by branching, visibility, ownership, and recovery needs — never by a fixed three-step/four-step cutoff. Both approaches need correlation IDs, duplicate handling, deadlines, retry budgets, and operational views of stuck work. Broker delivery and a workflow engine supply mechanisms; they never automatically define correct compensation.

### 4. Keep sagas out of single-transaction territory

Use one database transaction when the required changes fit its boundary. Two-phase commit can coordinate atomic commit across compatible resource managers, but a coordinator failure can block participants and the protocol adds operational coupling. Choose it when atomicity justifies those costs. A saga accepts intermediate commits and lacks global isolation, although its local transactions may still acquire locks.

When partial state would violate a hard invariant, redesign the boundary or use an appropriate atomic mechanism. Reservations, escrow, and a single authoritative owner can sometimes avoid an immediate cross-service invariant. A refund is a business action, not proof that no charge ever occurred.

## Summary

Trace a workflow through success, definitive failure and uncertain outcome. Persist progress and intent, give retries stable identities and design compensation as recoverable business work. The saga's contract includes visible partial state and operational reconciliation.

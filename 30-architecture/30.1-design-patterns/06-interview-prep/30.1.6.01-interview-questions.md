# Design Patterns Interview Questions

Practice describing the contract and one observable outcome before choosing a pattern name. The later scenarios add storage, concurrency and failure decisions to local object relationships.

## First Principles

Answer the prompt before reading its answer. Name the problem, trace one call or state change, identify a failure boundary and compare a simpler alternative. Use the learning chapters for implementation examples; this bank rehearses explanation and applied decisions.

The scenario questions ask for system mechanisms as well as pattern names. A correct label alone does not establish persistence, concurrency, security or delivery behavior.

## Explain the contract

[Creational chapters](../02-creational/INDEX.md), [structural chapters](../03-structural/INDEX.md), and [behavioral chapters](../04-behavioral/INDEX.md) provide the worked mechanisms.

### 1. What is the Singleton pattern and when would you use it?

**Answer:**
Singleton controls one instance within a defined scope, usually one loaded module or runtime. Processes, workers and independently loaded module copies can have separate instances. It does not establish a distributed singleton.

**When to use:**

- Database connection pools
- Configuration managers
- Logging services
- Caching mechanisms

**Follow-up:** What are the problems with Singleton?

- Global state hides dependencies, so test isolation depends on reset order; callers reach for the instance instead of receiving a dependency, which couples them to the runtime; and shared mutable state adds concurrency questions the type does not express.

---

### 2. What is the Factory pattern and why is it useful?

**Answer:**
Factory techniques centralize creation. A Simple Factory selects a product; Gang of Four (GoF) Factory Method lets a subclass override creation; Abstract Factory creates a compatible family. Identify which mechanism the example actually uses.

**Why useful:**

- Loose coupling
- Easier maintenance
- Better testability
- Flexibility in object creation

---

### 3. What is the Observer pattern and where is it used?

**Answer:**
The Observer pattern defines a one-to-many dependency between objects so that when one object changes state, all dependents are notified.

**Where used:**

- Node.js `EventEmitter` registrations
- Explicit UI or store subscriptions
- Local change notifications

A React state update or a brokered event alone does not prove a GoF Observer implementation. Explain registration, callback timing, cleanup and delivery failure in the actual mechanism.

---

### 4. What is the Adapter pattern?

**Answer:**
An adapter wraps one interface so callers can use it through the contract they already expect. The adapter owns the translation, so its cost is everything the two contracts do not share: error shapes, optional fields, units and object lifetime. A wrapper that renames methods without translating those is a compile-time habit rather than an adapter; the mapping is only exercised where the foreign API's failures arrive.

---

### 5. What is the Builder pattern?

**Answer:**
A builder accumulates configuration across calls and produces the finished object once. Its value is the invariant it can enforce at the final build step, rejecting a half-configured object that a wide constructor or a bag of optional properties would have accepted. Without such a check it is a setter chain with extra types, and a plain options object is easier to read when neither step ordering nor validation is required.

---

### 6. What is the Facade pattern?

**Answer:**
A facade exposes one small interface over several subsystems. The trade is control: callers lose access to options the facade does not surface, and the facade becomes the place where cross-subsystem ordering, retries and partial failure must be decided. Introduce one when that ordering is the recurring problem, not merely to shorten imports or hide a dependency graph from readers.

---

### 7. What is the difference between Creational, Structural, and Behavioral patterns?

**Answer:**
Design patterns are grouped by the structural pressure they manage rather than rigid framework labels. Identifying whether your constraint lies in valid construction, wrapper boundaries, or collaborator communication keeps you from reaching for the wrong category when requirements evolve.

- **Creational**: Deal with object creation (Singleton, Factory, Builder)
- **Structural**: Deal with object composition (Adapter, Facade, Decorator)
- **Behavioral**: Deal with object communication (Observer, Strategy, Chain of Responsibility)

---

### 8. What is the Decorator pattern?

**Answer:**
Decorator wraps a component through the same contract so responsibilities compose without a subclass per combination. Method compatibility alone does not establish behavioral substitution; changing wrapper order can change results and errors.

---

### 9. What is the Proxy pattern?

**Answer:**
A proxy stands in front of a subject through the same contract and decides when or whether the real object is reached. Access control, lazy creation and remoteness share that shape but carry different obligations: a lazy proxy moves cost to first use and needs a failing-initialization path, an access proxy must still enforce the resource's own policy at the target, and a remote proxy turns each local call into a deadline, a serialization mapping and an unknown-outcome problem.

---

### 10. What is the Strategy pattern?

**Answer:**
A strategy puts one interchangeable policy behind a shared contract, and the caller selects which instance to use. Interchangeability is a contract rather than a shared signature: every strategy must accept the same inputs, honor the same side effects and failure expectations, and stay indistinguishable to callers where the choice is meant to be transparent. A strategy that can throw where its siblings cannot, or that mutates shared state, breaks substitution while still compiling.

## Compare neighboring intentions

Use [foundations revision](../01-foundations/REVISION.md), [behavioral revision](../04-behavioral/REVISION.md), and [Repository](../05-architectural/30.1.5.01-repository.md) to check contracts after answering.

### 11. What are the SOLID principles and how do design patterns relate to them?

**Answer:**

- **S**ingle Responsibility: Keep independently changing stakeholder requirements separate; several methods can jointly preserve one coherent responsibility
- **O**pen-Closed: Open for extension, closed for modification
- **L**iskov Substitution: Subtypes must be substitutable for base types
- **I**nterface Segregation: Clients depend only on operations they need
- **D**ependency Inversion: Depend on abstractions, not concretions

Patterns can support particular design principles when their contracts are applied well; their presence does not prove SOLID compliance:

- Strategy → Open-Closed
- Factory → Dependency Inversion
- Interface Segregation → Interfaces split around actual client needs

---

### 12. What is the difference between Strategy and State patterns?

**Answer:**
Both patterns use delegation through a shared contract, yet they govern different kinds of change. Strategy lets the caller supply an interchangeable policy such as pricing or compression, whereas State models lifecycle rules inside an entity, enforcing legal state transitions and throwing on invalid events rather than letting callers pick behavior arbitrarily.

- **Strategy**: Caller selects an interchangeable policy; selection may remain fixed for the object lifetime
- **State**: Object changes behavior and enforces valid transitions as internal lifecycle state evolves

---

### 13. What is the difference between Decorator and Proxy patterns?

**Answer:**
Both wrap a target behind the same interface, yet their responsibilities and failure contracts differ. Decorator layers composable behavior around a component where wrapper order directly changes outputs, retries, or error mapping, whereas Proxy governs reachability, lazy creation, resource lifetime, or remote transport—requiring authorization enforcement, initialization failure handling, timeouts, and duplicate-effect rules at the boundary.

- **Decorator**: Adds composable behavior layers without modifying the wrapped class
- **Proxy**: Controls access, initialization timing, or remote transport while representing the subject

---

### 14. What is the Repository pattern and when should you use it?

**Answer:**
Repository mediates between domain rules and persistence mapping, exposing a domain-focused collection-like contract rather than raw database tables. Use it to keep business invariants testable, isolate database queries, and enforce explicit transaction or optimistic version boundaries—without wrapping an object-relational mapper (ORM) mechanically when a simple direct query fits better.

**When to use:**

- Domain-Driven Design (DDD) aggregates requiring isolated invariants
- Decoupled domain tests that exercise real persistence contracts separately
- Complex domain queries or optimistic concurrency control

---

### 15. What is the Chain of Responsibility pattern?

**Answer:**
Chain of Responsibility passes a request along ordered handlers, each deciding to act or forward it. Its failure modes all concern the path: nothing may handle the request, two handlers may both act, and a handler that re-enters the chain can loop. Decide whether an unhandled request is an error or a silent no-op, and keep the order reviewable as a list, because in a pipeline the order is behavior.

---

### 16. What is the Template Method pattern?

**Answer:**
Template Method fixes the sequence of an algorithm in a base class and leaves named steps for subclasses to override. It buys enforced ordering at the cost of inheritance: subclasses also inherit every other member, and the actual flow is visible only by reading the base. Prefer a composed sequence of functions when variants differ in one step, and reach for this when the ordering itself is the invariant to protect.

---

### 17. What is the Command pattern?

**Answer:**
Command turns a request into a value the caller can store, pass and defer. That object is what makes queueing, logging, retry and undo possible, and each adds its own contract: a queued command must survive serialization, a retried command needs an operation identity so the receiver can recognize a repeat, and undo requires the receiver to have recorded enough to reverse the effect. Without those, a command is a function reference with ceremony.

---

### 18. What is the Mediator pattern?

**Answer:**
Mediator gives one owner responsibility for a cohesive coordination protocol. Participants send relevant events to it rather than reaching into each other; it need not own every communication in the application.

---

### 19. What is the Composite pattern?

**Answer:**
Composite gives leaves and groups one contract so clients can walk a tree without type checks. Uniformity is both the point and the trap: an operation that is meaningful only for a group, or only for a leaf, has to become a silent no-op or a runtime error once it is on the shared interface. Decide which operations are genuinely total over the tree before flattening the types.

---

### 20. What is the Iterator pattern?

**Answer:**
An iterator yields elements in sequence while keeping the aggregate's internals private. The contract that decides its usefulness is what happens when the collection changes underneath it: snapshot the elements, fail on concurrent modification, or define whether inserts are visited. An iterator whose behavior under mutation is unspecified produces skipped and duplicated items that later appear as unrelated bugs.

## Reason about consequences

Use the [category learning flows](../INDEX.md) to find the source of a disputed mechanism; architectural boundaries are compared in [architectural revision](../05-architectural/REVISION.md).

### 21. How do you decide which design pattern to use?

**Answer:**
Start from the volatile responsibility and failure mode rather than working backward from a pattern catalog. Ask whether the pressure lies in valid construction, adapting a contract, or coordinating changing policies before introducing any extra abstraction:

1. **Problem type**: Identify the recurring structural or behavioral friction
2. **Context**: Check runtime constraints, team familiarity, and module boundaries
3. **Trade-offs**: Weigh indirection and wiring overhead against flexibility
4. **Existing patterns**: Align with conventions already proven in the codebase

**Decision framework:**

- Creating objects → Factory, Builder, Singleton
- Adding behavior → Decorator, Strategy
- Simplifying interface → Facade, Adapter
- Managing complexity → Composite, Chain of Responsibility

---

### 22. What is the relationship between Design Patterns and Architecture?

**Answer:**

- **Patterns**: Solutions to recurring design problems
- **Architecture**: High-level structure of the system

Local creation, policy and communication patterns can clarify responsibilities inside any of these architectures. They do not establish service boundaries, dependency direction or durable event delivery by themselves. For example, an event-driven service still needs persisted outgoing intent and consumer progress beyond a callback list.

---

### 23. How do you handle cross-cutting concerns with patterns?

**Answer:**
Cross-cutting concerns—logging, authentication, rate limiting, and transactions—belong at composable seams so handlers stay focused on business behavior. Wire request interception through deterministic middleware pipelines or Decorators, enforce reachability through Proxies, and bind transactions cleanly to the persistence boundary rather than burying hidden commits inside unrelated wrappers:

1. **Decorator Pattern**: Add composable behavior around core operations
2. **Chain of Responsibility**: Structure ordered middleware pipelines
3. **Proxy Pattern**: Enforce access control, caching, or rate limits at boundaries
4. **Aspect-Oriented Programming (AOP)**: Separate infrastructure hooks when supported by the runtime

---

### 24. What are anti-patterns related to design patterns?

**Answer:**
Pattern anti-patterns usually come from introducing indirection for hypothetical reuse before an actual axis of change exists, or from collapsing unrelated responsibilities into one central coordinator that is impossible to test in isolation:

1. **Pattern Overuse**: Introducing hierarchies and factories where a plain function works
2. **Gold Plating**: Adding speculative extension points nobody requested
3. **God Object**: Centralizing unrelated domain and transport logic in one class
4. **Spaghetti Code**: Tangled control and data dependencies that obscure execution order
5. **Resume-Driven Development**: Importing complex architectures without an operational need

---

### 25. How do you test code that uses design patterns?

**Answer:**
Test the contract that the pattern establishes: factory configuration and product compatibility; Strategy input, output, and failure equivalence; Observer order, cleanup and reentrancy; Decorator composition order; Proxy bypass, lifetime and failure behavior. Test database and remote-adapter guarantees at those boundaries rather than relying only on mocks.

---

### 26. How do patterns affect performance?

**Answer:**
Indirection, allocations and retained state have costs, but the pattern name does not determine their size. Trace the dominant work, measure the workload and include cache invalidation, resource lifetime and contention. A lazy proxy may move latency to first use; a singleton may reduce construction while increasing shared-state contention.

---

### 27. What is the relationship between patterns and SOLID principles?

**Answer:**
Suppose a new shipping provider needs a different input from the existing Strategy contract. Do not claim the Open-Closed Principle (OCP) means the client can never change. Decide whether to extend the shared contract, add an adapter or model a separate operation, then check compatibility with existing providers. Naming the pattern does not prove substitution.

---

### 28. How do you handle design patterns in distributed systems?

**Answer:**
A local wrapper or callback becomes a network boundary only through additional mechanisms. A remote Proxy needs deadlines, cancellation, status and serialization mapping and duplicate-effect handling. A brokered notification needs durable progress, acknowledgment, retry and ordering rules. Process-local Singleton identity does not coordinate service replicas.

---

### 29. What are modern alternatives to classic design patterns?

**Answer:**
Language mechanisms often make the same intention simpler: functions can express Strategy or local Command; generators implement Iterator; modules or scoped providers can own shared services. A Dependency Injection (DI) factory is not automatically GoF Factory Method, and a reactive stream has more delivery semantics than a local Observer list.

---

### 30. How do you refactor code to use design patterns?

**Answer:**
Refactor toward a pattern only after locking down observable behavior with characterization tests and isolating the specific duplication or branching that keeps breaking under change. Introduce one narrow seam at a time—such as an Adapter or Strategy behind a switching hotspot—and verify each incremental step rather than rewriting whole module trees speculatively:

1. **Identify friction**: Locate repeated variation or tangled branching in changing code
2. **Capture baseline behavior**: Write tests covering current success and failure outputs
3. **Introduce a seam**: Extract the contract behind a Strategy, Adapter, or boundary
4. **Refactor incrementally**: Land small verified changes and document why the seam exists

---

### 31. What is the impact of design patterns on code maintainability?

**Answer:**
Well-chosen patterns isolate volatile rules behind explicit contracts, localize bug fixes, and give reviewers shared names for ownership boundaries. Overused patterns degrade maintainability by scattering one cohesive flow across unnecessary classes, hiding runtime control flow behind indirect dispatch, and increasing the cognitive load needed to trace a single request.

**Positive:**

- Clear structure, ownership boundaries, and shared team vocabulary
- Isolated policy changes with lower regression risk

**Negative:**

- Unnecessary indirection and file sprawl when simpler code suffices
- Harder call-site tracing and debugging when lifetimes or wrapper order stay implicit

---

### 32. How do you handle design patterns in legacy code?

**Answer:**
Start by stabilizing observable outputs and side effects with characterization tests before restructuring legacy internals. Isolate quirky external or legacy data behind an Adapter or Anti-Corruption Layer (ACL) seam, extract volatile conditionals into focused Strategies step by step, and remove migration glue once old callers are retired rather than attempting a risky big-bang rewrite:

1. **Protect existing behavior**: Lock in current inputs, outputs, and error edges with tests
2. **Insert a Strangler Fig seam**: Route new capabilities cleanly alongside legacy paths
3. **Wrap with an Adapter or ACL**: Keep legacy field assumptions out of new modules
4. **Migrate incrementally**: Move one high-churn slice at a time while behavior stays green

---

### 33. What is the relationship between patterns and frameworks?

**Answer:**
Describe the actual mechanism in framework code: Express routes requests through middleware; a UI component tree composes rendering; provider registration constructs dependencies. A language `@decorator` can attach metadata without wrapping an object through a shared GoF interface. Framework lifecycle callbacks may use composition rather than Template Method inheritance.

---

### 34. How do you handle design patterns in microservices?

**Answer:**
Start with service boundaries, authoritative invariants and communication contracts. Apply local creation, policy and wrapper patterns inside a service where useful. Across services, account for durable intent, retries, idempotency, ordering and partial state. Command Query Responsibility Segregation (CQRS), sagas and event sourcing are optional choices solving different problems, not a mandatory package.

---

### 35. What are the trade-offs of using design patterns?

**Answer:**
Every pattern trades direct, inline code for a named indirection seam, buying flexibility along one axis while adding files, object wiring, allocation cost, and onboarding overhead. Adopt a pattern only when the team's recurring change burden outweighs those lifecycle, debugging, and cognitive-load costs:

| Benefit | Trade-off |
| --------------- | ---------------------------- |
| Code reuse | Added indirection complexity |
| Clear structure | Team learning curve |
| Testability | More boundaries and wiring |
| Maintainability | Potential over-engineering |

---

### 36. How do you explain design patterns to non-technical stakeholders?

**Answer:**
Use analogies:

- **Factory**: Like a manufacturing assembly line
- **Strategy**: Like different routes to the same destination
- **Facade**: Like a hotel concierge
- **Decorator**: Like adding toppings to a pizza

Connect the analogy to a concrete consequence: a carrier adapter lets checkout use one shipping contract while provider-specific status mapping stays at the boundary. Explain the extra mapping and tests it requires; a pattern name does not promise faster development or better quality.

---

### 37. What is the role of design patterns in code reviews?

**Answer:**
In review, patterns serve as a quick shared vocabulary for evaluating boundary ownership, substitution rules, and error paths rather than a checklist to force extra classes onto simple features:

- **Identify pattern usage**: Verify the implementation matches the pattern's contract
- **Spot over-engineering**: Replace speculative indirection with direct functions where clearer
- **Audit contracts**: Check the change owner, failure handling, and client expectations
- **Share knowledge**: Align the team on naming and boundary conventions

---

### 38. How do design patterns evolve over time?

**Answer:**
Host languages gradually absorb classic object wrappers into native features—first-class functions replace one-method Strategies, generators standardize Iterators, and `async` and `await` flatten callback chains. At the same time, distributed architectures and reactive UIs drive newer boundary patterns such as transactional outboxes, sagas, circuit breakers, and explicit hook ownership:

- **Classic patterns**: Foundational GoF contracts adapted cleanly when host primitives fall short
- **Language-level replacements**: TypeScript decorators, generators, closures, and `async` and `await` workflows
- **Reactive and frontend boundaries**: Component state ownership and hook synchronization rules
- **Distributed architecture patterns**: Event-driven messaging, sagas, and failure-isolation seams

---

### 39. What are the most overused design patterns?

**Answer:**
Avoid reaching for a pattern out of habit before verifying that an ordinary function, module, or explicit dependency cannot carry the requirement cleanly. Singleton, Factory, Observer, and Decorator are the most common culprits when simple direct wiring would make dependencies and call order obvious:

1. **Singleton**: Often used when explicit dependency injection makes state and test lifetimes clear
2. **Factory**: Applied over simple constructors or object literals that have no conditional logic
3. **Observer**: Used where a direct function call provides clearer ownership and failure handling
4. **Decorator**: Layered deeply when explicit configuration is far easier to trace and debug

---

### 40. How do you stay updated with design pattern trends?

**Answer:**
Skip superficial catalog summaries and study how real systems actually enforce boundaries under load and change. Inspect framework internals, read Architecture Decision Records (ADRs), requests for comments (RFCs), and incident postmortems to see where abstractions leaked, and prototype competing designs against failure cases before bringing a pattern to production:

- Trace production framework source code (DI containers, middleware pipelines, and routers)
- Study engineering postmortems, RFCs, and ADRs to learn failure and maintenance limits
- Compare competing implementations in small focused prototypes under error injection
- Review open-source architecture boundaries and discuss trade-offs in code reviews

## Apply the ideas to concrete systems

Check [Saga](../05-architectural/30.1.5.04-saga.md), [Adapter](../03-structural/30.1.3.01-adapter.md), and [Proxy](../03-structural/30.1.3.04-proxy.md) for the local and remote boundaries used below.

### 41. Design a URL shortener using design patterns.

**Answer:**
Define link creation, durable short-code uniqueness, destination validation, expiry and redirect behavior first. A generator policy can be a Strategy and a cache can be a Proxy, but a factory does not make randomly generated codes collision-free. Persist a unique mapping atomically and retry collisions; scope cache keys and invalidate expired or changed links.

---

### 42. Design a real-time notification system.

**Answer:**
Define notification identity, recipient and channel choice, persisted outgoing intent, retries and delivery evidence. Strategies and adapters can isolate channel APIs. A local Observer may notify in-process consumers; durable cross-process delivery needs a queue and idempotent handling. Provider acceptance is not proof that a user received the notification.

---

### 43. Design a plugin system for an application.

**Answer:**
Use an explicit approved plugin registry, validate manifests and negotiate capability and version contracts. Factories can construct plugins and Strategy can select behavior. A Proxy wrapper is not a sandbox: untrusted code needs an actual isolation boundary and capability restrictions. Define startup, errors, resource limits and disposal.

---

### 44. Design a payment processing system.

**Answer:**
Separate payment intent from provider attempts. Adapters translate provider meaning, Strategies select supported providers and a facade can expose focused operations. Persist a stable operation key, fingerprint and status; authorize each operation and reconcile uncertain outcomes. A timeout does not prove failure, and switching providers during an unknown outcome can double-charge.

---

### 45. Design a caching layer for a web application.

**Answer:**
Start with cache identity, freshness, capacity and invalidation after authoritative commit. A Proxy can wrap access and Strategy can choose eviction. Include tenant and caller visibility where the result depends on them; deduplicate safe in-flight loads and define negative-cache behavior. Notification-based invalidation still needs recovery from missed or duplicate messages.

## Defend and refine a design

### 46. How would you refactor a codebase that doesn't use design patterns?

**Answer:**
Choose one concrete behavior that is hard to change or trace. Capture its observable contract, introduce a boundary incrementally and verify representative success and failure cases. Pattern-free code is not inherently defective; preserve a simpler direct design when it already communicates the behavior clearly.

---

### 47. How do you handle design pattern disagreements in a team?

**Answer:**
Ground pattern debates in concrete change scenarios, failure paths, and day-to-day debuggability rather than personal preference or catalog purity. Prototype the direct alternative alongside the pattern on one slice of the feature so reviewers can weigh testability, cognitive load, and operational trace cost objectively:

1. **Compare concrete trade-offs**: Measure added indirection against the change cost saved
2. **Fit existing context**: Match established codebase seams unless they clearly fail
3. **Prototype both options**: Test readability and error paths on one real module
4. **Decide with a reviewable bar**: Avoid analysis paralysis and revisit if assumptions change

---

### 48. What questions should you ask before applying a design pattern?

**Answer:**
Before introducing a pattern seam, verify what requirement is genuinely volatile today, who owns resource cleanup and concurrent state when operations fail, and whether a direct function would keep behavior clearer for teammates. Favor reversible interfaces so structure earns its place only when change pressure arrives:

1. What concrete problem or repeated change are we solving today?
2. Would a direct function, union, or module remain clearer right now?
3. What indirection, allocation, and debugging costs does this add?
4. Who owns state, error propagation, and resource cleanup across failure paths?
5. How will new teammates test and trace this boundary six months from now?

---

### 49. How do you document design pattern usage?

**Answer:**
Never stop at naming a pattern—record the invariant the boundary protects, the concurrency or failure assumptions callers must honor, and the simpler alternatives that were rejected and why. Capture architectural boundaries in concise Architecture Decision Records (ADRs) paired with sequence diagrams for tricky error paths, and keep inline comments focused on non-obvious constraints:

- **ADRs**: Document the chosen invariant, failure boundaries, and rejected alternatives
- **Sequence diagrams**: Trace control flow, retries, and error propagation visually
- **Code comments**: Explain why an invariant or ordering constraint exists
- **Module READMEs**: Point maintainers to the composition root and contract tests

---

### 50. What's the most important thing to remember about design patterns?

**Answer:**
**Patterns are tools, not goals.** Use them when they solve a real problem, not just because they exist. The best pattern is the one that makes your code simpler, more maintainable, and easier to understand.

---

## Summary

Explain each intention through a concrete trace and its boundary conditions. A strong answer states who owns creation, delivery, state and recovery, and when a simpler design is enough.

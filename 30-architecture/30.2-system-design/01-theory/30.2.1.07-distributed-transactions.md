# Distributed Transactions

> Choose atomic commit when an invariant requires it; choose a recoverable workflow when intermediate states are acceptable.

## Definition

A **distributed transaction** coordinates one logical transaction across multiple resource managers or database partitions. Atomic commit requires every participant to reach the same commit/abort decision. A **saga** is a different contract: a workflow of committed local transactions with business compensation, which can expose intermediate states.

A shared or replicated coordinator can exist. Consensus orders replicated state, but does not by itself make independent shards' transactions atomic; distributed databases often combine consensus replication with an atomic commit protocol.

## First Principles

1. Once a participant promises it can commit, it cannot independently forget that promise without risking disagreement with peers.
2. A network timeout is uncertainty, not proof of abort. Recovery must discover the durable decision before contradicting it.
3. A committed local action cannot generally be erased from the world. Compensation is a later business action and may fail or be impossible.

## Select the Required Invariant

An order, inventory reservation and external payment may allow a visible pending state and later refund. A ledger invariant may require entries to commit together. Define that boundary before choosing a protocol. Keep the invariant in one local transaction when practical; a workflow crossing services does not automatically require global atomicity.

| Contract | Mechanism | Consequence |
| ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| All participants commit or abort | Atomic commit, commonly 2PC | Prepared participants retain durable state and may wait for recovery |
| Local commits with business recovery | Saga | Intermediate state, idempotent retries and compensation |
| One replicated transaction domain | Database's transaction protocol plus replication | Depends on its partitioning, isolation and failure contract |

## Two-Phase Commit

1. **Prepare:** each participant durably records sufficient state to commit and votes yes, or votes no. Prepared transactions typically retain locks or other conflict-control resources.
2. **Decide:** after every yes vote, the coordinator durably records commit; otherwise it records abort. It then delivers the decision, retrying as needed.

A participant recovering in the prepared state must learn the decision. Classic 2PC can block if that decision cannot be reached. A replicated coordinator improves recoverability and availability but does not remove every partition or participant dependency. A lost reply after a durable commit does not turn the transaction into an abort.

2PC is used in production databases, including transactions across shards. Whether it is suitable across application services depends on resource-manager support, failure recovery, latency and organizational boundaries; it is not categorically forbidden or limited to small systems.

## Three-Phase Commit

3PC inserts a pre-commit phase to permit nonblocking recovery under particular failure and bounded-timing assumptions. Those assumptions do not hold in a fully asynchronous, arbitrarily partitioned network. Timeouts alone cannot distinguish a slow peer from an unreachable one while preserving every desired agreement and progress guarantee.

Understand its assumptions instead of treating the additional phase as a general cure for 2PC blocking.

## Sagas and Compensation

```text
create pending order → reserve inventory → charge payment → confirm order
                              ↓ failure or uncertain outcome
                       reconcile, then release/refund/cancel as appropriate
```

Each step has its own local atomicity. A refund compensates a charge; it does not erase the charge or its observation. A timed-out payment may already have succeeded, so reconcile it by stable provider identity before charging again or assuming failure.

An orchestrator persists the workflow state and schedules steps. A durable replicated workflow engine need not be a single machine or a single point of failure. Choreography distributes reactions through events; the broker, subscriptions and causal flow still have failure modes and need observability.

| Style | Useful property | Cost |
| ------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Orchestration | Explicit progress, deadlines and recovery state | Operating and evolving the coordinator |
| Choreography | Services react through event contracts | Recovering an overall view of dependencies and progress |

Synchronous requests can implement saga steps; asynchronous delivery is useful for long waits and independent recovery. Neither transport supplies durability or compensation automatically. Use the canonical [Saga chapter](../../../20-backend/20.6-microservices/03-data-and-consistency/20.6.3.01-saga-pattern.md) for implementation examples rather than duplicating its state machine here.

## Recovery Mechanisms to Specify

- Stable operation identities for steps and compensations; record their effects at the correct transactional boundary.
- Durable intent/publication through an outbox where a local write must emit an event.
- Deadlines, retry budgets and an explicit uncertain-outcome/reconciliation state.
- Isolation of pending business objects so other workflows do not mistake them for finalized state.
- A recovery queue or manual resolution path when compensation fails or the external system cannot reverse an effect.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Calling compensation an ACID rollback | Intermediate observations and external effects remain |
| Assuming consensus alone commits all independent shards | No cross-participant atomic decision has been established |
| Marking a timeout as a definitive payment failure | A retry can charge twice |
| Keeping orchestration progress only in process memory | Restart loses the recovery state |
| Treating choreography as failure-free | Broker outages and missing subscriptions can stall the workflow |
| Promising a saga is always faster or available | Its steps and recovery dependencies determine that behavior |

## Summary

Atomic commit and recoverable workflows solve different contracts. 2PC makes a durable shared commit/abort decision and can block during uncertainty. Sagas expose local commits and recover through explicit compensation and reconciliation. Choose the invariant first, then specify durable state, retry identity, isolation and failure recovery.

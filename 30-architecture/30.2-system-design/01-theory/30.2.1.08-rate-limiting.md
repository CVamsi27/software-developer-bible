# Rate Limiting

One partner sends requests to six API replicas, each with its own limit of 100 requests per second. The fleet can admit up to 600 per second. Before choosing a token bucket or Redis, decide whether the contract is per replica or one shared partner budget:

```text
6 replicas × 100 req/s local limits = 600 req/s fleet — per replica or shared?
```

## Definition

**Rate limiting** is **admission control** bounding the request rate or weighted work admitted for a defined identity, and specifying what happens when the allowance is exhausted. It is a resource-protection or fairness policy, not a direct bound on simultaneous in-flight work.

> **Textbook Definition:** Rate limiting is admission control that bounds requests or resource consumption for a defined identity and interval, with specified burst allowances and behavior when the limit is exceeded.

## First Principles

1. **Concurrent admissions consume one authority's budget.** A fixed-window counter must check/update atomically; a token bucket must update credit and refill time together. Strict fleet-wide limits require coordination or safely allocated sub-budgets. The enforcement point can be a gateway or application, not necessarily the network edge.
2. **State encodes the burst contract.** An exact rolling window retains event times; a token bucket retains credit and refill time. Less state or a cheaper estimate changes the precision and burst behavior the limiter can promise.
3. **Loss of the budget authority forces an admission policy.** Rejecting preserves a strict protection bound at an availability cost; admitting can exceed it. Safely preallocated local budgets permit some admission without contacting the authority. This is an explicit failure contract; calling every limiter a CAP example obscures the actual consistency requirement.

## 1. Trace each algorithm's burst contract

Four elements define a limiter; changing any one changes the guarantee.

**Key** — per user, per IP, per API key, per tenant, per endpoint, or global. The key is the fairness unit: per-IP limits can group many users behind a NAT, while a user ID is unavailable until authentication succeeds. Trust forwarded client addresses only when a trusted proxy removes caller-supplied forwarding headers and supplies its own.

**Algorithm** — how the limiter remembers recent demand and handles bursts: fixed or sliding windows, a token bucket, or a leaky bucket (traced below).

**Limit and cost** — a limiter may count *weighted cost* rather than bare requests (a search costs 10, a read costs 1).

**Rejection behavior** — reject, queue within a bounded delay, or degrade; for HTTP, `429` may include `Retry-After` when the server can give a useful wait time.

For a fleet-wide limit, independent per-instance counters can multiply the allowance by the number of instances. Shared state can enforce one coordinated budget; allocated per-instance sub-budgets trade some utilization for fewer coordination calls.

Apply a limit where it protects a constrained resource or enforces a real fairness policy; not every endpoint needs one.

**Fixed window:** count requests in fixed time windows; reset at the boundary.

```text
Window 1 (00:00 - 00:59): 100 requests allowed
Window 2 (01:00 - 01:59): 100 requests allowed
```

A fixed window is simple — just an integer counter — but a burst fits at the boundary: with 100 per minute, a caller can make nearly 100 requests just before the boundary and 100 just after, approaching 200 in a short interval. **Use for** low-stakes APIs and simple cases.

**Sliding window:** count requests in a sliding window (last 60 seconds, not clock-aligned). Two implementations: a **sliding log** storing every request timestamp and counting those in the last 60s (exact rolling count, but memory-intensive at one entry per request); a **sliding counter estimate** tracking current- and previous-window counts and estimating with a weighted average (constant per-key state, but approximate):

```text
current_window_count = 80
previous_window_count = 100
elapsed_in_current_window = 30s (out of 60s)

sliding_count ≈ previous_window_count * (1 - 30/60) + current_window_count
              = 100 * 0.5 + 80
              = 50 + 80
              = 130
```

The exact log gives an exact rolling count; the two-counter estimate reduces the aligned-window discontinuity with constant per-key state. The estimate assumes the previous window's events spread evenly — a skewed arrival pattern makes its answer inaccurate, so it is no strict rolling-window guarantee. **Use** a rolling-window contract when the policy promises that bound; choose exact versus approximate state deliberately.

**Token bucket:** a bucket holds N tokens; each request consumes 1; tokens refill at rate R per second; empty means reject:

```text
Bucket capacity: 100 tokens
Refill rate: 10 tokens/sec

t=0: bucket has 100 tokens
t=0: request → bucket has 99 tokens
...
t=1: bucket has 100 tokens (99 + 10 refill tokens, capped; no intervening requests)
```

Allows bursts up to bucket capacity, refills credit at a configured rate up to capacity, and stays simple to implement — at the cost of tracking token count per key. **Use when** the policy needs a sustained refill rate plus an explicit maximum burst. A bucket with capacity `C` and refill rate `r` admits at most the stored credit plus credit replenished over the interval; it never promises a constant output rate. A provider may layer several throttles and quotas; never infer its complete contract from one algorithm.

**Leaky bucket:** in the queueing form, requests enter a bounded queue and drain at a fixed rate; when full, reject. A drop-based leaky bucket can reject excess traffic immediately instead of queueing it:

```text
Queue capacity: 100
Process rate: 10 req/sec

Requests come in → enqueue (up to 100)
Process loop: dequeue + process at 10/sec
If queue is full → reject new requests
```

The queueing form gives a smooth output rate but accepts a bounded input burst, adding wait time; the drop-based form sheds excess work instead. Neither guarantees a request completes within its deadline unless queue delay and service time are bounded. **Use when** strict rate smoothing is needed. Less common than token bucket.

| Algorithm | Allows bursts? | Memory | Complexity | Best for |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------- | ---------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Fixed window | Yes (boundary) | Low | Very low | Low-stakes APIs |
| Exact sliding log | Up to the rolling-window allowance | O(admitted events in window) | Medium | Exact rolling count |
| Sliding counter estimate | Approximate rolling allowance | O(1) per key | Medium | Lower state cost with documented error |
| Token bucket | Yes (up to capacity) | Low | Medium | Sustained budget with bounded burst |
| Queued leaky bucket | Can enqueue a bounded burst; drains smoothly | O(queue capacity) | Medium | Output shaping when queue delay is bounded |

Choose by contract: a token bucket fits a sustained rate with bounded burst credit; an exact sliding log fits a strict rolling-window count. If queueing is allowed, a leaky bucket can shape output at the cost of waiting time.

## 2. Key the budget to the fairness unit

| Key | Use case | Pros | Cons |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ | --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **IP** | Public APIs, anonymous traffic | Easy (no auth needed) | Network address translation (NAT) or carrier-grade NAT (CGNAT) means many users share an IP |
| **User ID** | Authenticated APIs | Fair per user | Need to be authenticated |
| **API key** | B2B APIs | Tracks partner usage | Partners share one key for many users |
| **Tenant ID** | B2B SaaS | Fair per tenant | One tenant can have many users |
| **Endpoint** | Protect specific expensive endpoints | Limits resource use | Doesn't limit per user |
| **Composite** (user + endpoint) | Most production systems | Granular | More state to track |

**In production:** stack keys — e.g. "100 req/sec per user" AND "10000 req/sec per tenant" AND "1000 req/sec on /search endpoint globally." Derive authenticated identities from verified credentials. A caller-controlled API key can identify a credential, but never proves the caller is authorized to use every resource named by the request.

## 3. Enforce where identity and resource meet

| Layer | Pros | Cons |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Edge (CDN, WAF)** | Can reject before origin work | Usually has network and token signals; business identity may require a trusted gateway or verified token |
| **API Gateway** | Centralized policy, sees all traffic | One more thing to maintain |
| **Application** | Full context, can vary by business logic | Per-app logic, harder to enforce globally |
| **Database / service** | Protects a specific downstream dependency or expensive operation | Work upstream of that point has already been spent; local limits may not represent the fleet total |

Choose enforcement where the needed identity and protected resource are visible. A gateway replicated across machines still needs coordinated state or allocated sub-budgets for a strict fleet allowance. Layer coarse early protection and precise application budgets when both address a real risk. Rate bounds never directly bound in-flight concurrency: slow requests can occupy many workers at a low arrival rate. Application rate limits cannot protect a network link already saturated by volumetric traffic; use upstream filtering and capacity controls for that boundary.

## 4. Return a useful rejection

- **HTTP 429 Too Many Requests** — standardized for rate limiting. The response may include `Retry-After`, either a delay in seconds or an HTTP date; a 429 response must not be stored by a cache.
- **Rate-limit metadata** — header names and meanings vary by API. The current IETF `RateLimit` header-field document is an active Internet-Draft, not a published RFC; check its Datatracker status and the server's contract before relying on it.

```http
HTTP/1.1 429 Too Many Requests
Content-Type: application/json
Retry-After: 60

{
  "error": "rate_limited",
  "message": "Too many requests. Try again in 60 seconds."
}
```

## 5. Coordinate the budget across the fleet

Independent counters can implement intentional per-instance protection, but never the same full fleet allowance (100 on A plus 100 on B permits 200). State whether the promised budget is local or global.

**Centralized store (Redis):** all servers share a Redis counter, incremented per request and checked against the limit:

```text
Server A: INCR rate:user:123 → 1
Server B: INCR rate:user:123 → 2
...
```

One atomic authority prevents two callers independently granting the same remaining allowance — at the cost of a Redis dependency and a network call's latency.

**First-arrival window in Redis with Lua:** this illustrative script validates positive integer configuration before changing state, then counts attempts atomically on one Redis authority. The first attempt starts the time to live (TTL); it is a first-arrival window, not a sliding-window algorithm or a clock-aligned fixed window. Denied attempts also increment the count, but never extend expiry. Concurrent access, replication/failover loss, and unavailable-store behavior remain part of the system contract:

```lua
local key = KEYS[1]
local limit = tonumber(ARGV[1])
local window = tonumber(ARGV[2])
local max_safe_integer = 9007199254740991
if not limit or not window or limit ~= math.floor(limit) or window ~= math.floor(window)
  or limit < 1 or window < 1 or limit > max_safe_integer or window > max_safe_integer then
  return redis.error_reply('limit and window must be positive safe integers')
end
local current = redis.call('INCR', key)
if current == 1 then
  redis.call('EXPIRE', key, window)
end
if current > limit then
  return 0 -- denied
end
return 1 -- allowed
```

**Token bucket in Redis:** store each key's remaining tokens and last-refill time. On every request, atomically: compute `tokens = min(capacity, stored_tokens + elapsed_seconds × refill_rate)`; if `tokens` covers the request's cost, subtract and allow, else deny; store the new count and timestamp. Use one authoritative clock and execute the read, calculation, and write as one script or server-side function so concurrent requests cannot spend the same credit. Expire inactive keys only after at least one full refill interval (`capacity / refill_rate`) — earlier expiry can grant a fresh full bucket before the prior bucket would naturally refill.

**Per-instance approximation:** each server holds a partial view. Two independent full allowances can permit twice the intended rate; calling local state a sliding counter never synchronizes it. Allocate per-instance budgets or explicitly accept the multiplied/approximate allowance.

| Implementation | Scope and behavior to verify |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Redis + Lua** | One Redis primary can atomically update shared state; failover, eviction, latency and store outage still affect the guarantee. |
| **NGINX `limit_req`** | Uses a leaky-bucket method with configurable burst handling; the key is configurable, so it need not be an IP. |
| **Envoy local rate limit** | Uses a token bucket in each Envoy process; it protects locally and does not by itself create one fleet-wide allowance. |
| **Envoy global rate limit** | Calls an external gRPC rate-limit service for a shared decision; this adds a dependency and network hop. |
| **Amazon API Gateway REST API** | Throttling targets use token buckets; configured limits are best-effort targets rather than guaranteed ceilings. |

## Apply the Contract

| Scenario | Budget key or cost | Failure boundary to explain |
| -------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Public partner API | Verified credential and tenant; include a shared fleet budget when promised | Independent replica limits multiply. Publish quota semantics and return useful retry guidance. |
| Login and password reset | Combine source and account signals where available | Enforce before expensive authentication work without exposing whether an account exists; use upstream controls for volumetric traffic. |
| Search and export | Charge by estimated work, not only request count | A rate limit does not cap concurrent long-running jobs; add concurrency bounds or a bounded queue. |
| Webhooks and notifications | Per trusted sender plus provider quota or cost | Retries need bounded backoff and idempotency; local admission does not guarantee downstream acceptance. |
| Paid tenant tiers | Per-tenant quota plus endpoint or global protection | Separate the customer contract from best-effort infrastructure throttles; monitor legitimate rejections before changing policy. |

## Common Mistakes

- Checking after the protected work. Derive a trusted identity and cost, then admit before the expensive operation; protect earlier stages separately if they are also costly.
- Calling local state a global quota. Independent instance counters multiply the total allowance unless budgets are partitioned or coordinated.
- Returning no useful wait information. Include `Retry-After` when the retry time is known; clients should still use bounded backoff and jitter.
- Scattering limits across services. Centralize the policy and make effective limits observable.
- No monitoring. Track rejection rates by endpoint, tenant and identity to distinguish misconfiguration, abuse and normal growth.
- Rate limiting only the API. Protect authentication, password-reset and export flows too; they can enable brute force, message abuse or data exfiltration.
- Limits disconnected from the contracted tier or resource budget. Publish the allowance, observe legitimate rejection, and adjust capacity or policy; paid traffic still needs a protection boundary.

## Summary

Rate limiting controls how many requests a caller may make in a period. Choose the caller identity, allowed rate, burst behavior and response after the limit is reached. Fixed windows are simple but allow bursts at a boundary; token buckets allow bounded bursts; sliding windows smooth the boundary more closely. Charge weighted cost when operations differ, and add a separate concurrency bound for long-running work. Multiple servers need a shared or deliberately partitioned limit. A 429 response can include Retry-After when the client should wait.

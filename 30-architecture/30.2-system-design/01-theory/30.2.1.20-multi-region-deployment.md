# Multi-Region Deployment

Serving users from multiple regions can reduce network distance and provide a recovery option when a region fails. It also adds data replication, routing, consistency, and operational work. The right design starts with the required user experience and recovery targets, then chooses where each kind of data is written and read.

## Definition

**Multi-region deployment** runs parts of a system in geographically separate locations to improve regional latency or recovery options. Regions reduce exposure to some failures, but can still share provider control planes, identity services, networks, or other dependencies. The design chooses — **per data type** — where writes land and how they replicate.

> **Textbook Definition:** Multi-region deployment places application and data components in geographically separated regions, with explicit policies for traffic routing, write authority, replication, and recovery across regional failures.

**RPO (recovery point objective)** is the maximum acceptable recent data loss, expressed as time. **RTO (recovery time objective)** is the target time to restore the service after a disruption.

Two common drivers motivate it, and they pull in different directions:

- **Disaster recovery** — survive losing a whole region. Here **RPO/RTO** dominate, and one writable region at a time (active-passive) is usually enough.
- **Latency** — serve users from a nearby region. Here routing and read locality dominate, which pushes toward active-active serving.

The design is therefore two decisions, not one:

1. **Topology** — *single-region* (regional failure risk), *active-passive* (one region writes; standby promoted on failure), or *active-active* (every region serves, and possibly writes).
2. **Replication and conflict strategy** — synchronous replication can preserve acknowledged writes for a defined failure model, at the cost of cross-region coordination; asynchronous replication can lose writes that have not reached another region. Quorum behavior depends on the protocol. Multi-writer data also needs a conflict rule or a single write owner.

Regions can become isolated from one another. During that partition, the system must follow its consistency and availability contract; multi-region does not remove the trade-off. Different data can use different policies, but every policy must preserve its business invariants during failover and recovery.

---

## First Principles

Multi-region decisions follow from network distance, failure targets, and data-handling requirements:

1. **Distance adds network delay.** Serving reads from a nearby replica or cache can reduce round trips, but a local read may be stale. The consistency model sets what the user can observe.
2. **Writes need an authority.** A system can route writes to one region, partition ownership, or coordinate writes across regions. Each option changes latency, availability, and conflict handling.
3. **Recovery needs a tested procedure.** Replication lag affects the recovery point objective (RPO); detection, promotion, and traffic routing affect recovery time objective (RTO). Split-brain prevention determines whether two regions can both accept writes safely.

## Why it matters

Consider multiple regions when measured latency, recovery objectives, or contractual data-location requirements justify the cost and operational complexity. A multi-region deployment does not itself guarantee a particular availability level; dependencies, data recovery, quotas, traffic control, and tested procedures all affect the result.

## 1. Deployment Topologies

### Single-region serving

```text
        users
         │
    GeoDNS / LB
         │
    ┌────┴────────┐
    │ us-east-1   │
    │ (active)    │
    └─────────────┘
```

- **RPO/RTO** depend on the backup, recovery, and restore process; they must be measured in exercises.
- **Use when**: the service accepts one region's failure domain and does not require regional recovery.

### Active-Passive (hot standby)

```text
   users in supported regions
              │
        traffic routing
              │
              ▼
          Region A ─── replication ───► Region B
          (active)                     (standby)

Normal traffic goes to A. Route to B only after the tested
fencing, promotion, and dependency-readiness procedure.
```

- **In this configuration**, reads and writes use the active region; writes replicate asynchronously to the standby. Other standby configurations can use synchronous replication or serve selected reads.
- **Failover** can be manual or automated. Transfer write authority using the datastore's protocol, then update routing; a DNS change alone cannot safely promote a writer.
- **RPO** depends on replication mode and lag when the failure occurs.
- **RTO** depends on detection, authority transfer, capacity, and traffic routing; DNS caching is only one possible delay.
- **Use when**: one write authority is acceptable and the service needs a separately tested recovery region.

### Active-Active (multi-region writes)

```text
Users near Region A ──► Region A (serves traffic and accepts configured writes)
                          ⇄ replication / coordination ⇄
Users near Region B ──► Region B (serves traffic and accepts configured writes)
```

- **Reads** can use a nearby region when its consistency is acceptable. **Writes** must follow the configured ownership or coordination protocol; proximity does not confer write authority.
- **Writes** propagate to other regions asynchronously or via a quorum.
- **Multi-region writes = conflict potential.** See section 4.
- **RPO/RTO** depend on the consistency protocol, topology, and failure scenario; active-active traffic alone does not make either zero.
- **Use when**: traffic needs local serving in multiple regions and the application has an explicit write-ownership or conflict policy.

### Home-region writes with regional reads

One possible hybrid setup is:
- **Per-region primary for writes** (e.g. each user has a home region; writes go there)
- **Cross-region reads** served from local replicas
- **Replication** can be async; normal-operation write ownership prevents same-key writers only when every write follows that ownership rule

Home-region ownership can avoid concurrent writes for the same key during normal operation, but failover still needs fencing or a clear ownership transfer. Local reads may lag behind the home region.

## 2. Routing: How Traffic Gets to a Region

| Mechanism | Resolution | Latency to redirect | Best for |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| **DNS-based routing** | DNS resolver receives an address based on policy and health signals | Changes are affected by TTLs and client/resolver caching | Coarse regional selection |
| **Anycast network** | BGP routes an address toward an advertised network location | Route convergence and provider policy affect changes | Edge ingress and network-level routing |
| **HTTP proxy or load balancer** | Routes requests using HTTP metadata and health state | Can change routing per request | Application-aware routing |
| **CDN** | Serves cached content or selects an origin | Cache and origin policies determine behavior | Static content and supported dynamic paths |
| **Client routing** | Client chooses among configured endpoints | Depends on client refresh and fallback logic | Applications that can safely manage endpoint choice |

Routing layers can be combined. For example, a DNS or anycast edge can direct traffic to a regional proxy, which then routes to a service. Health checks must represent the ability to serve useful requests; a failed control plane or unhealthy dependency can make a green process check misleading.

**The DNS caching problem:** changing a DNS answer does not immediately move every client. Resolvers, client caches, open connections, health-check intervals, and failover automation all affect the observed recovery time. Anycast and proxy routing have their own convergence and health-detection behavior; none guarantees instant failover.

## 3. Cross-Region Replication

### Synchronous

- The write acknowledgment waits for the remote replicas or quorum required by the protocol. The leader need not be in the caller's region.
- Acknowledged writes can survive a regional failure only if the replication protocol and failure model guarantee that outcome. This may reduce write availability during a partition.
- **Latency cost** includes the quorum or replica round trips required by the protocol. Measure it for the chosen topology.
- **Examples**: Spanner documents synchronous replication and strong consistency; other databases have different protocols and configuration constraints.
- **Use when**: the consistency requirement justifies cross-region coordination and the application can accept its latency and availability effects.

### Asynchronous

- **Writes** wait for the local acknowledgment contract, then replicate remotely in the background; local durability can still add waiting.
- A regional write can be acknowledged before other regions receive it. The potential loss on regional failure depends on the replication lag and recovery behavior at that moment.
- **Latency cost** is lower than waiting for remote acknowledgment, but remote reads can be stale and concurrent writes need a defined resolution policy.
- **Example**: DynamoDB Global Tables' multi-Region eventual consistency (MREC) mode replicates asynchronously, item by item. A transaction is atomic in its source Region but is not replicated as one unit, so another Region can temporarily observe only some of its writes. Conflicting writes to one item resolve by an internal-timestamp last-writer-wins rule. Its multi-Region strong consistency (MRSC) mode coordinates cross-region writes and supports zero RPO, with higher write and strong-read latency. MRSC requires exactly three Regions from one supported Region set: three replicas, or two replicas plus a witness that cannot serve reads or writes. It does not support transactions, TTL, or local secondary indexes; competing updates to the same item can fail with `ReplicatedWriteConflictException`. These are product-specific contracts, not generic meanings of asynchronous and synchronous replication.
- **Use when**: the application accepts those consistency and recovery semantics.

### Quorum-based

- **Writes** go to `W` of `N` replicas before acking.
- **Reads** go to `R` of `N` replicas.
- Read/write quorum overlap (`R + W > N`) can make a read intersect a prior write in a simple model with the same `N` replicas and stable membership. It does not alone prove linearizability or protect against every membership change or failure; the protocol, version ordering, recovery, and read/write rules determine the guarantee.
- **Use when**: the datastore documents tunable consistency and the application understands those guarantees.

### Replication topology

```text
All-to-all:       R1 ⇄ R2 ⇄ R3, with additional R1 ⇄ R3 link
Hub-and-spoke:   R2 ⇄ R1 (hub) ⇄ R3
Chain:           R1 → R2 → R3
```

- **All-to-all**: each region can replicate to each other region. More paths to manage as the number of regions grows.
- **Hub-and-spoke**: one region forwards or coordinates replication. Simpler topology, but that role needs its own redundancy and recovery plan.
- **Chain**: each region forwards changes to the next. Later regions depend on every earlier link in the chain.

## 4. Multi-Region Writes: The Hard Part

Concurrent writes need an ordering or conflict rule whether they happen in one region or several. A single authority can serialize accepted changes; independent multi-region writers can accept changes without first observing one another.

### Strategies

**1. Avoid conflicts by sharding**
- **Assign each key or tenant a write owner.** Route writes to that authority; failover must fence the old owner before another region accepts writes.
- **Cost**: cross-region access may add latency, and changing ownership needs a migration or routing plan.

**2. Last-Writer-Wins (LWW)**
- A conflict resolver chooses one version according to a defined ordering, often a timestamp or internal version.
- **Simple, but potentially lossy**: one concurrent update may be discarded. Clock and tie-breaking behavior are implementation-specific.
- **Example**: DynamoDB MREC documentation describes per-item last-writer-wins resolution using an internal timestamp. Do not assume all Global Tables modes behave identically.

**3. Vector clocks**
- Each version carries the causal history. On conflict, you can detect "true concurrent" writes.
- A version vector can help identify causally related versus concurrent updates. The application still needs a policy for resolving concurrent versions.

**4. CRDTs (Conflict-free Replicated Data Types)**
- Data types with merge rules that make replicas converge after receiving the same updates.
- **Examples**: grow-only counters and sets. Rich text collaboration needs more specialized operations and metadata.
- **Cost**: convergence does not automatically preserve every business invariant; the chosen data type and operations constrain what the application can express.

**5. Operational Transform (OT)**
- An alternative approach for collaborative editing transforms concurrent operations according to a defined ordering and transformation protocol. OT and CRDTs are distinct families of collaboration algorithms.

### Decision framework

| Use case | Strategy |
| ------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| User profile, account settings | Choose conflict behavior from product semantics; merge or surface concurrent edits where needed |
| Counters | A suitable CRDT counter or an authoritative counter, depending on accuracy and invariants |
| Collaborative document | A tested OT or CRDT protocol for the document model |
| Money / ledger | Preserve atomic accounting invariants with one authority or a strongly coordinated database protocol |
| Inventory | Use reservations or another atomic ownership/availability rule |
| Cart | Per-key ownership or an explicit merge policy |
| Search index | Rebuildable projection with an ordering/version rule |

Financial data needs explicit atomicity, ordering, and recovery guarantees. A single write authority is one way to preserve them; a database that coordinates writes across regions is another, with latency and availability trade-offs. Do not treat eventual convergence as sufficient for a ledger invariant.

## 5. Data Residency & Sovereignty

Data location and cross-border transfer obligations depend on the jurisdiction, data type, parties, and transfer mechanism. Do not infer that a regulation requires every record to remain inside one country's borders. Confirm applicable requirements with current official guidance and qualified legal advice.

**Patterns:**
- **Regional data partitioning** — keep selected data in approved locations when contracts or applicable rules require it.
- **Access controls and transfer safeguards** — decide where data is stored, processed, backed up, and accessed by operators or subprocessors.
- **Minimization and pseudonymization** — reduce or separate identifying fields where the use case permits; a token is not automatically anonymous or non-reversible.
- **Encryption and key location** — use controls required by the threat model and policy; encryption alone does not settle transfer obligations.

**Architectural impact:**
- Regional location constraints may affect latency and the design of backups, support access, analytics, and telemetry.
- Include logs, analytics, and operational access in the data-flow review; they can contain personal or sensitive information too.

In a design discussion, ask which data must be stored or processed where, who may access it, and how backups and telemetry are handled. Avoid giving legal conclusions from a region name alone.

## 6. Disaster Recovery (RPO / RTO)

Disaster recovery strategies are often described as **backup and restore**, **pilot light**, **warm standby**, and **multi-site active-active**. These names describe increasing levels of readiness, not guaranteed RPO or RTO values. AWS documents these four options for its environment; other platforms and organizations may use different terms.

Set target RPO and RTO from the business requirement, then test the full recovery path. Measure detection, decision/authority transfer, data recovery, dependency readiness, capacity, and routing. Include data corruption and operator-error recovery: a second live region can replicate bad writes just as quickly as good ones.

---

## 7. Cost

Multi-region usually adds cost for replicated compute and storage, cross-region transfer, additional observability, and operational work. Actual pricing varies by provider, region, service, traffic direction, and discounts, so estimate it from current provider pricing and a workload model.

| Resource | What changes with more regions |
| ------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Compute | Replicas need enough capacity for normal traffic and the chosen failover target |
| Data transfer | Replication and cross-region reads may incur transfer charges and latency |
| Database | Replicas, consensus, backups, and operational limits depend on the database design |
| Storage | Each replica or backup adds storage according to retention and replication policy |
| Operations | Monitoring, testing, support, and incident response span more failure domains |

Model normal and failover traffic separately. A standby that cannot handle the required load does not meet its recovery target.

## Code Examples

### Regional failover is a controlled state change

This is a runbook outline, not a generic promotion script. Exact commands depend on the database and routing service:

1. Confirm the incident and the approved failover decision.
2. Fence the old write authority so it cannot accept new writes after promotion.
3. Inspect replica health and lag; compare potential data loss with the documented RPO.
4. Promote using the database's supported procedure and verify the new writer.
5. Route traffic gradually or according to the tested plan, then observe errors and data integrity.
6. Rebuild or rejoin the recovered region without allowing two writers to conflict.

If the old primary cannot be fenced or its state is uncertain, stop and follow the system's split-brain recovery procedure rather than blindly promoting another copy.

### CRDT counter (G-Counter)

```typescript
// Teaching sketch: each actor has one monotonically increasing component.
// In production, actor identity and durable update handling need a protocol.
class GCounter {
  private counts: Map<string, number> = new Map();

  inc(actor: string, by = 1) {
    if (!Number.isSafeInteger(by) || by < 0) throw new RangeError("by must be a non-negative safe integer");
    const next = (this.counts.get(actor) ?? 0) + by;
    if (!Number.isSafeInteger(next)) throw new RangeError("component overflow");
    this.counts.set(actor, next);
  }

  value(): number {
    const total = [...this.counts.values()].reduce((sum, count) => sum + count, 0);
    if (!Number.isSafeInteger(total)) throw new RangeError("total overflow");
    return total;
  }

  merge(other: GCounter): GCounter {
    const merged = new GCounter();
    const keys = new Set([...this.counts.keys(), ...other.counts.keys()]);
    for (const k of keys) {
      merged.counts.set(k, Math.max(this.counts.get(k) ?? 0, other.counts.get(k) ?? 0));
    }
    return merged;
  }
}

// Replicas merge component-wise maxima. Each actor must have a single
// monotonic owner; two writers sharing one actor ID can lose increments.
```

### Trace convergent counting versus independent writers

A records its own component as 3 and B records its own component as 2. Merging produces `{A:3, B:2}`, total 5. Receiving B's same state again changes nothing because merge uses maximum, not addition. If two independent writers both use actor ID A and each advances 3 to 4, merging yields A=4 rather than 5: one increment is lost. Unique durable actor ownership is part of the protocol, not just a map key choice.

### Stable write ownership

Store or derive ownership using a mapping that remains stable when regions are added or removed. A simple modulo over a region list can remap many users during a topology change. If ownership changes, coordinate the migration and fence the previous writer. Route reads to local replicas only when their staleness is acceptable for that operation.

## Common Mistakes

- **Confusing "active-active" with "no conflict".** Independent concurrent changes need a conflict policy. A coordinating datastore may serialize or reject them instead; state which guarantee the system provides.
- **Forgetting data-transfer costs.** Cross-region replication at scale can dwarf compute. Estimate the bandwidth first.
- **Assuming DNS TTL equals failover time.** Resolver caches, client behavior, open connections, health detection, and data promotion all matter.
- **Assuming one residency rule covers all data.** Review storage, processing, backup, operator access, and transfer requirements for the relevant jurisdiction and data.
- **Choosing eventual conflict resolution for a ledger invariant.** Make accounting authority and consistency explicit; eventual convergence alone is not enough.
- **Using a home region without a transfer protocol.** Region failure can leave ownership ambiguous; fencing and recovery must be part of the design.
- **Health checks that pass for a dead region.** Health check the actual data path, not just `/health`. The bug: a service returns 200 but the database is unreachable.
- **No recovery exercise.** A written plan does not show whether the actual data, dependencies, capacity, permissions, and routing can recover.
- **Promoting a stale replica without understanding the loss.** Verify replication state and compare it with the RPO before promotion; follow the database's supported promotion procedure.
- **Skipping the failover-capacity model.** Calculate how much surviving regions must serve when one region is unavailable.
- **Confusing "global" with "everyone sees the same data".** A deployment can serve several regions while replicas are temporarily inconsistent; availability still depends on routing, capacity, dependencies, and failure behavior.

## Best Practices

1. **Choose topology from user-facing latency, recovery, and consistency requirements.**
2. **Name the write authority for each data type.** Include ownership transfer and fencing during failover.
3. **Use CRDTs only when their merge rules preserve the application's needed semantics.**
4. **Preserve financial and inventory invariants with an explicit consistency protocol.**
5. **Exercise regional and data-recovery scenarios.** Use a cadence based on risk and operational change.
6. **Measure actual recovery time and data loss in exercises.** Do not infer them from DNS settings or product labels.
7. **Check health at the level needed to route useful work safely.** Avoid a dependency check that causes unsafe automatic failover.
8. **Estimate replication, read, and failover traffic using current prices.**
9. **Document the tested RPO, RTO, limitations, and operator actions.**

## Performance Considerations

| Concern | Single-region | Active-passive | Active-active (sharded writes) | Active-active (multi-region writes) |
| --------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Write latency | Local acknowledgment contract | Local for asynchronous standby; cross-region wait if synchronous | Home-owner path plus its acknowledgment contract | Local for asynchronous acceptance; cross-region coordination for stronger guarantees |
| Read latency (home region) | Local | Local | Local | Local |
| Read latency (other region) | Remote network path | May require a remote read | Local only if replica freshness is sufficient | Local only if the read consistency contract permits it |
| Availability | Determined by service design, dependencies, and failure coverage | Depends on standby and failover behavior | Depends on traffic and write ownership | Depends on protocol and shared failure modes |
| RPO | Restore point of selected backup | Replication lag or backup point | Depends on replication mode and failure | Depends on consistency protocol and failure |
| RTO | Restore and provision time | Detection, promotion, and routing time | Traffic shift and data-path behavior | May avoid traffic failover, but data recovery can still take time |
| Cost | Baseline | Additional standby, replication, and recovery resources | Additional serving capacity and replication | Additional coordination, data, and operations |

---

## Summary

- **Choose a topology** from latency, consistency, recovery, and cost requirements.
- **Choose a write authority and replication mode** for each data type; define conflict resolution and failover fencing.
- **Treat routing as one part of failover.** DNS, proxies, clients, and open connections have different recovery behavior.
- **RPO and RTO are objectives; observed loss and recovery time are tested outcomes**, not properties guaranteed by an architecture label.
- **Data-location requirements need a specific data-flow review**, including backups and telemetry.
- In a design discussion, state the invariant, recovery target, routing approach, replication behavior, and cost model.

# Technical Debt — Identification, Prioritization, Remediation

## Definition

**Technical debt** is a design or implementation choice whose current form raises the cost or risk of later work. The financial metaphor distinguishes **principal** (work needed to change the design) from **interest** (recurring friction or risk while it remains). The choice can be deliberate or discovered after learning more.

> **Textbook Definition:** Technical debt is future engineering cost associated with a design or implementation decision that makes later modification or operation harder relative to an available alternative.

## First Principles

Engineers choose under incomplete knowledge and limited time. New requirements can reveal that yesterday's reasonable structure makes today's changes expensive. Remediation also consumes finite capacity and can break working behavior, so every imperfect module cannot be rewritten immediately.

A useful debt decision compares future relevant work with the cost/risk of changing the structure. Appearance alone is insufficient. A long function that rarely changes may matter less than a small shared rule repeatedly causing mismatched billing.

## Separate Intent From Prudence

| Quadrant | Example | Management question |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Deliberate/prudent | Keep one storage adapter for launch; record expected replacement trigger | Was the speed benefit worth measured later friction? |
| Deliberate/reckless | Ignore known tenant boundary because release is near | Does immediate risk require remediation before more work? |
| Inadvertent/prudent | New information reveals a better boundary | Which future changes justify adapting it? |
| Inadvertent/reckless | Unexamined coupling repeatedly causes failures | What design/review/training change prevents recurrence? |

This is a reasoning aid, not four precise monetary categories. Bugs, missing security controls and unsupported dependencies can require urgent fixes regardless of whether calling them debt is useful. Debt need not have been consciously chosen; the quadrant includes inadvertent decisions.

## Make One Debt Item Reviewable

Suppose three booking entry points repeat availability checks and have diverged. The debt item records:

- **Evidence:** changes to one rule require three edits; two recent defects show different accepted slots.
- **Affected next work:** rescheduling will add a fourth caller and another chance to diverge.
- **Options:** unify one authoritative check; constrain callers now; or retain duplication with a documented review/test obligation.
- **Principal:** estimated implementation, behavioral tests, data migration if needed and rollout/rollback effort.
- **Interest:** repeated engineer time, support incidents and business risk, with uncertainty stated.
- **Owner and exit condition:** one rule is authoritative and entry-point behavior is verified under concurrent booking, not merely “class has fewer lines.”

Do not equate all repeated syntax with repeated knowledge. Different business rules can start alike and later change independently; forcing them through one generic abstraction can create new coupling.

## Prioritize Against Actual Work

A simple illustrative appraisal: a repair costing 100 hours and saving about three hours per week breaks even in roughly 33 weeks **if** that work continues and the estimate is right. Include rollout risk and maintenance of the new abstraction. A security/data-integrity constraint can override that economic calculation.

Use frequency of change, concrete failure impact, affected roadmap, support burden and dependency risk. A score is a decision aid, not objective measurement: averaging ordinal 1–5 labels and dividing by estimated hours does not supply a universal priority. Record assumptions and revisit them when the roadmap changes.

A change failure rate or lead time can provide context, but team-level metrics do not isolate one module's debt from workload/staffing changes. Compare relevant before/after tasks and incidents rather than attributing every improvement to a refactor.

## Strangler Migration Changes Ownership Gradually

The **strangler fig** approach replaces selected capabilities behind a routing boundary. It reduces the size of each migration, but does not make every slice low risk or independently deployable: shared data, side effects and downstream contracts can couple the paths.

```text
request → routing decision → old capability (authoritative for remaining scope)
                         └→ new capability (authoritative for migrated scope)
```

Choose scope and ownership explicitly. Copy historical data, close the concurrent-change gap, compare behavior, then transfer writes under a fence/cutover protocol. Both implementations must not accept conflicting writes merely because a route percentage changes. Retain a recovery path only while old code/data remain compatible; deleting the old route immediately can remove rollback.

## Branch by Abstraction Separates Callers From Replacement

An adapter/interface can keep callers stable while an implementation changes. It still needs a compatible semantic contract: amount units, error classification, idempotency, timeouts and result meaning can differ between providers. The abstraction does not make those differences disappear.

Resolve asynchronous flag/configuration decisions before selecting an implementation; a Promise used directly in `if` is truthy, regardless of its eventual boolean. Once an operation chooses a provider, retries and reconciliation must continue under its recorded provider identity. Switching an uncertain charge to another provider can charge twice.

Prefer comparing pure reads/calculations with representative data. **Shadowing** means running a non-authoritative path to compare outputs. Running both old and new payment handlers concurrently is not harmless comparison: both can charge or send notifications. Suppress/isolate side effects, normalize meaningful outputs and avoid logging raw sensitive results.

## Reduce Test and Documentation Debt at the Boundary

Characterization tests record current observable behavior before a change, including awkward cases. Then distinguish behavior to preserve from a known defect to fix. Checking that a mock insert was called proves neither correct tenant scope nor protection against concurrent booking.

Prioritize contracts, invariants, failure/recovery paths and integration boundaries. Coverage reports identify unexecuted code; 100% line coverage cannot establish correctness, and a universal 100/80/60 target does not rank risk.

Documentation should carry the actual API/recovery/decision contract: identity, constraints, version boundaries, evidence and owner. An architecture decision record explains alternatives and consequences under its context; generic “PostgreSQL is ACID” text does not explain why it was selected here. Keep it current when assumptions change.

## Keep the Improvement Bounded

1. Identify one recurring cost/risk and the next change it affects.
2. Choose a smallest useful boundary and measurable exit condition.
3. Capture relevant existing behavior and failure cases.
4. Change in reviewable stages with a compatibility/recovery path.
5. Compare actual outcomes and remove obsolete adapters, flags and documentation.

Avoid bundling unrelated rewrites into every feature. Opportunistic cleanup is useful when it lowers the cost of the current change without hiding risk; structural remediation sometimes needs its own scheduled work. A fixed percentage of every sprint is an allocation policy, not a guarantee that debt will decline.

## Summary

Technical debt becomes actionable through concrete future friction or risk, a bounded option and an owner. It can be deliberate or discovered through learning. Prioritize the affected work, preserve observable contracts during replacement and prove improvements at failure/authority boundaries; cleaner-looking code alone is not repayment evidence.

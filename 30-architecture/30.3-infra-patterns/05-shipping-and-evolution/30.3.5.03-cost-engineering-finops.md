# Cost Engineering & FinOps

## Definition

**FinOps** connects technology consumption with financial accountability and business value. Engineers, finance and product owners use allocation, forecasts and measured outcomes to decide which resources and reliability commitments are worth paying for.

> **Textbook Definition:** FinOps is a collaborative operating discipline that connects technology consumption, financial accountability and business value through allocation, forecasting, optimization and shared decision-making.

The FinOps Foundation frames spend in scopes aligned to business ownership. Depending on the organization, a scope can include public or private cloud, SaaS, licenses, data platforms, AI or data centers; the practice is not limited to public-cloud invoices.

## First Principles

Usage-based infrastructure turns compute time, stored bytes, requests and transfers into different billable quantities. Architecture changes those quantities. The same resource can support several products or teams, so its invoice does not automatically identify who benefited.

Reducing a bill can mean efficiency, or it can mean lost demand and degraded service. Useful decisions therefore compare total cost, useful output and service constraints together. Savings also have an implementation cost and may trade away recovery capacity or flexibility.

## Find the Quantity Behind the Charge

```text
workload demand → architecture/resource usage → pricing terms → invoice
                             │                         │
                             └→ owner/allocation + useful business output
                                                       ▼
                                       decision, forecast and measured result
```

Separate compute, storage, requests, transfer/NAT, telemetry, third-party APIs and commitment charges. Tags help allocation but do not cover every charge; shared resources and untaggable service costs need an explicit allocation rule. Review estimated, invoiced and amortized costs under the chosen reporting convention.

There is no universal highest-yield lever order. An I/O-bound database may waste little CPU yet need more storage performance; another system's transfer or retained logs can dominate. Rank measured quantities and risk rather than fixed percentages of an imagined bill.

## Rightsize Useful Capacity, Not One Average

Review representative peak cycles, memory working set, CPU throttling/burst credits, I/O, queues and service-level objectives. A p95 value misses its top 5% by definition and can miss a rare critical workload. Halving limits from one low-memory sample can produce out-of-memory failures.

Distinguish requested/reserved capacity, limits, actual use and billable allocation. A Kubernetes request affects scheduling and some autoscaler utilization ratios; lowering it can change scale behavior without lowering traffic. Verify recovery capacity and one-instance/zone loss behavior before removing headroom.

Scheduled shutdown can save idle environment cost, but depends on boot time, work that must remain scheduled and avoided charges. Clean up resources through owner approval and dependency checks. “Untagged” is an investigation signal, not authorization to delete a database.

## Storage Tiering Is a Total-Cost Decision

| Storage choice | Useful question |
| ------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Frequent-access storage | Does required latency/access justify ongoing capacity? |
| Infrequent-access class | Will retrieval/request/minimum-duration charges outweigh lower storage cost? |
| Instant archive retrieval | Does the provider actually offer immediate reads for this class? |
| Restore-based archive | Do restore delay, temporary restored copies and repeated retrieval fit recovery needs? |

S3 Glacier **Instant** Retrieval and Glacier **Flexible** Retrieval are different classes: the latter requires restoration rather than ordinary immediate access. Exact prices/delays depend on region, size, tier and current terms; compare the documented calculator/price model for the planned workload instead of memorizing fixed storage ratios.

Include transition requests, object sizes, minimum duration/size charging, versioned copies and deletion/hold constraints. A lifecycle rule implements a selected policy, not a universal “30 days hot → archive” prescription. [Data Retention & Archival](30.3.5.04-data-retention-archival.md) owns the policy and expiry boundary.

## Interruptible Capacity Needs Recovery Without the Warning

Spot/preemptible capacity trades price for interruption and uncertain availability. EC2 stop/termination notices normally precede interruption by two minutes; hibernation differs. Do not generalize that to every provider or depend on receiving the warning.

Trace a queued job: commit output and its resume evidence at a bounded boundary, then acknowledge delivery. A crash between output and checkpoint must not duplicate/skip effects. A warning can stop new admission and accelerate cleanup, but recovery must also work after abrupt process loss.

Diversify suitable capacity and keep an explicit fallback/admission policy. Fallback does not guarantee spare on-demand capacity or restore lost throughput. Interactive paths can use interruptible capacity only when redundancy, latency and failure behavior meet their contract; “stateless” alone is insufficient. Checkpointable batch work is often a simpler starting point.

## Autoscaling Can Amplify the Wrong Signal

| Trap | Mechanism | What to verify |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Flapping scale decisions | Noisy signal repeatedly changes desired capacity | Stabilization, readiness/warmup and useful response time |
| Large always-on minimum | Idle reserved capacity remains billable | Required recovery/latency versus traffic-shaped floor |
| Dependency bottleneck | More callers overload the same database/API | End-to-end limits and workload admission |
| Transfer-heavy architecture | Traffic crosses charged network boundaries | Actual paths and current provider terms |
| Per-instance telemetry/sidecars | Scale duplicates base overhead | Resource cost and needed observability/security contract |

Co-locating components can reduce transfer but also reduce fault isolation. VPC endpoints can change NAT paths and costs but have their own charges/support scope. Evaluate the resulting system instead of treating a topology change as a free saving.

## Trace Unit Economics With Consistent Boundaries

An illustrative service has $9,600 allocated monthly cost and 3,000,000 successful orders:

```text
cost per successful order = 9,600 / 3,000,000 = $0.0032
projected new cost/order = $0.0047
relative increase = (0.0047 - 0.0032) / 0.0032 ≈ 46.9%
```

Define what counts as an order and which costs are included. Retry requests are infrastructure work but not extra useful orders; counting them as output can hide waste. Volume, product mix, errors and allocation changes can alter the ratio without a code regression. Track total spend and demand as well.

Revenue per unit minus relevant cost per unit is **contribution/gross profit under the chosen cost boundary**; gross-margin percentage additionally divides by revenue. Third-party and support allocation must be defined before presenting the ratio as product profitability.

## CI Estimates Are Forecasts, Not the Next Invoice

An infrastructure cost diff can estimate supported resource changes under input assumptions. It usually cannot infer actual request counts, traffic distribution, cache hit rates, data transfer, negotiated terms or all runtime services from configuration alone.

Compare the intended candidate against the intended base, supply a reviewed usage scenario and label uncovered charges. A tool posting a pull-request comment does not enforce a spending threshold; a separate policy must evaluate the estimate and fail or request the documented review. Keep credentials and workflow privileges under the normal trusted execution model; detailed pipeline implementation belongs in the [CI/CD stack](../../../40-platform/40.3-ci-cd/INDEX.md).

Use provider budgets/anomaly signals as delayed financial feedback, not guaranteed real-time shutdown controls. Choose when a cost alert warrants paging: service failure and slow budget drift often require different response paths. Measure actual post-release quantities and compare them with the forecast.

## Shared Ownership Makes the Loop Work

- **Showback** reports allocated cost to the responsible teams; **chargeback** applies it to their budgets. Neither is a required step before every optimization.
- Forecast demand and selected pricing commitments with finance; reserved commitments and interruptible capacity are different risk contracts.
- Record proposed savings, implementation cost, service constraints, owner and evidence horizon.
- Verify the result after representative workload/recovery cycles and revise assumptions when demand changes.

## Summary

FinOps makes technology usage, financial terms and useful output part of one decision. Allocate costs under clear rules, optimize measured quantities while preserving service/recovery requirements, and validate forecasts against actual results. Unit cost, total spend and reliability together distinguish efficiency from simply serving less work.

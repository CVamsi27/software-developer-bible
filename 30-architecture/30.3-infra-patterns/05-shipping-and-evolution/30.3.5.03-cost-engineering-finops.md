# Cost Engineering & FinOps

Monthly spend falls from $9,600 to $8,000, but successful orders fall from 3 million to 1.5 million. Total spend is lower while cost per order rises. Did the service become more efficient? The invoice alone cannot answer:

```text
$9,600 → $8,000 spend, but 3M → 1.5M orders: unit cost +66.7%. Efficiency?
```

## Definition

**FinOps** connects technology consumption with financial accountability and business value. Engineers, finance, and product owners use allocation, forecasts, and measured outcomes to decide which resources and reliability commitments are worth paying for.

> **Textbook Definition:** FinOps is an operational framework and cultural practice that maximizes business value from technology, enables timely data-driven decisions and creates financial accountability through collaboration among engineering, finance and business teams.

The FinOps Foundation frames spend in scopes aligned to business ownership. Depending on the organization, a scope can include public or private cloud, SaaS, licenses, data platforms, AI, or data centers; the practice is never limited to public-cloud invoices.

## First Principles

Usage-based infrastructure turns compute time, stored bytes, requests, and transfers into different billable quantities. Architecture changes those quantities. The same resource can support several products or teams, so its invoice never automatically identifies who benefited.

Reducing a bill can mean efficiency, or it can mean lost demand and degraded service. Useful decisions therefore compare total cost, useful output, and service constraints together. Savings also hold an implementation cost and may trade away recovery capacity or flexibility.

## 1. Find the quantity behind the charge

```text
workload demand → architecture/resource usage → pricing terms → invoice
                             │                         │
                             └→ owner/allocation + useful business output
                                                       ▼
                                       decision, forecast and measured result
```

Separate compute, storage, requests, transfer/NAT, telemetry, third-party APIs, and commitment charges. Tags help allocation but cover no charge universally; shared resources and untaggable service costs need an explicit allocation rule. Review estimated, invoiced, and amortized costs under the chosen reporting convention.

There is no universal highest-yield lever order. An I/O-bound database may waste little CPU yet need more storage performance; another system's transfer or retained logs can dominate. Rank measured quantities and risk rather than fixed percentages of an imagined bill.

## 2. Rightsize useful capacity, not one average

Review representative peak cycles, memory working set, CPU throttling/burst credits, I/O, queues, and service-level objectives. A p95 value misses its top 5% by definition and can miss a rare critical workload. Halving limits from one low-memory sample can produce out-of-memory failures.

Distinguish resource requests, limits, observed use, and the provider's billing or allocation basis. In Kubernetes, [requests affect scheduling](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/scheduling-eviction/kube-scheduler/), and the [Horizontal Pod Autoscaler's resource-utilization target](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/workloads/autoscaling/horizontal-pod-autoscale/) is measured relative to requests. Lowering a request can therefore change placement and scaling signals without lowering demand. Verify recovery capacity and one-instance/zone loss behavior before removing headroom.

Scheduled shutdown can save idle environment cost, but depends on boot time, work that must remain scheduled, and avoided charges. Clean up resources through owner approval and dependency checks. "Untagged" is an investigation signal, not authorization to delete a database.

## 3. Tier storage by total cost

| Storage choice | Useful question |
| ------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Frequent-access storage | Does required latency/access justify ongoing capacity? |
| Infrequent-access class | Will retrieval/request/minimum-duration charges outweigh lower storage cost? |
| Instant archive retrieval | Does the provider actually offer immediate reads for this class? |
| Restore-based archive | Do restore delay, temporary restored copies and repeated retrieval fit recovery needs? |

S3 Glacier **Instant** Retrieval and Glacier **Flexible** Retrieval are different classes: the latter requires restoration rather than ordinary immediate access. Exact prices/delays depend on region, size, tier, and current terms; compare the documented calculator/price model for the planned workload instead of memorizing fixed storage ratios.

Include transition requests, object sizes, minimum duration/size charging, versioned copies, and deletion/hold constraints. A lifecycle rule implements a selected policy, not a universal "30 days hot → archive" prescription. [Data Retention & Archival](30.3.5.04-data-retention-archival.md) owns the policy and expiry boundary.

## 4. Recover without the spot warning

Spot/preemptible capacity trades price for interruption and uncertain availability. For EC2, stop/termination notices target a two-minute warning on a best-effort basis; hibernation starts immediately. Other providers hold different interruption contracts, and recovery must never depend on receiving a warning.

Trace a queued job: commit output and its resume evidence at a bounded boundary, then acknowledge delivery. If both writes share a datastore, commit them atomically. Across systems, make replayed effects idempotent or deduplicated and reconcile before acknowledging; a checkpoint alone cannot make remote side effects happen exactly once. A warning can stop new admission and accelerate cleanup, but recovery must also work after abrupt process loss.

Diversify suitable capacity and keep an explicit fallback/admission policy. Fallback never guarantees spare on-demand capacity or restores lost throughput. Interactive paths can use interruptible capacity only when redundancy, latency, and failure behavior meet their contract; "stateless" alone is insufficient. Checkpointable batch work is often a simpler starting point.

## 5. Stop autoscaling from amplifying the wrong signal

Right-sizing chooses resources per instance. Horizontal autoscaling changes replica count; vertical autoscaling adjusts resources per replica. The traps below focus on horizontal scaling, where a noisy or indirect signal can multiply idle cost or overload a shared dependency. [Kubernetes supports both approaches](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/workloads/autoscaling/).

| Trap | Mechanism | What to verify |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Flapping scale decisions | Noisy signal repeatedly changes desired capacity | Stabilization, readiness/warmup and useful response time |
| Large always-on minimum | Idle reserved capacity remains billable | Required recovery/latency versus traffic-shaped floor |
| Dependency bottleneck | More callers overload the same database/API | End-to-end limits and workload admission |
| Transfer-heavy architecture | Traffic crosses charged network boundaries | Actual paths and current provider terms |
| Per-instance telemetry/sidecars | Scale duplicates base overhead | Resource cost and needed observability/security contract |

Co-locating components can reduce transfer but also reduce fault isolation. For AWS, gateway VPC endpoints for S3 and DynamoDB carry no hourly or data-transfer charge, while interface endpoints carry hourly and data-processing charges. Endpoint fit also depends on service coverage and routing requirements; see [AWS EKS networking cost guidance](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eks/latest/best-practices/cost-opt-networking.html). Evaluate the resulting system instead of treating a topology change as a free saving.

## 6. Trace unit economics and forecast honestly

An illustrative service holds $9,600 allocated monthly cost and 3,000,000 successful orders:

```text
baseline cost per successful order = 9,600 / 3,000,000 = $0.003200
new cost per successful order = 8,000 / 1,500,000 ≈ $0.005333
unit-cost increase = (0.005333 - 0.003200) / 0.003200 ≈ 66.7%
```

In this example, monthly spend fell about 16.7%, but unit cost rose about 66.7%. Define what counts as an order and which costs are included. Retry requests are infrastructure work but not extra useful orders; counting them as output can hide waste. Product mix, errors, and allocation changes can alter the ratio without a code regression. Compare like periods and track total spend, demand, and reliability together.

Revenue per unit minus costs included under a defined accounting boundary gives a profit measure. Call it **contribution** only when the included costs match the organization's contribution-margin definition; call it **gross profit** when they follow its cost-of-goods-sold definition. Divide by revenue to calculate the corresponding margin percentage. Define third-party and support allocation before presenting the result as product profitability.

An infrastructure cost diff can estimate supported resource changes under input assumptions. It usually cannot infer actual request counts, traffic distribution, cache hit rates, data transfer, negotiated terms, or all runtime services from configuration alone. Compare the intended candidate against the intended base, supply a reviewed usage scenario, and label uncovered charges. A tool posting a pull-request comment enforces no spending threshold; a separate policy must evaluate the estimate and fail or request the documented review. Keep credentials and workflow privileges under the normal trusted execution model; detailed pipeline implementation belongs in the [CI/CD stack](../../../40-platform/40.3-ci-cd/INDEX.md). Use provider budgets/anomaly signals as delayed financial feedback, not guaranteed real-time shutdown controls. Choose when a cost alert warrants paging: service failure and slow budget drift often require different response paths. Measure actual post-release quantities and compare them with the forecast.

Close the loop with shared ownership: **showback** reports allocated cost to the responsible teams; **chargeback** applies it to their budgets — neither is a required step before every optimization. Forecast demand and selected pricing commitments with finance; reserved commitments and interruptible capacity are different risk contracts. Record proposed savings, implementation cost, service constraints, owner, and evidence horizon. Verify the result after representative workload/recovery cycles and revise assumptions when demand changes.

## Summary

FinOps makes technology usage, financial terms and useful output part of one decision. Allocate costs under clear rules, optimize measured quantities while preserving service/recovery requirements, and validate forecasts against actual results. Unit cost, total spend and reliability together distinguish efficiency from simply serving less work.

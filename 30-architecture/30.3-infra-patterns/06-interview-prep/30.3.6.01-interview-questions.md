# 30.3.6.01 — Infra & Ops Patterns Interview Questions

> Senior-depth operations questions drawn from this stack's learning chapters — health/lifecycle contracts, retry and idempotency discipline, backups proven by drills, coordination, scaling levers, and shipping without downtime. Each answer names the mechanism and the failure mode it guards.

## First Principles

This set probes the operations contracts (→ 30.3.1–30.3.5), not tool trivia:

1. **Ops is contracts between replaceable parties** — your app and the orchestrator, a request and its retry, the current and next deployment. The senior tells are statements about *who relies on what*, not about specific products.
2. **Mechanisms here preserve retry safety, support safe replacement, or bound failure propagation** — derive the answer from which of the three it buys and the follow-ups answer themselves.

## Answer Framework

```text
ANSWER STRUCTURE:
  1. The contract (who promises what to whom)
  2. The mechanism (how the pattern enforces it)
  3. The failure mode it guards (the incident the interviewer is fishing for)
  4. The operational answer (gates, drills, budgets, runbooks)
  5. The trade-off (when the pattern isn't worth it)
```

## Beginner

**Q1: What's the difference between liveness and readiness probes?**

**Answer:** Liveness asks whether restarting this instance is likely to repair a local failure. Readiness asks whether it should receive the relevant serving traffic; failure changes routing without necessarily restarting. Their predicates can overlap, but a shared dependency outage must not blindly trigger a fleet restart. Controller timing and routing propagation mean a failed readiness result is not instantaneous removal everywhere.

**Q2: What does a graceful shutdown actually have to do?**

**Answer:** Coordinate routing and local admission, stop starting new work, complete or safely hand off bounded in-flight work, then close dependencies and exit under the grace policy. Routing can lag: closing a listener alone does not prove every upstream stopped sending. Keep dependencies open until ordinary responses finish, handle upgraded sockets/workers separately, and account for preStop time inside the same platform grace budget. Forced termination does not undo a committed effect.

**Q3: What is an idempotency key, and why do retries need one?**

**Answer:** A stable operation key lets the server recognize a retry of the same logical action. The service must atomically bind that key and request fingerprint to the effect and retained result; reusing a key with a different payload must not silently replay the old outcome. This can prevent duplicate effects within the retention window, but does not make the network deliver exactly once. External payments also need provider idempotency or reconciliation.

**Q4: What do RPO and RTO mean?**

**Answer:** RPO: how much data you can afford to lose (time between the last recovery point and the failure). RTO: how long you can afford to be down. Both are business tolerances first — the backup schedule and restore architecture are derived from them, not the reverse. A backup strategy that can't state its RPO/RTO is a hope, not a strategy.

**Q5: Blue-green vs canary — when does each fit?**

**Answer:** Blue-green retains a prepared alternate fleet and changes routing; canary limits selected exposure while evaluating outcomes. They can combine. Neither switch is globally atomic, rollback is not automatically instant, and a small candidate can corrupt shared data. Choose retained capacity, representative evidence, mixed-version/data compatibility and recovery requirements; duplicate fleet cost need not mean twice every infrastructure resource.

## Intermediate

**Q6: What's the correct relationship between retry, timeout, and backoff?**

**Answer:** Per-attempt bounds limit a call; a shared overall deadline also covers attempts and sleeps. Backoff changes spacing, jitter spreads callers, and an additional-attempt budget caps amplification. Classify the endpoint and outcome, and keep mutation identity after uncertainty. A client may retry HTTP 429 according to the endpoint contract and `Retry-After`; a 4xx status is not automatically retryable, and a 5xx response is not automatically safe to repeat. Independent retries in the application, SDK and proxy can multiply work.

**Q7: Feature flags are operational infrastructure — what's the ops contract?**

**Answer:** Define owner, purpose, decision polarity, trusted subject/context, propagation objective and uninitialized/stale behavior. Remove temporary release branches when compatibility/rollback windows close; maintain permanent operational controls with drills. A flip controls future admissions or defined checkpoints, not accepted remote effects. Production flipping is not the only valid test, and a control-plane acknowledgment is not fleet convergence.

**Q8: Why do leader-election systems need fencing tokens?**

**Answer:** A paused old holder can resume after replacement. A durable increasing epoch lets the protected resource reject older epochs once newer authority reaches it. That fence alone does not prove a lease remains current; strict current-authority writes need an atomic authority check/mutation boundary. Lease clocks do not stop the old process, and external receivers need their own identity/fencing/reconciliation contract.

**Q9: You need to scale a relational database. What's the lever order?**

**Answer:** There is no mandatory lever order. Identify CPU/I/O, lock, query, connection, read-consistency, write or storage pressure, then choose the relevant change and measure it. Index/query work can reduce load; pools bound useful concurrency; replicas serve eligible reads with lag; partitioning organizes/prunes within one server; sharding changes routing/locality/transactions. Partitioning is not automatically write scale, and exhausting every unrelated lever is not a prerequisite for a measured distribution need.

**Q10: What does a backup drill have that a backup doesn't?**

**Answer:** A timed drill exercises actual artifacts, keys, tools, recovered target-state checks, application behavior and cutover/fencing. It distinguishes byte integrity from meaningful recoverability and records the selected failure scenario and boundaries. RPO/RTO are targets; achieved recovery point/time are evidence. One successful drill does not prove larger datasets, missing history or failed identity infrastructure.

**Q11: Zero-downtime deploys of schema changes — how?**

**Answer:** Expand compatible schema, bridge/upgrade every writer, backfill with a concurrency/progress policy, validate meaning and only then make new reads authoritative. Keep old fields/readers through the declared support/rollback window before contraction. Reverting code is safe only while its schema/data/event/asset contracts remain compatible. Readiness does not detect every business regression; canary/semantic evidence is still needed.

## Advanced

**Q12: A deploy coincides with a latency spike. How do you attribute it?**

**Answer:** Decompose by deploy mechanics first — new-version startup storms (connection floods at boot), cache cold-start on the new fleet, health-gate flapping causing rotation churn. Then check the version-skew window: old and new versions serving simultaneously means shared state (migrations, caches, queue payloads) must be compatible *both ways* — a violation shows as errors only during rollout. The senior signal is treating the deploy window as a distinct failure class with its own checklist, not lumping it into generic latency triage.

**Q13: Design the pipeline for right-to-erasure (GDPR deletion).**

**Answer:** Map applicable data classes and copies, verify the request, and obtain a scoped policy decision including deadlines and lawful exceptions. Schedule idempotent per-store work and track actual verification, versions, holds and failed actions. Pseudonymization is not automatic erasure. Backups need approved beyond-use/retention and restore re-erasure handling; cryptographic erasure requires every key/plaintext path to be covered. Hold/deletion precedence and scope are policy decisions, not a universal override slogan.

**Q14: Your team's on-call load keeps rising with the service count. What levers do you pull, in order?**

**Answer:** Measure page sources, repeated manual work and actual customer impact before choosing a sequence. Make alerts actionable and scoped, automate well-understood recovery with bounded permissions, and address the source failure. Consolidation or isolation can help under different coupling/capacity models; neither is universally last or always beneficial. Track changes in incidents/toil rather than equating all pages with distinct bugs.

**Q15: What's the failure mode of "we rollback on error-rate"?**

**Answer:** Rollback is a state machine, and naive triggers misfire in both directions: flapping (rollback → healthy → redeploy → rollback loops) needs hysteresis and a cool-down; and rollback can be *unsafe* — if the new version ran a forward-only migration, the previous version can't serve. The contract: rollback plans are validated against migration compatibility (expand/contract keeps both versions servable), and automated rollbacks have a bounded attempt count with a human escalation path.

## Behavioral & Scenario Drills

**Q16 (scenario): Friday 16:00 — monitoring shows the standby database is 4 hours behind. What do you do?**

**Answer:** Find which recovery paths rely on this standby and whether reads are serving stale state. Four hours of replica lag is not automatically the system RPO if another protected recovery path has a newer complete point; it can already violate a user-read contract. Investigate replay conflicts, network/storage, workload and retained WAL/slots. Report measured exposure against the affected objectives, avoid unsafe promotion, and prioritize corrective capacity/traffic decisions under the failure model.

**Q17 (behavioral): Tell me about a time you traded technical debt against feature pressure.**

**Practice scenario (situation → task → action → result; replace the example with experience you can substantiate):** A flag-gated legacy path stayed active after a migration because launch work took priority. The engineer recorded its owner, measured operational or delivery cost, target removal date and compatibility condition, then negotiated a bounded follow-up. Report the actual outcome only if it happened. The interview point is the tracking and trade-off process, not a borrowed success story.

**Q18 (follow-up trap): "We do canary deploys — do we still need blue-green?"**

**Answer:** Canary controls exposure/evidence; blue-green retains another serving version and supports a routing transition. They are orthogonal and can combine, but neither is mandatory alongside the other. Blue-green does not make a shared schema migration reversible or fix incompatible writes. Choose an escape/recovery path from the specific state, traffic, capacity and convergence requirements rather than asserting what most mature platforms do.

## Degradation & Capacity

**Q19: Design a circuit breaker — and name the contract its fallback must honor.**

**Answer:** Closed admits and measures selected outcomes; open rejects; half-open admits bounded recovery probes. Define sample/window/threshold, per-attempt versus operation accounting, late-result generation handling and probe concurrency. Fallback must meet the caller's declared freshness/authorization/correctness contract, or return an honest error. Observe state/rejections/recovery; alerts depend on customer impact rather than paging for every opening.

**Q20: What can readiness probes never see, and what covers the gap?**

**Answer:** A readiness predicate sees only what it explicitly checks at that time. Even a deep check cannot establish the complete user journey or future behavior. Combine local readiness with customer SLIs, version/business checks and bounded admission/degradation under the service contract. Blind traffic removal or error-based routing during a shared outage can itself reduce useful capacity.

**Q21: Design load shedding. What do you drop first, and what does the client get?**

**Answer:** Define authenticated priority classes and admission limits before expensive allocation. Preserve bounded control-plane/probe capacity, and reject deferrable work first under the agreed business policy. Use429 for appropriate client throttling and503 for temporary service overload, with a supported retry delay when useful; clients still need deadlines and budgets. Never trust an arbitrary priority header, and cap active/queued/waiting work rather than only making timeout responses faster.

**Q22: Connection pools are shared fates — size them like it.**

**Answer:** Every instance's pool multiplies: 50 pods × 20 connections = 1000 against a database tuned for hundreds — pool sizing is a *fleet* calculation, not a per-pod one. The failure mode: one slow dependency holds connections open, the pool exhausts, and every caller queues behind it — the pool converts one slow dependency into total unavailability. Discipline: pool caps derived from fleet math and DB connection limits; borrow-timeout fast (fail that request, keep the rest alive); and pool-wait metrics alerted on, because queue-time growth is the leading indicator before the outage. Same logic for thread pools and worker counts — anything shared is a blast-radius boundary to design, not a config default.

**Q23 (scenario): Design checkout to survive a recommendation-service outage.**

**Answer:** Keep order/inventory/payment authority independent of recommendations. Give optional calls bounded capacity/time and a declared fallback such as no suggestions; derive timing from the request budget rather than universal tens-of-ms. Avoid waiting for optional remote work inside the database transaction. Verify that loss of recommendations does not change required pricing, authorization or persisted order semantics.

**Q24 (behavioral): Tell me about a time graceful degradation saved an incident from being an outage.**

**Practice scenario:** Practice a recommendation provider that stalls while checkout retains its required order/payment path. Explain predesigned bounded admission/deadline, honest empty-suggestion fallback and observable degraded state. Replace the scenario with measured facts from your own experience before presenting it as an incident result. Tax computation must not be assumed optional or safely replaceable by a flat estimate; its legal/business contract can make it critical.

**Q25 (follow-up trap): "We added retries to every call — isn't that resilience?"**

**Answer:** Untargeted retries amplify load precisely when a dependency may have less spare capacity. If each request makes one retry, attempts can approach twice the original request volume; retries at several layers can multiply that further. Retry only outcomes the operation can safely repeat, cap attempts within a shared deadline and retry budget, honor endpoint-specific throttling guidance, and coordinate retries with circuit breakers and admission limits. Validation failures usually stop; a server error may still leave a mutation's outcome uncertain, so preserve its operation identity or reconcile before repeating it.

## Summary

1. **Probe semantics serve different controllers** — liveness repairs, readiness routes; conflating them turns outages into restart storms
2. **Retry without idempotency is duplication** — keys, budgets, and retry-ability classification are the discipline
3. **Backups are proven by drills** — targets and measured achieved recovery must remain distinct
4. **Scaling follows the measured constraint** — identify the mechanism, new consistency boundary and recovery cost
5. **Shipping safely is version-skew management** — expand/contract plus health gates, with rollback plans that respect migrations

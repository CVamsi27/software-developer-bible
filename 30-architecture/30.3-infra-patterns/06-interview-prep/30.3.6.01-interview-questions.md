# 30.3.6.01 — Infra & Ops Patterns Interview Questions

> Senior-depth operations questions drawn from this stack's 14 concept chapters — health/lifecycle contracts, retry and idempotency discipline, backups proven by drills, coordination, scaling levers, and shipping without downtime. Each answer names the mechanism and the failure mode it guards.

## TL;DR

Senior-level infra/ops questions with model answers — readiness vs liveness, why retries need idempotency keys, RPO/RTO as business numbers, fencing tokens, the scaling-levers order, blue-green vs canary, and the flag debt problem. The senior signal is *contract thinking*: every answer names the two parties of the contract (app ↔ orchestrator, producer ↔ consumer, current ↔ next version) and what breaks when one side violates it. Every question is derivable from the stack's chapters (→ 30.3.1–30.3.5).

## First Principles

This set probes the operations contracts (→ 30.3.1–30.3.5), not tool trivia:

1. **Ops is contracts between replaceable parties** — your app and the orchestrator, a request and its retry, the current and next deployment. The senior tells are statements about *who relies on what*, not about specific products.
2. **Every mechanism here buys exactly-once semantics, safe replacement, or bounded blast radius** — derive the answer from which of the three it buys and the follow-ups answer themselves.

## Answer Framework

```text
ANSWER STRUCTURE:
  1. The contract (who promises what to whom)
  2. The mechanism (how the pattern enforces it)

  4. The operational answer (gates, drills, budgets, runbooks)
```

## Beginner

**Q1: What's the difference between liveness and readiness probes?**

**Short answer:** Liveness asks "is the process wedged?" — failing it restarts the container. Readiness asks "can this instance serve traffic right now?" — failing it removes the pod from Service rotation without restarting. They answer different questions and must not be the same endpoint: a ready-but-degraded dependency shouldn't trigger a restart loop, and a started-but-warming app shouldn't receive traffic.

**Go deeper (senior):** "The contract is with the orchestrator: liveness is the *self-repair* knob, readiness is the *traffic* knob. Pointing both at the same deep health check couples restarts to dependency outages — a restart storm on top of an outage."

**Q2: What does a graceful shutdown actually have to do?**

**Answer:** On SIGTERM: stop accepting new work (close the listener, deregister or fail readiness), finish or hand off in-flight work, flush buffers, then exit within the orchestrator's grace period. The orchestration contract pairs it with readiness: the pod should leave rotation *before* it starts refusing connections.

**Q3: What is an idempotency key, and why do retries need one?**

**Answer:** A client-supplied unique key that lets the server recognize a repeated operation and return the original result instead of executing it again. Retries exist because networks and services fail (at-least-once delivery); idempotency keys convert that into exactly-once *effects* — without one, a retried payment is a double charge.

**Q4: What do RPO and RTO mean?**

**Answer:** RPO: how much data you can afford to lose (time between the last recovery point and the failure). RTO: how long you can afford to be down. Both are business tolerances first — the backup schedule and restore architecture are derived from them, not the reverse. A backup strategy that can't state its RPO/RTO is a hope, not a strategy.

**Q5: Blue-green vs canary — when does each fit?**

**Answer:** Blue-green swaps between two full environments: instant cutover, instant rollback, but 2× capacity during the window and the whole fleet changes at once. Canary shifts a growing percentage of traffic to the new version: blast radius is proportional, feedback is real-user-based, but rollback takes an automated gate to be fast. Fit: blue-green when you need atomic all-or-nothing and can afford the duplicate fleet; canary as the default for stateless services with good metrics.

## Intermediate

**Q6: What's the correct relationship between retry, timeout, and backoff?**

**Answer:** Timeouts bound each attempt; backoff spaces retries; jitter decorrelates them so a recovered dependency isn't hit by a thundering herd of synchronized clients. The senior additions: retry *budgets* (cap the fraction of traffic that can be retries, or a struggling dependency gets 2× load) and retry-ability classification — only retry what's safe to repeat, and only when the failure is transient. Retrying on 4xx or on non-idempotent operations amplifies incidents.

**Q7: Feature flags are operational infrastructure — what's the ops contract?**

**Answer:** Flags decouple *deploy* from *release* and provide a kill path faster than rollback. The contract has a price: every live flag is permanent branching in code paths, so flags need owners, expiry dates, and a cleanup pipeline — "flag debt" is technical debt with its own SLA. Kill switches for critical paths get a tested kill path (a flag you've never flipped in production is untested infrastructure).

**Q8: Why do leader-election systems need fencing tokens?**

**Answer:** Because leadership messages arrive late: an old leader that's been partitioned (or GC-paused) doesn't know it lost, and will keep writing. A fencing token — a monotonically increasing number issued with the lease — lets the downstream store reject writes from any holder whose token is stale. Lease time alone doesn't fix this; clocks and GC pauses see to it. The practical answer for cron: coordination via a lock store plus idempotent job design, not "hope the intervals don't overlap."

**Q9: You need to scale a relational database. What's the lever order?**

**Answer:** In order of increasing cost and risk: (1) indexes and query work — the cheapest capacity is the query you stop running; (2) caching reads; (3) vertical scaling — real, and buys time; (4) read replicas — read scale with replication lag as the new failure mode; (5) partitioning/sharding — write scale, at the cost of cross-shard transactions and operational complexity. The senior tell is naming lag and cross-shard semantics as the price, and refusing to shard until the earlier levers are exhausted.

**Q10: What does a backup drill have that a backup doesn't?**

**Answer:** Evidence. An untested backup is an assumption about RPO *and* about restorability — corrupt snapshots, expired credentials, and undocumented steps only surface during a restore. The drill rehearses to a target: restore into a clean environment, validate data integrity, and measure achieved RTO against the stated one. The 3-2-1(-1-0) rule's last digit — zero restore errors verified by test — is the whole point.

**Q11: Zero-downtime deploys of schema changes — how?**

**Answer:** Expand/contract: add the new column/table (expand), deploy code that writes both and reads the new, backfill, then remove the old path (contract) — each step leaves the system fully working and rollbacks are code-reversions, not data reversions. The forbidden move is a destructive migration in the same release as the code that needs it. Pair with health-gated rollouts so a bad deploy fails its readiness gate instead of the canary metrics.

## Advanced

**Q12: A deploy coincides with a latency spike. How do you attribute it?**

**Answer:** Decompose by deploy mechanics first — new-version startup storms (connection floods at boot), cache cold-start on the new fleet, health-gate flapping causing rotation churn. Then check the version-skew window: old and new versions serving simultaneously means shared state (migrations, caches, queue payloads) must be compatible *both ways* — a violation shows as errors only during rollout. The senior signal is treating the deploy window as a distinct failure class with its own checklist, not lumping it into generic latency triage.

**Q13: Design the pipeline for right-to-erasure (GDPR deletion).**

**Answer:** It's a data-lineage problem: identity is the key, and deletion must reach every store keyed by it — primary DB, replicas (via binlog propagation, so lag bounds completion), caches (TTL bounds exposure), search indexes, object storage, warehouse (usually via delete vectors or scheduled rebuilds), and backups (historical artifacts — typically handled by retention expiry + documented policy rather than rewriting history). The deliverables: a lineage map, per-store deletion mechanics with time bounds, and an auditable completion record. Legal hold overrides erasure — the retention tier system has to encode that precedence.

**Q14: Your team's on-call load keeps rising with the service count. What levers do you pull, in order?**

**Answer:** Alert hygiene first — every page must be actionable (SLO burn alerts over per-host noise), and alert volume is a bug count. Then removal of toil: automate the runbooks that page repeatedly (restart loops, disk cleanup, certificate rotation). Then topology: blast-radius isolation (bulkheads, per-tenant cells) so incidents are small and don't wake everyone. Structural last: consolidation — fewer, boring services beat many clever ones; the debt chapter's quadrant applies to architecture too.

**Q15: What's the failure mode of "we rollback on error-rate"?**

**Answer:** Rollback is a state machine, and naive triggers misfire in both directions: flapping (rollback → healthy → redeploy → rollback loops) needs hysteresis and a cool-down; and rollback can be *unsafe* — if the new version ran a forward-only migration, the previous version can't serve. The contract: rollback plans are validated against migration compatibility (expand/contract keeps both versions servable), and automated rollbacks have a bounded attempt count with a human escalation path.

## Behavioral & Scenario Drills

**Q16 (scenario): Friday 16:00 — monitoring shows the standby database is 4 hours behind. What do you do?**

**Answer:** First classify: replication lag is a *backup/HA* problem right now, not an outage — RPO exposure has grown to 4 hours, which is the number to report. Check the obvious causes (long-running query blocking WAL replay, network saturation, replica undersized for write volume). Then decide whether the day's write volume has made the lag worse and whether any operation must be deferred (heavy batch jobs are classic lag amplifiers). The interview point: you *report* RPO exposure immediately, you don't wait for it to become an outage, and afterwards the fix is lag *monitoring against an SLO*, not a one-time repair.

**Q17 (behavioral): Tell me about a time you traded technical debt against feature pressure.**

**Story to tell (situation → task → action → result):** Deliberate debt: a flag-gated legacy path kept alive through a migration window past its planned removal date because launch pressure won. Made the debt explicit — owner, dollar-of-latency cost, expiry date on the board — and negotiated a debt-sprint when the expiry lapsed. Result: the path died, and the team adopted "flags and debt expire by default" as policy. The interview point is the *tracking mechanism*, not the heroics: debt that isn't written down with an expiry is debt that never gets paid.

**Q18 (follow-up trap): "We do canary deploys — do we still need blue-green?"**

**Answer (the nuanced answer they want):** They solve different failure classes: canary limits *traffic* exposure to a bad version; blue-green bounds *rollback time* for a bad deploy that already went everywhere (and for migrations, where traffic-splitting doesn't apply). Most mature platforms run canary as the default and keep a blue-green-shaped escape hatch for stateful components and schema migrations. "One replaces the other" is the trap; "they bound different risks" is the answer.

## Summary

1. **Probes are two contracts, not one** — liveness repairs, readiness routes; conflating them turns outages into restart storms
2. **Retry without idempotency is duplication** — keys, budgets, and retry-ability classification are the discipline
3. **Backups are proven by drills** — RPO/RTO are stated numbers, achieved numbers, or lies
4. **Scaling has a lever order** — and the senior answer names the failure mode each lever introduces
5. **Shipping safely is version-skew management** — expand/contract plus health gates, with rollback plans that respect migrations

## Cheat Sheet

```text
LIFECYCLE:  liveness = restart knob · readiness = traffic knob · SIGTERM: stop→drain→exit
RETRY:      timeout per attempt · backoff+jitter · retry budget · idempotency keys
FLAGS:      decouple deploy/release · kill switch needs a tested kill path · flags expire
BACKUPS:    3-2-1(-1-0) · RPO/RTO stated & drilled · restore game days
COORDINATION: leases expire · fencing tokens reject stale leaders · idempotent cron
DB SCALE:   indexes → cache → vertical → replicas (lag!) → shard (cross-shard cost)
DEPLOYS:    blue-green = atomic + fast rollback · canary = proportional blast radius
            expand/contract migrations keep rollback servable
FINOPS:     rightsizing → tiering → spot → unit economics → cost checks in CI
DRILLS:     every number on the dashboard was proven in a game day, or it's fiction
```

# 40.11.02 — Nginx Reverse Proxy Interview Questions

> Reverse-proxy practice for process ownership, configuration, graceful reloads, buffering, health checks, rate limiting and implementation choices. Explain the mechanism and test its changed conditions; the prompts do not predict a particular interview.

## First Principles

This set probes event-loop economics and edge responsibilities (→ 40.11.01):

1. **The senior tells are mechanism statements**: "old workers drain in-flight while new workers serve" beats "reload is zero-downtime" — juniors memorize directives; seniors narrate what the process actually does.
2. **Assign each boundary an owner** — worker time, sockets and buffers are finite. TLS, limits and headers may have different owners across the edge and application; routing through a proxy does not remove downstream authorization or resource budgets.

## Answer Framework

```text
ANSWER STRUCTURE:
  1. The model (event loop per worker, master manages lifecycle)
  2. The mechanism (what nginx actually does on this path)
  3. The failure mode you're guarding (blocked loop, lost conns, 502s, hot upstream)
  4. The operational answer (nginx -t, reload, logs, $request_time vs upstream time)
  5. The trade-off (when nginx is the wrong tool → HAProxy/Envoy/ALB)
```

## Beginner

**Q1: What's the difference between a forward proxy and a reverse proxy?**

**Answer:** A forward proxy acts for a client reaching other origins; a reverse proxy acts for an origin reaching its backends. Protection or anonymity depends on authentication, headers and network policy. TLS can terminate on either kind of explicitly trusted intermediary; the role alone does not make interception appropriate.

**Q2: Why did nginx beat thread-per-connection servers at scale?**

**Answer:** An event-driven worker handles ready I/O rather than dedicating one waiting thread to each connection. Each connection still needs descriptors, state, buffers and possibly upstream sockets. Measure memory, CPU and workload limits; fixed KB/MB comparisons or a concurrency count alone do not establish throughput.

**Q3: What do the master and worker processes each do?**

**Answer:** The master initializes configuration/listeners, manages workers and handles lifecycle signals. Workers accept and process requests under configured privileges. auto uses detected CPUs as a starting point; CPU/resource limits, affinity and workload measurements determine whether that choice fits.

**Q4: What is TLS termination, and what do you gain and lose at the proxy?**

**Answer:** Terminate HTTPS at the proxy, speak plain HTTP (or mTLS) internally. Gain: certificates renew in one place, handshake CPU off app servers, HTTP/2 to clients while upstreams stay HTTP/1.1. Lose: plaintext on the internal segment — re-encrypt in zero-trust estates, or use SSL passthrough when end-to-end TLS is mandatory (and lose L7 visibility).

**Q5: How does nginx serve a static file differently from a dynamic request?**

**Answer:** A static request maps to a file-output path; sendfile can reduce copying where supported, while cold I/O, TLS and filesystem behavior still cost work. A proxy request selects an upstream and forwards/relays HTTP under buffering and timeout policy. Neither path is universally CPU-free.

## Intermediate

**Q6: Explain nginx's config context hierarchy and how inheritance works.**

**Answer:** `events` and `http` are siblings in main; `server` and `location` nest inside http. Inheritance depends on the directive — so `gzip on` at `http` level applies to every server unless a server turns it off. The practical test: a directive belongs at the highest level where it's uniformly true; per-path overrides belong in `location` blocks.

**Q7: What actually happens on `nginx -s reload`, and why run `nginx -t` first?**

**Answer:** Reload sends SIGHUP to the master: it re-reads and validates config — if validation fails the old config stays — then spawns new workers with the new config and tells old workers to finish in-flight connections and exit, while listen sockets stay shared the whole time. The goal is graceful replacement, not a guarantee of zero failures under every traffic/resource condition. `nginx -t` first to catch malformed configuration before the reload attempt, and because `-t` catches typos but *not* semantic errors — a valid config can still point at the wrong upstream port.

**Q8: A mobile client on a bad network downloads a 10 MB export. What does nginx do for you?**

**Answer:** `proxy_buffering on` (the default) is slow-client armor: nginx reads the upstream response into configured memory/disk buffers as quickly as capacity allows, freeing the upstream connection, then dribbles bytes to the slow client at its own pace. Without it, the client's 50 KB/s pins the upstream connection for minutes and exhausts the pool. Disable buffering when timely streaming requires it; long-polling does not automatically require that policy.

**Q9: How do health checks work in open-source nginx vs HAProxy/Envoy?**

**Answer:** NGINX open-source upstreams track specified unsuccessful attempts under max_fails/fail_timeout, with single-server and retry-policy conditions. NGINX Plus provides active checks; other proxies have their own supported active-check features. A probe can also race with failure, so it is not a guarantee that the next request succeeds.

**Q10: How do you rate-limit correctly with `limit_req`?**

**Answer:** The zone holds per-key leaky-bucket accounting; choose a trusted key. burst sets excess capacity. nodelay admits that excess immediately while its accounting drains at the configured rate; it does not hold those requests in a delay queue. Choose 429 or another explicit rejection contract and measure actual bursts/limits; local zones are not a coordinated fleet quota.

## Advanced

**Q11: Why is one blocking call in a worker catastrophic, and what are the escapes?**

**Answer:** A blocking call delays that worker's scheduled work; it does not automatically freeze every other worker. Supported aio threads offloads relevant file operations to NGINX-managed threads. sendfile can reduce copies but cold file access may still block. Keep heavy application work outside the proxy and inspect module behavior.

**Q12: WebSocket proxying — what breaks and what do you set?**

**Answer:** Pass the hop-by-hop `Upgrade` and `Connection: upgrade` headers explicitly; NGINX does not forward them by default. On versions before 1.29.7, set `proxy_http_version 1.1`; 1.29.7 and later use it by default. `proxy_read_timeout` is the idle interval between upstream reads, so set it above the expected heartbeat gap or keep the default heartbeat interval below it. During reload, `worker_shutdown_timeout` bounds how long old workers drain; clients need a reconnect policy.

**Q13: What changed in upstream keepalive in NGINX 1.29.7, and how do you support older versions?**

**Answer:** Since 1.29.7, NGINX defaults proxied HTTP to HTTP/1.1 and keeps an idle upstream connection cache (32 connections per worker). Before that version, deployments commonly needed HTTP/1.1, an empty `Connection` header, and an upstream `keepalive` setting. Explicit `keepalive N` sizes the idle cache; it does not cap active connections. Verify the deployed version and backend behavior before changing it. See the [NGINX 1.29.7 announcement](https://blog.nginx.org/blog/keep-alive-to-upstreams-is-now-default-in-nginx-1-29-7).

**Q14: Why is `ip_hash` session affinity usually the wrong answer?**

**Answer:** It hashes the client IP to pin a user to one server — but behind corporate NAT an entire office is one "client", so the hash hot-spots one server and scale-out breaks. The senior answer: sessions belong in a shared store (Redis), the app stays stateless (→ factor VI of twelve-factor), and the LB picks servers on load, not identity.

**Q15: nginx vs HAProxy vs Envoy vs a cloud ALB — how do you choose?**

**Answer:** Compare actual requirements: static/caching support, active checks, protocol handling, dynamic discovery/control plane, observability and operating ownership. Product capabilities vary by version/edition. Multiple layers can be justified, but measure their cost and trust/retry boundaries instead of assuming every mature deployment needs a mesh and self-managed proxy.

## Behavioral & Scenario Drills

**Q16 (scenario): After a deploy, 502s spike. Walk your diagnosis.**

**Answer:** A 502 reports failure to obtain a valid upstream response; inspect the error log before assigning a cause. Check refused/reset connections, invalid responses, pool availability and whether only one backend is affected. Startup readiness can send traffic to an unready process, while `proxy_connect_timeout` bounds a connection attempt rather than application boot time. An expired upstream wait normally produces a 504. Compare statuses and timings with retries, uploads, buffering and slow-client behavior before choosing a repair.

**Q17 (behavioral): Tell me about a config change that caused an outage despite testing.**

**Fictional example to analyze; use your own evidence in an interview:** A rate-limit change passed `nginx -t` and reload — but the new `location` block shadowed an existing one, sending `/api/` to the wrong upstream; syntax was valid, semantics were wrong. The fix: staged config testing against production traffic shapes (WebSockets, uploads, streaming), a weighted canary on the upstream for risky routing changes, and scripted `nginx -t && nginx -s reload` so validation is never skipped. Result: reloads became boring.

**Q18 (follow-up trap): "Kubernetes ingress and CDNs exist — is nginx still relevant?"**

**Answer (the nuanced answer they want):** The community ingress-nginx controller generated NGINX configuration, but it retired in March 2026 with no further fixes or security updates. The Ingress API remains; use a maintained controller or Gateway API implementation. Other controllers need not use NGINX. Raw proxy knowledge still helps diagnose the data plane actually installed. CDNs push caching one layer out, but origin TLS termination, rate limiting, and header policy still live at a proxy you own. The layers compose; knowing what each does makes the boundary a decision instead of a default.

## Edge Responsibilities

**Q19: What does proxy-layer caching buy that a CDN doesn't, and how do you configure it safely?**

**Answer:** A proxy cache can reduce repeated upstream work in a chosen inner or origin layer; a CDN can cache eligible responses farther out. Neither location makes identity isolation automatic. Define key variants, bypass and no-store decisions for authenticated/cookie requests, upstream cache-control, stale limits and purge recovery at every layer.

**Q20: Why do security headers belong at the proxy, and what's the smuggling risk?**

**Answer:** Assign header owners explicitly: shared transport headers may live at an edge while an application-specific content security policy needs application context. Test inheritance and every response path. Request smuggling arises when HTTP parsers disagree on framing; reject ambiguity and verify supported proxy/backend versions rather than treating all headers as one edge policy.

**Q21: How do you handle large uploads and downloads without Wedging the proxy?**

**Answer:** Bound body size, read/wait intervals, admitted work, memory/disk buffers and end-to-end deadlines. proxy_read_timeout and client_body_timeout are idle intervals, not entire-transfer timers. Direct object-storage transfers may reduce application load when the authorization/workflow contract permits them. Streaming, downloads and uploads need distinct buffering tests.

**Q22: What is HTTP request smuggling, and why does it implicate your proxy config?**

**Answer:** Request smuggling occurs when a front end and back end parse request boundaries differently, often around conflicting or duplicated `Content-Length` and `Transfer-Encoding` fields. A connection can then carry bytes one layer treats as a second request while another treats as a body. Keep supported proxy and application versions current, reject ambiguous framing at the boundary, and test the exact proxy-to-origin protocol path; do not assume one generic parser rule covers every version or hop. The proxy protects only the paths that actually pass through it.

**Q23: What belongs in the proxy's access logs, and what do you log at scale?**

**Answer:** Record request identity, selected upstream, status/retry observations, cache status and relevant timings with sensitive-field policy. request_time versus upstream_response_time does not alone assign blame: uploads, retries, buffering and client reads affect the values. Choose sampling and retention from incident/compliance needs and measure dropped telemetry.

**Q24 (scenario): A CVE drops in your app framework and the fix ships in 6 hours. What does the proxy buy you?**

**Answer:** A verified route restriction or other targeted edge rule may reduce exposure while a patch ships. Test the specific exploit and supported normal paths; the proxy cannot block every vulnerability or bypass route. Record owner, expiry/review and monitoring, deploy the application fix, then deliberately remove or retain the mitigation.

**Q25 (follow-up trap): "Why self-run nginx when managed load balancers exist?"**

**Answer:** Use managed or self-operated layers according to required behavior, observability, control and cost. Managed load balancers also expose reviewable API/IaC settings and useful routing policies; self-operated proxies require availability, upgrades and trust configuration. A layered NLB/proxy design is one option, not the universally mature answer.

## Summary

1. **Explain the event loop precisely** — many sockets can wait concurrently, but blocking work delays other work on that worker
2. **Reload semantics are a senior tell** — validate, spawn, drain, graceful worker replacement; `-t` catches syntax, not semantics
3. **Know versioned defaults and capacity boundaries** — keepalive, buffering, burst, connection counts, and file descriptors
4. **The proxy is the trust boundary** — TLS, limits, and header policy have explicit owners across the chosen boundaries
5. **Tool choice is a trade-off answer** — nginx edge / HAProxy throughput / Envoy dynamic / ALB managed, often layered

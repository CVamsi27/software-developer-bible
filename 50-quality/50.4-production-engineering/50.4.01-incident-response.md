# Incident Response — Triage, Communication, Postmortems

## Definition

**Incident response** is the process a team follows to identify a service problem, reduce its impact, resolve it and learn from it. Agreeing on responsibilities before an incident helps the team act quickly under stress.

> **Textbook Definition:** Incident response coordinates detection, impact assessment, mitigation, recovery and follow-up for a service disruption, with explicit decision ownership and stakeholder communication.

## First Principles

During an outage, several people act on incomplete information at once. Two uncoordinated mitigations can interfere, and a silent recovery attempt can leave support communicating the wrong impact. The team needs a shared state, a decision owner, and a record of actions and observations.

Reducing impact and explaining the cause are related but distinct tasks. A safe mitigation can restore service before the cause is understood. An unsafe rollback can worsen corrupted state. Choose the least harmful verified action available while investigation continues.

## Why Incident Response Matters

A response process makes urgent work easier to coordinate. It connects user impact to priorities, turns investigation into controlled actions, and keeps affected people informed. It cannot promise that every outage is avoidable or that a postmortem will prevent every recurrence.

## How the Roles Fit Together

An **incident commander** coordinates priorities and decisions. An **operations lead** organizes investigation and mitigation. A **communications lead** maintains updates for responders and stakeholders. A small response may begin with one person holding several responsibilities; split them when coordination and investigation compete for attention. Record every handoff so the role never becomes unowned. [Google’s incident-management guidance](https://sre.google/sre-book/managing-incidents/) describes this structure.

```text
user impact → declare shared incident → assign responsibilities
                    ↓                         ↓
             common action log ← investigation and mitigation
                    ↓                         ↓
              stakeholder updates ← recovery observations
```

Declaring an incident requests coordination; it does not certify the first diagnosis. Define escalation from impact, duration, spread, data risk and the help required. The commander can raise or lower severity as evidence changes.

### Severity is a local response contract

| Example category | Impact | Process consequence to agree in advance |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Critical | Widespread failure or active serious data/security risk | Immediate coordinated response and specialist escalation |
| High | Important journey degraded for a meaningful population | Urgent owner response and explicit update cadence |
| Lower urgency | Limited effect with an acceptable workaround | Scheduled work or a smaller response appropriate to impact |

Labels such as SEV1–SEV4 and acknowledgment targets differ by organization. An alert threshold is evidence for triage, not a universal mapping to severity. Security response may need evidence preservation and containment that differ from an ordinary availability rollback.

## Trace a Booking Outage From Detection to Recovery

This is a hypothetical service incident, not a report of a production event.

1. **Detect:** booking failures rise after a deployment. Confirm the affected operation, tenant/population and observation window. A healthy `/health` response does not establish successful bookings.
2. **Coordinate:** declare the incident, name the commander and operations owner, and open one shared action log. Pause further rollout while the team assesses the state.
3. **Investigate a bounded question:** did the deployment increase database connections or make queries wait longer? Compare connection counts, pool waits, database waits and rollout history. Correlation with a release is a hypothesis until the path is checked.
4. **Choose mitigation:** if the old binary remains compatible with the current schema and writes, consider the tested rollback. Otherwise disable the affected feature, shed selected load, or apply a reviewed forward fix. Identify the intended effect and stop/reversal condition before acting.
5. **Observe:** record the action time and compare customer-facing failure rate, latency and database pressure afterward. If errors fall but orders remain stuck, recovery is incomplete.
6. **Recover:** reconcile ambiguous writes and affected records according to their identifiers. Hold a suitable observation window and verify the critical journey before announcing resolution.
7. **Learn:** construct the timeline, examine contributing conditions and detection gaps, and assign specific follow-up actions with owners and validation evidence.

A restart might reduce temporary pressure, but can also trigger reconnect storms or abandon work. A failover can lose unreplicated writes or create conflicting ownership. Name those boundaries rather than presenting “restart/rollback/fail over” as interchangeable fixes.

## Example: Investigate Connection Exhaustion Safely

The following are read-only PostgreSQL diagnostics for a role permitted to inspect the relevant statistics. They do not identify a connection leak by themselves.

```sql
SHOW max_connections;

SELECT application_name, state, count(*) AS client_backends
FROM pg_stat_activity
WHERE backend_type = 'client backend'
GROUP BY application_name, state
ORDER BY client_backends DESC;

SELECT pid, application_name, state, xact_start, state_change,
       wait_event_type, wait_event
FROM pg_stat_activity
WHERE backend_type = 'client backend'
  AND state IS DISTINCT FROM 'idle'
ORDER BY xact_start NULLS LAST;
```

`active` means executing a query; it may still be waiting. `idle` often means a normal pooled connection awaiting work. `idle in transaction` means an open transaction has stopped issuing queries and can retain locks or a snapshot. `state_change` timestamps a state transition; `query_start` is not an idle-duration clock. Privileges affect which details are visible. See [PostgreSQL’s activity view](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/monitoring-stats.html#MONITORING-PG-STAT-ACTIVITY-VIEW).

Compare those counts with the application’s pool limits **across replicas**, waiting requests, administrative reserves and recent scale changes. A full connection allowance may come from many correctly configured pools rather than a leak. The [connection-pooling chapter](../../20-backend/20.4-database/05-operations-and-scaling/20.4.5.01-connection-pooling.md) owns acquisition, timeouts and sizing.

Do not bulk-terminate every idle backend. That can disconnect healthy pools, abort transactions and trigger retries. If cancellation or termination is necessary, identify the owned client and operation, obtain the required operational decision, understand the write outcome, and observe recovery. Query cancellation and connection termination have different effects; neither fixes the underlying source of pressure.

A runbook should include target/environment verification, permissions, read-only checks, action preconditions, bounded mitigation, stop conditions, recovery observations and escalation contacts. Rehearse it in an owned environment. A list of commands without those decisions is incomplete.

## Keep Communication Attached to Verified Impact

A useful update names the affected operation, current phase, verified scope, workaround and next update time. Separate confirmed facts from hypotheses; do not invent an ETA to fill a template.

```text
Incident: Booking API failures
Status: Investigating
Impact: Elevated booking errors in the measured production window;
        affected scope is still being checked.
Action: Rollout paused; responders are examining database pressure.
Workaround: Not yet verified.
Next update: [specific time and timezone]
```

Keep a fixed cadence appropriate to the incident even when the diagnosis has not changed. Restrict sensitive security or customer details to their proper audience. Use one working timeline for mitigation decisions, observations and handoffs; public messages may omit private details without losing the essential status.

## Turn a Postmortem Into Specific Follow-Up

**Blameless** means investigating the conditions that made an action reasonable or possible, without shaming a person. It does not remove factual ownership or accountability. Record human actions, automation, missing controls, and organizational constraints together. [The SRE Workbook’s incident-response chapter](https://sre.google/workbook/incident-response/) connects coordination to learning.

```markdown
# Postmortem: [incident]

## Impact
[Operation, population, period, correctness/data consequences, uncertainty]

## Timeline
[Times and timezone; detection, decisions, actions, observations, recovery]

## Contributing conditions
[Trigger, propagation, prevention/detection gaps, evidence and alternatives]

## Follow-up actions
| Change | Owner | Due date | Evidence of completion |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [Specific control or repair] | [Owner] | [Date] | [Check or observation] |

## Remaining risk
[Unresolved conditions, tracking and review owner]
```

“Be more careful” supplies no mechanism to verify. “Reject a pool configuration whose replica-wide connection budget exceeds the approved allowance” identifies a concrete control. Check whether completed actions actually reduce the relevant failure mode; creating a document or ticket is not the same as implementing the repair.

## Real-World Decisions

A game day can rehearse incident roles, permission gaps and recovery observations. The [chaos chapter](50.4.03-chaos-engineering.md) owns fault injection and bounded experiments; do not introduce new faults while an actual incident is uncontrolled.

Hand over when fatigue degrades reasoning. State impact, active hypotheses, actions tried, next decisions and open risks; confirm who now owns command. Track recurrence and response friction, while remembering that shorter observed recovery alone may reflect a less severe incident rather than better handling.

## Common Mistakes

- Investigating privately while no one coordinates overlapping changes.
- Equating an alert, green process health, or falling error count with complete customer recovery.
- Rolling back without checking schema, durable writes or security evidence.
- Terminating healthy idle pools or restarting every replica together.
- Treating severity labels and response-minute targets as universal standards.
- Closing a postmortem when action items are merely written rather than verified.

## Best Practices

Prepare roles and runbooks before the incident. Declare uncertainty and impact early, keep a shared timeline, prefer bounded known-safe mitigation, and observe its real effect. Restore customer correctness as well as availability. Follow through on specific actions and rehearse the next response with the team that owns it.

## Summary

Incident response coordinates impact assessment, investigation, mitigation and recovery. Clear ownership and shared observations prevent conflicting work. Choose mitigation from its state and safety boundaries, communicate verified impact, and turn the timeline into owned repairs with evidence of completion.

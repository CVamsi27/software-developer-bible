# SLIs, SLOs, and Error Budgets

## TL;DR

**SLI is the measurement, SLO the internal promise, SLA the external contract** — in increasing order of commitment. The SLO is chosen from what users actually feel (a latency or availability SLI measured at the edge), and the **error budget** (1 − SLO) turns reliability into a spendable resource: 99.9% is ~43 minutes a month that features may consume. Alert on burn *rate* (fast burn for pages, slow burn for tickets) rather than on individual failures, and when the budget is exhausted the priority flips from shipping to stability — that switch is the whole point of having one.

---

## First Principles

(→ 40.4.3.01 covers the axioms; the practice view:)

1. **Error budgets operationalize reliability trade-offs**: burn the budget → feature freeze on the service until reliability recovers — the policy converts SLO from dashboard to *decision*.
2. **Multi-window burn alerts are the paging contract** (→ 40.4.3.02): fast+slow burn pairs catch both spikes and bleeds without flapping.

## Definition

**SLI vs SLO vs SLA:** An SLI (Service Level Indicator) is a metric — like "99.5% of requests complete under 200ms". An SLO (Service Level Objective) is the target you set for that metric — like "we aim for 99.5%". An SLA (Service Level Agreement) is a contractual commitment with consequences — like "if uptime drops below 99.5%, we owe customers credits". SLOs are internal engineering goals; SLAs are external promises. Error budgets come from SLOs: if your SLO is 99.9%, your error budget is 0.1% — meaning you can afford 43 minutes of downtime per month before you must stop shipping features and focus on reliability.


| Term | Definition | Example |
| --------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| **SLI** (Service Level Indicator) | Quantitative measure of service behavior | Request latency, error rate, throughput |
| **SLO** (Service Level Objective) | Target value for an SLI | 99.9% of requests complete in < 200ms |
| **SLA** (Service Level Agreement) | Contractual commitment with consequences | 99.5% uptime, credits if violated |
| **Error Budget** | Allowed failures before SLO is breached | 0.1% = 43 minutes/month of downtime |

## How It Works

### The Reliability Hierarchy: SLI → SLO → SLA

Reliability engineering translates human user expectations into mathematical contracts through three complementary constructs:

1. **Service Level Indicator (SLI)**: The quantitative measurement of service performance over time.
   $$\text{SLI} = \frac{\text{Good Events}}{\text{Total Valid Events}} \times 100$$
   Examples: Successful HTTP responses ($<500$) divided by total requests; API calls completed within 200ms divided by total requests.

2. **Service Level Objective (SLO)**: The target reliability percentage agreed upon internally between engineering and product teams over a specified compliance window (typically rolling 30 days).
   $$\text{Target} = 99.9\% \text{ over 30 days}$$

3. **Service Level Agreement (SLA)**: The legal or commercial contract with external customers defining penalties (service credits, financial compensation) if service quality breaches agreed floors.
   Rule of thumb: Always set SLAs looser than internal SLOs (e.g. 99.5% SLA for a 99.9% SLO) to ensure internal teams detect and remediate degradation before legal liabilities trigger.

4. **The Error Budget**: The inverse of the SLO ($1 - \text{SLO}$). If the SLO is 99.9%, the error budget is 0.1%. It transforms reliability from an abstract ideal into a finite, spendable resource allocated between feature velocity (risk taking, chaos experiments, releases) and technical debt remediation.

### Downtime Allowances by SLO Tier

| Target SLO | Downtime / Month (30 days) | Downtime / Year (365 days) | Target Workload Profile |
| ----------- | -------------------------- | -------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| **99.0%** | 7.2 hours | 3.65 days | Internal dashboards, batch data pipelines |
| **99.5%** | 3.6 hours | 1.83 days | Non-critical customer portals |
| **99.9%** | 43.2 minutes | 8.76 hours | Standard SaaS APIs, e-commerce storefronts |
| **99.95%** | 21.6 minutes | 4.38 hours | Core authentication, API gateways |
| **99.99%** | 4.32 minutes | 52.6 minutes | Payment processors, high-frequency ledgers |
| **99.999%** | 25.9 seconds | 5.26 minutes | Emergency services, telecommunications trunk |

### Multi-Window Multi-Burn-Rate Alerting

Alerting on simple error thresholds (e.g. "error rate > 1%") creates alert fatigue from transient blips. Modern SRE uses **burn rate alerting**:
- **Burn Rate 1**: Consumes 100% of the error budget over exactly 30 days.
- **Burn Rate 14.4**: Consumes 2% of budget in 1 hour; 5% in 6 hours (triggers emergency page).
- **Multi-Window Requirement**: Requires both a long window (e.g. 1 hour) and a short reset window (e.g. 5 minutes) to exceed the burn rate threshold simultaneously. This prevents pages when a brief 30-second spike has already resolved.

## Code Examples

### 1. Prometheus SLI Recording Rules (PromQL)

```yaml
# prometheus-sli-rules.yaml
groups:
  - name: api_sli_recording_rules
    interval: 30s
    rules:
      # Availability SLI: Ratio of non-5xx requests over 5m rolling window
      - record: job:http_requests_success:ratio_rate5m
        expr: |
          sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="api-gateway", status!~"5.."}[5m]))
          /
          sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="api-gateway"}[5m]))

      # Latency SLI: Ratio of requests completing in under 200ms
      - record: job:http_requests_fast:ratio_rate5m
        expr: |
          sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_bucket{job="api-gateway", le="0.2"}[5m]))
          /
          sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_bucket{job="api-gateway", le="+Inf"}[5m]))
```

### 2. Multi-Window Multi-Burn-Rate Alerting Rule

```yaml
# prometheus-slo-alerts.yaml
groups:
  - name: checkout_slo_alerts
    rules:
      # Page immediately: 2% of 30-day budget burned in 1 hour (14.4x burn rate)
      # Both 1-hour and 5-minute windows must validate burn to avoid transient spike pages
      - alert: CheckoutApiErrorBudgetBurningFast
        expr: |
          (
            sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout-api", status=~"5.."}[1h]))
            /
            sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout-api"}[1h]))
          ) > (1 - 0.999) * 14.4
          and
          (
            sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout-api", status=~"5.."}[5m]))
            /
            sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout-api"}[5m]))
          ) > (1 - 0.999) * 14.4
        for: 2m
        labels:
          severity: page
          tier: tier-1
        annotations:
          summary: "Checkout API burning error budget at 14.4x normal pace"
          description: "At current burn rate, 30-day error budget will deplete within 50 hours."
```

### 3. Programmatic Error Budget Calculator

```typescript
export interface ErrorBudgetReport {
  sloPercentage: number;
  totalWindowMinutes: number;
  allowedDowntimeMinutes: number;
  consumedDowntimeMinutes: number;
  budgetRemainingPercentage: number;
  isBudgetExhausted: boolean;
}

export function calculateErrorBudget(
  sloPercent: number,
  failedRequests: number,
  totalRequests: number,
  windowDays: number = 30
): ErrorBudgetReport {
  const totalWindowMinutes = windowDays * 24 * 60;
  const allowedDowntimeFraction = (100 - sloPercent) / 100;
  const allowedDowntimeMinutes = totalWindowMinutes * allowedDowntimeFraction;

  const actualErrorRate = totalRequests > 0 ? failedRequests / totalRequests : 0;
  const consumedFraction = actualErrorRate / allowedDowntimeFraction;
  const budgetRemainingPercentage = Math.max(0, (1 - consumedFraction) * 100);

  return {
    sloPercentage: sloPercent,
    totalWindowMinutes,
    allowedDowntimeMinutes: Math.round(allowedDowntimeMinutes * 100) / 100,
    consumedDowntimeMinutes: Math.round(consumedFraction * allowedDowntimeMinutes * 100) / 100,
    budgetRemainingPercentage: Math.round(budgetRemainingPercentage * 100) / 100,
    isBudgetExhausted: budgetRemainingPercentage <= 0,
  };
}
```

## Real-World Use Cases

- **E-Commerce Checkout Availability (99.9%)**: Paging on-call when 2% of the monthly error budget burns in 1 hour; non-critical deployments freeze automatically during peak traffic windows if remaining budget drops below 10%.
- **Tier-1 Financial Ledger API (99.99%)**: Tight 4.32 minutes/month error budget; canary deployments auto-rollback within 60 seconds if canary error rate burns more than 0.1% of the monthly allocation.
- **Internal Backoffice Reporting (99.0%)**: Relaxed SLO permitting rapid feature deployments, routine schema migrations, and scheduled maintenance windows without triggering false off-hours pages.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Risk / Root Cause | Proper SRE Practice |
| ------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Setting an unrealistic 100% uptime SLO | 100% is economically unviable and prevents engineering teams from releasing features | Set SLOs based on user tolerance; preserve an error budget for innovation and deployments |
| Paging on instantaneous error rate or raw count spikes | Temporary 5-second network retries trigger false on-call wakeups | Implement multi-window multi-burn-rate alerts (e.g. 1h + 5m window requirement) |
| Measuring SLIs from internal health checks instead of edge | Health checks pass even when edge CDN, TLS termination, or API gateway routing is failing | Measure SLIs directly at user-facing boundary ingress or client telemetry endpoints |
| Having SLOs without an enforceable Error Budget Policy | When reliability degrades, product teams continue pushing features, ignoring stability | Agree on peacetime policies: code freezes and reliability sprints trigger when budget hits 0% |
| Setting public SLA identical to internal SLO | An internal SLO breach immediately incurs legal penalties and financial SLA credits | Provide a safety buffer: if internal SLO is 99.9%, set contractual SLA at 99.5% |

## Best Practices

1. **Measure at the User Boundary** — capture SLIs from the API Gateway or edge proxies where real customer requests terminate
2. **Standardize on Rolling 30-Day Windows** — calendar month windows reset artificially on the 1st; rolling windows provide continuous reliability accountability
3. **Establish Error Budget Policies in Peacetime** — define explicit rules for feature freezes and reliability-only sprints before an outage consumes the budget
4. **Use Multi-Window Burn Rate Alerts** — pair long and short timeframes to catch fast drains while eliminating transient noise
5. **Differentiate Tiers by Criticality** — avoid blanket SLOs across all services; assign 99.99% to transactional cores and 99.0% to non-blocking background workers

## Summary

1. **SLI is the measurement, SLO is the promise, SLA is the contract** — in that order of increasing external commitment
2. **The error budget converts reliability into a spendable resource** — 99.9% means 43 minutes/month of allowed failure; features and stability trade inside it
3. **Burn-rate alerting catches outages early without alert fatigue** — multi-window (fast + slow) alerts fire on pace, not absolute counts
4. **The error-budget policy is pre-agreed governance** — budget exhausted → feature freeze → reliability work; decided in peacetime, executed in incident time
5. **SLOs are product decisions, not engineering vanity metrics** — pick SLIs users actually feel (latency, success rate, freshness) at thresholds tied to behavior

## Cheat Sheet
```text
SLIS, SLOS & ERROR BUDGETS CHEAT SHEET
============================================================

CORE DEFINITIONS:
  • SLI (Indicator) -> What you measure: Good events / Total events (e.g. 99.92%)
  • SLO (Objective) -> Internal target: Agreed reliability goal (e.g. 99.9% over 30d)
  • SLA (Agreement) -> External contract: Legal commitment with financial penalties (e.g. 99.5%)
  • Error Budget    -> Allowed failure: 1 - SLO (e.g. 0.1% for 99.9% SLO)

MONTHLY DOWNTIME BUDGETS (30 DAYS):
  • 99.0%   -> 7.2 hours
  • 99.5%   -> 3.6 hours
  • 99.9%   -> 43.2 minutes
  • 99.95%  -> 21.6 minutes
  • 99.99%  -> 4.32 minutes
  • 99.999% -> 25.9 seconds

BURN RATE FORMULA:
  • Burn Rate = (Observed Error Rate) / (Allowed Error Budget Rate)
  • Rate 1.0  = Depletes 100% budget in exactly 30 days
  • Rate 14.4 = Depletes 2% budget in 1 hour (PAGE ON-CALL)
  • Rate 6.0  = Depletes 5% budget in 6 hours (URGENT TICKET)

ERROR BUDGET GOVERNANCE:
  • Healthy (>50%)   -> Normal feature delivery and chaos experiments
  • Low (<20%)       -> Extra review required; halt non-essential releases
  • Exhausted (0%)   -> Feature freeze; 100% engineering effort on reliability
```

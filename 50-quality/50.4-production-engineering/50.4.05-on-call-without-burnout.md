# On-Call Without Burnout

## Definition

Sustainable on-call is an engineered system — rotation topology, paging criteria, alert lifecycle, recovery policies — designed so that carrying the pager does not degrade either service quality or engineer health. Its inputs are alert design and incident process; its outputs are measurable: interrupt load per shift, time-to-acknowledge, post-shift recovery, retention of on-call staff.

```text
ON-CALL SYSTEM DESIGN
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ ROTATION      primary / secondary, weekly or daily slots,    │
│               follow-the-sun optional, max 1 week in 4-6     │
│ PAGING RULES  page = actionable + urgent + user impact       │
│               everything else -> ticket or dashboard         │
│ GOVERNANCE    monthly page audit: prune, dedup, retune       │
│ HUMANE POLICY comp time post-incident, no-blame, swap rights │
│ HEALTH METRICS pages/shift p50+p90, night interrupts,        │
│               ack latency, skipped handoffs                  │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

## First Principles

On-call is sustainable only if the *system* of response is healthy:

1. **Pages must be actionable and rare** (→ 40.4.3.02): every noise page spends the responder's trust; the burnout math is pages-per-shift × cognitive cost — fix alerting before rotating people.
2. **Follow-the-sun and compensations are structural answers**: the load must be shared and *paid* — on-call burden is a real cost that either appears in staffing/comp or appears as attrition.
3. **Post-incident care is part of the system**: the blameless review (→ 50.4.01) is also the debrief that converts stress into learning rather than residue.

## Why it matters

Burnout is not soft talk here — it is an availability risk. Exhausted responders misdiagnose, escalate late, and quit; attrition then concentrates pager load on survivors in a death spiral. Google's SRE book sets explicit limits (maximum 2 pages per 12-hour shift, 25% of time on-call) precisely because interrupt load is quantifiable. Interviewers at senior level increasingly ask "how did you keep your team healthy?" — expecting named metrics and policy changes, not wellness-app referrals.

## Why Do We Need It?

- **Alert fatigue is measurable**: when 80% of pages require no action, responders stop treating pages as signals; real incidents get acknowledged late.
- **Sleep fragmentation compounds**: each nighttime page costs more than its minutes — recovery research shows cognitive impairment lasting hours.
- **Fairness prevents rot**: unbalanced rota (one "expert" absorbing everything) creates single points of human failure.
- **Handoffs lose context**: undocumented shifts force rediscovery of ongoing issues, doubling MTTR across boundaries.
- **Retention economics**: replacing a senior engineer costs ~6 months of salary+productivity; rotation hygiene is cheap insurance.
- **Legal exposure**: uncompensated overnight work violates labor norms in many jurisdictions (EU working-time rules explicitly).

## How It Works

### Rotation design

```text
FOLLOW-THE-SUN (3 regions)          SINGLE-TZ TEAM
  EU 09-18 UTC+1 ──> US 09-18 EST    primary  : this week's responder
  US hands to APAC ──> APAC->EU       secondary: escalation + shadow,
  nobody carries nights               next week's primary (training)

RULES THAT KEEP IT FAIR:
• Max 1 week on primary per 4-6 weeks (per person)
• Secondary is mandatory - single-person rota is a resignation risk
• Swaps allowed anytime via tooling, never via DM guilt
• New joiners ride along as secondary for 2 cycles minimum
```

### The paging philosophy

A page must pass three tests simultaneously:

1. **Actionable**: something can be done *now* by the responder (restart, failover, rollback, scale).
2. **Urgent**: waiting until business hours causes user-visible damage or SLO burn.
3. **Symptom-based**: fires on what users experience (error rate, latency), not on internal guesses (CPU 85%).

Everything else routes to tickets, dashboards, or business-hours notifications:

| Signal | Route |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------- |
| 5xx rate burning error budget fast | Page |
| Single-node OOM, auto-restarted, fleet healthy | Ticket |
| Disk 75% full, growth trend slow | Business-hours task |
| Replica lag 30s, read-your-writes affected | Page (user impact) |
| Deploy failed pipeline | Notify team channel |

### Alert fatigue governance

Run a monthly page audit with three moves:

- **Prune**: any alert that fired ≥5 times with zero actions taken gets deleted or demoted to ticket. Track "action ratio" per alert rule.
- **Dedup**: collapse flapping sources — group by service+symptom, use inhibition (node-down silences its dependents' alerts).
- **Retune**: multi-window burn-rate alerts instead of static thresholds kill most false pages.

### Handoff ritual

Fifteen minutes, structured, written:

```markdown
## Handoff template (filled before the call)
- Open incidents: INC-2201 (payments latency, status: mitigated, watch till 14:00)
- Risky changes this week: search reindex Thursday (rollback ready)
- Known noisy alerts silenced: legacy-cron-disk (ticket #889)
- Follow-ups owed: postmortem INC-2196 due Friday
- Nothing else outstanding
```

The written record survives; verbal-only handoffs evaporate by lunchtime.

## Code Examples

### Multi-window burn-rate paging (the anti-noise pattern)

```yaml
# Prometheus alert: page only on fast SLO burn, ticket on slow burn
groups:
  - name: checkout-slo
    rules:
      - alert: CheckoutErrorBudgetFastBurn
        expr: |
          (
            sum(rate(http_requests_total{service="checkout",code=~"5.."}[5m]))
            /
            sum(rate(http_requests_total{service="checkout"}[5m]))
          ) > (14.4 * 0.001)
          and
          (
            sum(rate(http_requests_total{service="checkout",code=~"5.."}[1h]))
            /
            sum(rate(http_requests_total{service="checkout"}[1h]))
          ) > (14.4 * 0.001)
        labels:
          severity: page          # 2% budget in 1h -> wake someone
        annotations:
          runbook: https://wiki/runbooks/checkout-error-rate

      - alert: CheckoutErrorBudgetSlowBurn
        expr: |
          (
            sum(rate(http_requests_total{service="checkout",code=~"5.."}[6h]))
            /
            sum(rate(http_requests_total{service="checkout"}[6h]))
          ) > (6 * 0.001)
        labels:
          severity: ticket        # 5% budget in 6h -> morning task
        annotations:
          runbook: https://wiki/runbooks/checkout-error-rate
```

### Measuring on-call health from PagerDuty-style exports

```typescript
type Incident = {
  alertKey: string;
  createdAt: Date;
  acknowledgedAt: Date;
  resolvedAt: Date;
  escalated: boolean;
};

export function healthReport(shifts: Incident[], pagesAllowed = 2) {
  const byAlert = new Map<string, number>();
  let nightPages = 0;
  const ackSeconds: number[] = [];

  for (const inc of shifts) {
    byAlert.set(inc.alertKey, (byAlert.get(inc.alertKey) ?? 0) + 1);
    const hour = inc.createdAt.getHours();
    if (hour >= 22 || hour < 7) nightPages++;
    ackSeconds.push(
      (inc.acknowledgedAt.getTime() - inc.createdAt.getTime()) / 1000,
    );
  }

  const noisy = [...byAlert.entries()]
    .filter(([, count]) => count >= 5)
    .map(([key]) => key);

  return {
    pagesPerShiftP90: percentile(shifts.length, 0.9),
    exceedsGoogleLimit: shifts.length > pagesAllowed * 2, // per 12h
    nightInterrupts: nightPages,
    medianAckSeconds: percentile(ackSeconds.sort((a, b) => a - b), 0.5),
    pruneCandidates: noisy, // feed monthly governance review
  };
}
```

Teams publish this monthly next to their SLO dashboard — pager health gets the same visibility as service health.

## Real-World Use Cases

1. **Marketplace kills half its alerts** — audit found 61% of pages had zero action ratio; pruning and moving thresholds to burn-rate cut pages/shift from 11 to 1.8 while MTTR *improved* (responders trusted pages again).
2. **Follow-the-sun rollout across Berlin/Austin/Singapore** — night interrupts per engineer dropped to near zero; secondary slot doubles as cross-region training, ending the "only Dave understands billing" bus factor.
3. **Post-incident comp policy formalized** — anyone paged between 22:00–07:00 takes the next morning off automatically, tracked in the rota tool; voluntary compliance became consistent practice, and attrition on the platform team fell the following year.
4. **Inhibition rules end cascade storms** — one ToR switch failure used to fire 40 dependent-service alerts; alertmanager inhibition now delivers exactly one page ("network zone B unreachable") with a runbook covering blast radius.
5. **Startup adopts humane on-call early** — two-engineer team alternates weeks with explicit rules: max one night-page cycle per month each, paid on-call stipend, and permission to wake the founder (who owns tertiary); sustainable through seed-stage scale.

## Common Mistakes

```yaml
# BAD: threshold alert on internal metric, pages all night
- alert: HighCPU
  expr: cpu_usage > 0.85
  labels: { severity: page }   # CPU 86% at 03:00 = nobody acts, everyone wakes

# GOOD: symptom-based, burn-rate gated, runbook linked
- alert: CheckoutLatencyBudgetBurn
  expr: burn_rate(latency_slo) > 14.4   # fast-burn only
  labels: { severity: page }
  annotations:
    runbook: wiki/runbooks/checkout-latency
```

Recurring failures:

- **Single-person rota** ("Dave knows it best") — both a burnout machine and a bus factor of one.
- **No secondary** — first vacation reveals the design flaw.
- **Tribal handoffs** — hallway "you got anything?" with no written state; open incidents vanish.
- **Paging on causes, not symptoms** — disk/CPU/memory pages flood while user-facing failures arrive via customer support Twitter.
- **Hero culture rewards** — celebrating the 3 AM martyr instead of asking why the system needed one; no-blame must extend to *taking* the page, not just causing it.
- **Metrics collected, never acted on** — dashboards showing 15 pages/shift that nobody treats as a defect; set a target and govern toward it.

## Best Practices

1. Cap on-call frequency (≤1 week in 4-6 per person) and enforce with rota tooling, not goodwill.
2. Always staff primary + secondary; treat solo coverage as an outage of the on-call system itself.
3. Page only actionable, symptom-based, user-impacting conditions — everything else is a ticket.
4. Run a monthly page audit: prune high-volume/no-action alerts, add inhibition, convert thresholds to burn rates.
5. Written handoff template, completed before every shift change; include risky deploys and open watches.
6. Automatic comp time after night incidents (e.g., next morning off after 22:00–07:00 pages); stipends where legally required.
7. No-blame response: blameless postmortems plus explicit norms that waking someone is always acceptable.
8. Track and publish on-call health metrics monthly; give them an owner like any other reliability target.

## Performance Considerations

There is a feedback loop between operational load and system performance: fatigued responders raise MTTR, which lengthens shifts under stress, which deepens fatigue — breaking the loop early (alert pruning) improves both humans and metrics. Keep runbook steps executable in under five minutes each; slow runbooks inflate resolution time disproportionately at night. Automate swappable rota management — manual coordination overhead grows quadratically with team size and quietly pushes engineers back to DM-based scheduling. Finally, measure acknowledgment latency as a leading indicator: rising ack times predict fatigue weeks before attrition does.

## Summary

- On-call is a designed system, not a rite of passage: rotation topology, paging criteria, governance, and recovery policy.
- Pages must be actionable, urgent, and symptom-based; everything else degrades to tickets without shame.
- Primary/secondary rotations with frequency caps and optional follow-the-sun distribute load and train successors.
- Monthly alert audits (prune/dedup/retune to burn-rate) keep action ratios high and trust in pages intact.
- Humane policies — comp time, stipends, swaps, no-blame — are reliability controls, not perks.
- Health metrics (pages/shift, night interrupts, ack latency) belong on the same wall as SLOs with an owner and targets.

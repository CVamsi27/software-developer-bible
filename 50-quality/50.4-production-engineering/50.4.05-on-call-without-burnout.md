# On-Call Without Burnout

A database failure wakes one engineer six times — and nobody else can authorize recovery. Moving that engineer to another week fixes nothing: the team needs fewer duplicate interruptions, a working backup path, and enough capacity to improve the recurring failure.

```typescript
// Three shifts with 0, 2 and 6 pages: nearest-rank p90 is 6.
healthReport([{ pages: 0, nightInterrupts: 0, acknowledgedInSeconds: [], unacknowledged: 0 },
              /* two more normalized shift records */]);
```

## Definition

Sustainable **on-call** assigns responsibility for incident response while controlling interruption **load**, escalation, staffing and recovery time. A rotation is sustainable when the team can protect user outcomes without relying on repeated exhaustion or one indispensable responder.

> **Textbook Definition:** An on-call system assigns time-bounded responsibility for responding to service incidents, with escalation, actionable alerts and staffing arrangements that sustain both reliability and engineer health.

## First Principles

Incidents arrive unevenly, and response consumes attention that cannot simultaneously serve planned engineering work. A roster distributes responsibility but cannot create capacity — and repeated night interruptions change the responder's ability to investigate safely the next day. Reliable response therefore requires three linked decisions: which signals interrupt a person, who can act or escalate, and how the team restores capacity after the interruption.

## How It Works

### 1. Design Coverage From the Actual Workload

Name a primary responder, a backup and an escalation owner for each interval. Explain their authority, access and expected availability. Train new responders with supported practice before making them solely responsible, and make swaps explicit in the roster so an alert still reaches the right person.

Measure the workload before choosing shift length and rotation frequency: a quiet weekly rotation and repeated overnight incidents impose different costs. There is no universal shift length or page quota that makes a rotation sustainable — include required response time, handoff gaps, backup capacity and recovery time in the design. **Follow-the-sun** transfers responsibility between teams during their local working hours, but works only with qualified responders at every boundary, including weekends and holidays. Use explicit UTC intervals, account for daylight-saving changes, and identify who owns gaps or failed handoffs.

### 2. Page for an Urgent Action, Including Imminent Harm

A page should identify current or imminent user harm plus an action that cannot wait for the normal work queue. A cause-based signal can qualify when the causal path and deadline are clear — a disk predicted to fill in ten minutes may warrant action before user errors begin.

| Observation | Example route | Condition that changes the decision |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Sustained booking error-budget burn | Page the responder | Verify the measurement population, coverage and ongoing impact |
| One replica restarts; fleet capacity remains adequate | Ticket or automated follow-up | Page if repeated loss threatens required redundancy or user outcomes |
| Disk growth predicts exhaustion next week | Planned work | Page when the remaining action window becomes short |
| Replica lag breaks a required freshness contract | Page or disable affected reads | A workload that explicitly accepts that staleness may have a different policy |
| Deployment fails before traffic changes | Notify the release owner | Page if the failure left production degraded or blocks urgent recovery |

The [SLO chapter](50.4.02-sli-slo-error-budgets.md) owns the worked burn-rate calculation and alert example. Multi-window rules reduce some transient pages but need missing-data, low-traffic and escalation policies — they do not make every noisy rule correct. Group related signals into an incident and use inhibition carefully: suppressing dependent alerts prevents cascade pages, but an overly broad rule can hide a second independent failure. Rehearse representative combinations and retain enough context in the delivered page.

### 3. Trace an Alert Audit

In a **hypothetical** month, a pool warning pages twelve times. Eight pages arrive after automatic recovery and require no further action; four precede booking failures and require admission control. Do not delete the rule just because most pages produced no command — compare its timing and recovery context. The team might ticket the recovered warning, page sustained user impact, and add an early saturation alert when action can still prevent failure. Then check both interruption count **and missed or delayed detection** after the change.

Review duplicates, flapping, stale runbooks, access failures and repeated incidents with the team, and fund follow-up work. A well-tuned alert still creates excessive load when the service is unstable or the rotation is understaffed.

### 4. Measure Shifts, Including Quiet Ones

A percentile is computed over a defined set of observations — one shift total cannot estimate the distribution across shifts. Keep one record per shift, including shifts with zero pages, and define whether the counting unit is a notification, grouped incident or actual interruption. This pure function consumes **already normalized shift records**: `pages` counts the chosen page unit, `nightInterrupts` counts the subset waking the responder during the agreed quiet-hours window, and each acknowledgment delay corresponds to one page.

```typescript
type Shift = {
  pages: number;
  nightInterrupts: number; // Subset of pages that interrupted the responder during quiet hours.
  acknowledgedInSeconds: number[];
  unacknowledged: number;
};

function nearestRank(values: number[], probability: number): number | null {
  if (values.length === 0) return null;
  const sorted = [...values].sort((a, b) => a - b);
  return sorted[Math.ceil(probability * sorted.length) - 1];
}

export function healthReport(shifts: Shift[]) {
  for (const shift of shifts) {
    if (![shift.pages, shift.nightInterrupts, shift.unacknowledged]
          .every(n => Number.isSafeInteger(n) && n >= 0) ||
        shift.nightInterrupts > shift.pages ||
        shift.acknowledgedInSeconds.length + shift.unacknowledged !== shift.pages ||
        !shift.acknowledgedInSeconds.every(n => Number.isFinite(n) && n >= 0)) {
      throw new RangeError('Invalid normalized shift');
    }
  }
  const acknowledgments = shifts.flatMap(s => s.acknowledgedInSeconds);
  return {
    shiftCount: shifts.length,
    totalPages: shifts.reduce((n, s) => n + s.pages, 0),
    pagesPerShiftP90: nearestRank(shifts.map(s => s.pages), 0.9),
    nightInterrupts: shifts.reduce((n, s) => n + s.nightInterrupts, 0),
    acknowledgmentP50Seconds: nearestRank(acknowledgments, 0.5),
    unacknowledged: shifts.reduce((n, s) => n + s.unacknowledged, 0),
  };
}
```

For three shifts with 0, 2 and 6 pages, nearest-rank p90 is 6. If the two-page shift acknowledges in 10 and 30 seconds and the six-page shift in 5, 5, 20, 60 and 120 seconds plus one unacknowledged page, acknowledgment p50 is 20 seconds with 1 unacknowledged. The missing page is neither a zero-second success nor silently discarded. An empty set returns null percentiles, not evidence of a healthy rotation — and a small sample produces a coarse percentile, so examine distributions, high-impact incidents and responder feedback as well. The export adapter must assign pages to the correct roster interval, deduplicate as specified, and classify night interruptions using the responder's time zone and agreed quiet hours; it does not infer fatigue or legal compliance.

### 5. Transfer Responsibility and Protect Recovery Time

A handoff records open incidents, current hypotheses, actions and stop conditions, next checks, recent risky changes, muted alerts with expiry, and escalation contacts. The incoming owner acknowledges responsibility. Use a shared incident record for the detailed timeline rather than copying incompatible summaries into multiple chats. After a disruptive shift, arrange backup coverage and protected recovery time — and never rely on an exhausted responder to negotiate rest during an incident.

Check fairness across locations, seniority, caregiving constraints and expert dependencies: an escalation that always reaches the same specialist is a hidden second rotation. Improve access, documentation and training so the system holds more than one viable recovery path. Compensation, availability requirements and working-time rules depend on the employment arrangement and jurisdiction; set them with the relevant policy owners. Design coverage, authority and interrupt policy together, and give the health of the response system an owner.

## Common Mistakes

- Moving responders around while leaving unstable services and repeated pages unaddressed.
- Calling a single scalar a percentile or omitting zero-page shifts from the denominator.
- Classifying night pages using the export server's local clock rather than the responder's roster.
- Treating an unacknowledged page as successful or pruning an alert without checking missed detection.
- Promising global coverage without accounting for gaps, holidays and handoff ownership.

## Summary

Sustainable on-call depends on actionable urgent pages, capable coverage, explicit handoffs and recovery capacity. Measure real shifts and their interruptions — including missing acknowledgments and quiet shifts. Improve the service and staffing as well as alert quality, and verify that fewer pages still protect user outcomes.

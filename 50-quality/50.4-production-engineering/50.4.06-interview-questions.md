# Production Engineering Interview Questions


## Definition

These questions practice operating a service: coordinating incidents, defining reliability targets, verifying recovery and keeping response work sustainable. Treat each answer as a reasoning model. In an interview, connect it to a truthful example, your own contribution and the conditions that would change the decision.

## First Principles

Production decisions combine incomplete evidence, user harm and limited response capacity. A strong answer explains the observation, the available action, its preconditions and how the result is verified. A memorized rule such as “always roll back” fails when written state has changed.

### Q1. Production is down, three teams are involved. Walk me through your first fifteen minutes.

**Model answer:** Confirm the user impact, open a shared incident record and name a coordinator. Assign investigation and communication owners as people become available; initially one responder may cover several roles. Record the current deployment, affected operations and a short hypothesis list. Prefer a known-safe mitigation while doing enough diagnosis to check its preconditions: a previous binary must still understand the current schema and written data. Announce the next update time and verify recovery at the user boundary, including queued or uncertain writes.

**Probe:** What if rollback could destroy state? → Check binary/schema compatibility and write ownership. Use a bounded alternative or forward repair when the old version cannot safely operate on current state.

### Q2. What makes a postmortem blameless *and* useful? Most are neither.

**Model answer:** Describe what responders knew at each point and why their actions made sense with that information. Separate the trigger, contributing conditions, detection gaps and recovery obstacles instead of reducing the explanation to one person or one root cause. Assign a small set of risk-reduction actions with owners, dates and measurable checks. For example, a pool-exhaustion incident might produce a fleet-wide connection-capacity check and a load test that proves overload queues remain bounded. Blameless analysis preserves accountability for following through; it does not guarantee every incident class can be eliminated.

### Q3. Explain error budgets to a product manager who wants 99.99% for everything.

**Model answer:** First define the user operation, eligible population, good outcome and window. A 99.9% request-success target permits 100 bad events among 100,000 eligible requests; an elapsed-time target over exactly 30 days permits 43.2 minutes, and these are different contracts. Compare stricter targets with user tolerance, dependency capability, operational cost and contractual requirements. Agree separately on actions during fast burn or exhaustion, including exceptions for security and remediation releases. There is no universal target by service name or fixed tenfold cost for another nine.

**Probe:** What if the target has no response policy? → Identify the owner and agree on actions, exceptions and resumption criteria before treating the dashboard as a release control.

### Q4. How do you run a chaos experiment without causing an outage?

**Model answer:** You cannot guarantee that a production experiment causes no harm. Define a falsifiable hypothesis, the baseline user metrics, authorized scope, exclusions, stop thresholds and a tested way to end the injection. For a Redis-loss experiment, trace whether the database can absorb fallback reads rather than assuming fallback is safe. Start in a controlled environment, then use the smallest representative production slice when the residual risk is acceptable. A stopped injection can leave retries, queues or corrupted data behind, so check recovery after the fault is removed.

### Q5. A team wants to launch a new service. What does your production readiness review actually check?

**Model answer:** Ask for evidence at the service boundaries: ownership, access controls, user-relevant indicators, actionable alerts, dependency failure behavior, load limits and recovery. Have the team demonstrate a compatible rollback or forward repair and restore a backup if persistent state matters. Derive the load profile and headroom from forecast uncertainty, redundancy and scaling delay; twice projected peak is an illustrative test point, not a universal gate. Record unresolved risks with an owner, scope and expiry rather than treating every checkbox as equally launch-blocking.

### Q6. Your best engineer is burning out on call. What do you change?

**Model answer:** Discuss workload and immediate recovery with the engineer, then review page volume, sleep interruptions, difficult incidents, staffing, escalation support and follow-up work. Poor alert quality is one possible cause; a small rotation or hostile response culture can produce burnout even with actionable alerts. Remove duplicate or nonurgent pages, improve runbooks and reserve funded time to reduce recurring incidents. Ensure backup coverage, protected rest and a fair rotation. Choose sustainable staffing from the actual interrupt load rather than prescribing the same rotation size to every team.

### Q7. What is toil, how do you measure it, and when should you refuse to automate it?

**Model answer:** Toil is manual, repetitive, automatable operational work with little lasting value that grows with service size. Measure time and interruptions, including hidden work outside tickets, then compare automation cost with expected frequency and risk. Automating a daily validated cleanup may free more capacity than automating a rare investigation. Keep judgment-heavy containment decisions under human control while automating safe evidence collection or bounded substeps. Review the automation owner, failure mode and maintenance cost; a script that requires constant rescue has merely moved the toil.

### Q8. Design alerting for an SLO. Why multi-window burn rates instead of threshold alerts?

**Model answer:** Burn rate divides the observed bad-event fraction by the fraction the objective allows. At 99.9%, a 1.44% bad fraction is 14.4 times the allowed rate. One hour corresponds to approximately 2% of a 30-day request budget only under comparable, constant eligible-request volume. Actual contribution is bad events in the alert window divided by the allowed bad-event count for the SLO evaluation window; busy and quiet periods can differ from the time-based estimate. Require a long window and a short window to exceed the threshold so a resolved spike is less likely to page. Choose slower ticket rules and urgent page rules from the tolerated budget loss and response time. Missing metrics, zero traffic and sparse requests need their own policies; exhaustion does not by itself determine the release policy.

### Q9. What makes an alert worth paging a human? Defend your filter.

**Model answer:** A page should identify current or imminent user harm and an action that a responder can take within the required time. A nearly full disk can qualify when exhaustion is imminent and immediate action prevents failure; a slowly growing disk may be a ticket. Group related signals into one incident, attach a usable runbook and distinguish coverage failure from healthy traffic. Review actionable pages, repeated interruptions and missed incidents together so reducing noise does not hide harm. An interrupt budget is a team policy informed by staffing and workload, not a universal number.

### Q10. How do you capacity-plan a service you expect to 10x?

**Model answer:** Start with arrival rate, traffic mix, payload sizes, data growth, dependency limits and seasonal bursts. Measure where latency or errors rise as concurrency grows; the first limit might be the database pool, an external quota or queue age rather than CPU. Include a failed replica or region, scaling delay and recovery time in the headroom model. Load-test a representative topology with defined stop conditions, then state the forecast assumptions and earliest expected limit. Revisit the model as traffic and data change; tenfold growth is not evidence that a redesign is automatically required.

### Q11. How do you make deploys boring?

**Model answer:** Use small reviewable changes, reproducible builds, automated checks and progressive exposure with explicit comparison and stop criteria. A canary must receive enough representative traffic to detect the failure modes you care about. Feature flags help separate deployment from exposure, but disabling a flag does not undo data writes, queued work or schema changes. Test rollback compatibility and define forward repair where rollback cannot restore correctness. Track delivery and failure metrics with the team to diagnose bottlenecks, rather than treating one percentage threshold as proof of a testing defect.

### Q12. Your observability bill tripled in a quarter. What do you do?

**Model answer:** Attribute cost by signal, service, ingestion volume, label cardinality, retention and vendor pricing before choosing a remedy. Compare the increase with traffic growth and newly required evidence; a higher bill may buy useful coverage. Reduce unnecessary high-cardinality metric labels, tune trace sampling and log retention, and preserve the signals needed for incidents, audits and debugging. Replay important investigation queries against the proposed policy to check what evidence would disappear. Governance and procurement can both matter; verify the saving and coverage after the change.

### Q13. What makes a runbook something the on-call will actually use at 3am?

**Model answer:** Start with the page and user symptom, the service owner, prerequisites and read-only observations with expected outputs. Each mutation needs a bounded target, authority, preconditions, expected effect, stop rule and a way to verify recovery. For a database-pool page, inspect fleet-wide connections and long transactions before cancelling a specific query; do not terminate every idle connection. Include escalation and communication steps when the expected improvement does not occur. Exercise the procedure and record the environment in which commands were verified; syntax checks cannot prove operational safety.

### Q14. Multi-region failover: what do you actually test before you trust it?

**Model answer:** Define the recovery time objective (RTO: tolerated recovery time) and recovery point objective (RPO: tolerated data loss). Verify target-region capacity, dependencies, credentials, routing behavior and data freshness. Trace write ownership: fence the old writer before promotion so a network partition cannot leave two writers accepting changes. Measure cutover, restore or replication recovery, user correctness and the path back. Choose rehearsal scope and cadence from risk and maturity; a production test needs explicit authority and abort conditions, and a staging result has stated limits.

### Q15. How do you communicate an ongoing incident to executives?

**Model answer:** State confirmed user impact, scope, start time and what remains unknown. Summarize the current mitigation and the next decision in language that helps the audience act; avoid guessing revenue loss or a recovery time. Name the communication owner and next update time, including when there is no new result. Escalate concrete decisions such as whether to suspend an unsafe write path. Correct earlier statements when evidence changes and keep internal investigation detail separate from the approved external message.

### Q16. A postmortem action item keeps slipping for two quarters. What do you do?

**Model answer:** Find whether the item lacks ownership, capacity, a workable design or enough risk reduction to justify its cost. Split an oversized item into a useful bounded mitigation and a longer repair, each with a validation criterion. Bring the residual risk and competing work to the owner who can fund it. Record an explicit deferral, compensating control and review date if it will not ship. Repeatedly changing the due date without a decision hides risk; shipping an ineffective control just to close the ticket also fails.

### Q17. What belongs in an on-call handoff, and how does follow-the-sun change it?

**Model answer:** Transfer responsibility explicitly: the incoming responder acknowledges the current incident state, hypotheses, actions taken, stop conditions and next decision. Include recent risky changes, muted alerts with expiry, pending work, access needs and escalation contacts. A written record supports every rotation; a short overlap helps clarify active incidents. Follow-the-sun coverage requires available responders and tested handoffs, not just teams in different time zones. Confirm who owns the incident during gaps or holidays and how an unanswered escalation is routed.

### Q18. A data-corruption incident: how does the playbook differ from an availability incident?

**Model answer:** Contain the affected write or propagation path when doing so reduces harm, and preserve evidence before destructive repair. Determine the records, time window, consumers and backups affected; a healthy API does not prove correct data. Compare quarantine, forward repair and point-in-time recovery, including valid writes that a restore would discard. Validate a bounded repair on a copy or controlled slice before wider execution, then reconcile downstream systems and unknown outcomes. Track service recovery and data recovery separately, with required communication and follow-up owned explicitly.

### Q19. When does an ops incident become a security incident, and what changes?

**Model answer:** Escalate according to the security response policy when unauthorized access, exploitation or unexplained suspicious behavior is possible; proof of malicious intent is not required. Coordinate containment with evidence preservation, because restarts or deletion may destroy useful state. Limit access to sensitive investigation material while keeping responders informed enough to act. Security, operations, legal and communications roles follow the established authority model and applicable obligations. Rehearse the escalation path so uncertainty does not delay the right specialists.

### Q20. When is deleting a service the best reliability investment?

**Model answer:** Compare the service value and mandatory obligations with its recurring operational cost and risk. Inventory consumers, data-retention requirements, scheduled jobs and hidden dependencies before accepting low request volume as evidence of disuse. Offer a migration path, announce a sunset, meter use and verify that replacement behavior works. Disable a bounded part first where useful, then remove credentials, infrastructure and pages after the agreed recovery window. Keep required records; deletion is a managed change with owners and evidence, not merely switching off an expensive component.

### Q21. p99 doubled but the code did not change. Where do you look?

**Model answer:** Check the latency measurement, affected routes, traffic mix and timing first. Use traces and resource signals to distinguish dependency latency, database waits, connection queues, cache misses, garbage collection and shared-host contention. A dependency percentile is not automatically the end-to-end percentile: parallelism, call frequency and timeouts change the distribution. Compare affected and unaffected requests and test the strongest hypothesis with a bounded observation. Mitigate the confirmed constraint and verify user latency without hiding increased errors or stale responses.

### Q22. What is your production-access model, and why not standing access?

**Model answer:** Prefer least privilege, automated deployment identities and time-bounded human access appropriate to the task. Define separate ordinary operational and emergency access paths with authentication, scope, audit, expiry and review. Test that a responder can obtain necessary access during an identity-provider or approval-system failure. Temporary access limits the exposure window but cannot prevent misuse within the granted privileges. Choose approval and review rules from the data sensitivity and incident urgency rather than assuming every production-read session has the same risk.

### Q23. What does a weekly ops review actually review?

**Model answer:** Review user outcomes, budget consumption, incidents, interrupt load, recurring toil, capacity, cost and overdue risk-reduction work. Use trends and supporting examples rather than displaying every dashboard. Resolve concrete choices: fund an action, retire a noisy page, accept a bounded risk or change ownership. Give each decision an owner and follow-up criterion, and check previous decisions at the next review. Adjust cadence and participation to the operation; a weekly thirty-minute meeting is one workable format.

### Q24. How do you run a game day that is worth the money?

**Model answer:** Choose a scenario and measurable learning objective, such as detecting a database failure and restoring correct bookings within the agreed recovery target. Obtain authority, bound the environment and fault, and name a controller with stop and recovery procedures. Exercise the actual paging, roles, access and runbook path while protecting responders and users from unnecessary surprise. Improvisation can be sound judgment; capture why the written procedure was insufficient rather than assuming every deviation was wrong. Debrief observations, assign improvements and verify them in a later rehearsal.

### Q25. How do you raise the operational maturity of a team that ships fast but pages constantly?

**Model answer:** Review user harm, incidents, change failures and interrupt load with the team, then choose the highest-impact constraints. That might be alert quality, unsafe write recovery, missing capacity or insufficient staffing; use the evidence to set the order. Introduce a small measurable change, such as a compatible canary gate or a fixed recurring page, and verify the result. Support incident leadership practice, useful postmortems and funded follow-up work. Judge progress by user outcomes and sustainable workload as well as delivery speed, so fewer pages do not simply reflect suppressed monitoring.

- Cross-tenant denial tests and architecture checks can support readiness evidence. They are automated checks, not chaos experiments; explain the boundary each actually verifies. Cite your own contribution only when supported by your work records.

## Common Mistakes

- Reciting incident roles without explaining action preconditions, recovery checks and escalation.
- Treating SLOs as dashboards instead of governance wired to deploy policy.
- Describing chaos tooling instead of experiment design (hypothesis, steady state, abort conditions).

## Best Practices

- Explain the measured improvement and residual risk; use automation where the action and failure model are understood.
- State the scope, authority and stop condition for risky actions. Distinguish your experience from a proposed approach.

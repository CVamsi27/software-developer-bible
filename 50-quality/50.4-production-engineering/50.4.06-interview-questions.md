# Production Engineering Interview Questions


## Definition

This set probes whether a candidate has *operated* production, not just deployed to it: running an incident as a commander instead of a firefighter, deriving SLO policy from business consequences, designing experiments with bounded blast radius, and keeping the humans sustainable. Each answer names its tradeoff — the habit that separates operated experience from read experience.

## First Principles

This set probes incident leadership (→ 50.4.01) and the SLO-as-policy contract (→ 50.4.02).:

1. **The senior tells are trade-off statements**: "X when Y, Z when not-Y" — juniors memorize answers; seniors recite the *conditions* under which each answer is right.
2. **Every question here maps to an axiom chain, not a fact** — if you can derive the answer from the linked chapters, you can answer the variant questions too (that's the point of → First Principles sections).

### Q1. Production is down, three teams are involved. Walk me through your first fifteen minutes.

**Model answer:** "Declare first, diagnose second — a declared false alarm costs minutes, an undeclared real one costs the outage. Roles before work: incident commander (coordinates, does not type commands), operations lead (runs mitigations), communications lead (status page + stakeholders on a fixed cadence, say every 15 minutes — silence is how trust dies). Commander's first decisions: is customer impact confirmed (not just alerts), is there a safe rollback or feature flag to flip *before* root-causing, and what is the stop-the-line rule — anyone with evidence can call it. Mitigation before diagnosis, always: restart/rollback/flag now, root-cause in the postmortem, not on the bridge."

**Probe:** "What if the roll back might destroy state?" → "Then it is not a safe rollback — that judgment call goes to the commander with the state model in front of them, and the alternative is forward-fix or partial traffic cutover. The rule is not 'always roll back'; it is 'prefer known-safe mitigation'."

### Q2. What makes a postmortem blameless *and* useful? Most are neither.

**Model answer:** "Blameless is about systems: 'the deploy tool allowed this' not 'Bob deployed this' — but useful means specific: exact timeline with clock times, the detection gap (time-to-detect vs time-to-mitigate), and the contributing factors ranked by leverage. The part most orgs skip: action items with owners and dates, each tagged preventive vs mitigative, and a standing review of whether *last quarter's* action items shipped. A postmortem without closed action items is a confession, not a control. And one senior habit: ask 'what would have caught this automatically?' — every incident should retire a class of incident, not an instance."

### Q3. Explain error budgets to a product manager who wants 99.99% for everything.

**Model answer:** "An SLO is a product decision wearing math. 99.9% on a service means 43 minutes of budget a month — the budget is what lets you ship: deploys, experiments, and maintenance all spend it. I derive the number from consequence: what does one minute of downtime cost in revenue, churn, and regulatory exposure, per service tier? That usually yields 99.9% for internal tools, 99.95% for the checkout path, and *not* 99.99% anywhere unless a contract literally sells it — because each nine costs roughly 10x the engineering. The policy the math buys: budget burned above threshold freezes feature deploys in favor of reliability work, automatically. That converts an SLO from a dashboard into governance."

**Probe:** "What's the failure mode of SLO adoption?" → "Symptom-SLOs nobody acts on — ten dashboards, zero policy. Start with one user-journey SLO wired to one consequence."

### Q4. How do you run a chaos experiment without causing an outage?

**Model answer:** "A chaos experiment is a hypothesis test, not a sabotage: 'if Redis dies, the serving path degrades to DB reads within p99 budget' — with a steady-state metric defined *before* injecting anything. Blast radius bounded in advance: one instance, one availability zone, one percentage of traffic, with an abort condition (error rate or latency threshold trips → automatic rollback of the experiment). Scope by maturity: start in staging against production-like topology, then production with feature-flagged traffic slicing. The experiment that matters most is the boring one — kill a dependency during peak and watch whether the *on-call runbook* actually works, because the runbook is part of the system."

### Q5. A team wants to launch a new service. What does your production readiness review actually check?

**Model answer:** "Fewer checkboxes than most PRRs — the ones that predict incidents. Does it have an SLO and a dashboard tied to it? Are alerts actionable (each one has a runbook entry and a human action, or it is deleted)? Does it degrade gracefully — what happens to it and to its callers when each dependency is down? Rollback story tested, not documented — actually exercised in staging. Capacity: known limits and a load test at 2x projected peak. Security basics: secrets in a manager, authn/authz on every endpoint, and the data-classification answer. And ownership: a named team, an on-call rota, and a runbook — an unowned service is an incident on a timer. PRR exists to move launch-blocking conversations *before* launch, not to gate with paperwork."

### Q6. Your best engineer is burning out on call. What do you change?

**Model answer:** "First treat it as an alerting problem, not a personal-resilience problem — burnout on call is almost always alert quality. Pull the last 60 days of pages: anything under ~50% actionable means the noisy alerts are consuming the human. Fix by deleting or auto-ticketing non-actionable alerts, page-only-actionable policy, and make primary/secondary rotations with real follow-the-sun handoffs if the team spans timezones. Structural levers: scheduled comp-time after bad nights, a hard rule that postmortem action items from pages land within the sprint (fatigue has a cause, and it is usually the same root cause as the pages), and rotation sizing so primary duty lands ~1 week in 6–8, not 1 in 3. The on-call health metric I watch: pages per shift and sleep-interruption count — trending up is a staffing bug."

### Q7. What is toil, how do you measure it, and when should you refuse to automate it?

**Model answer:** "Toil is operational work that is manual, repetitive, automatable, tactical, and scales with service growth — the definition matters because each adjective is a test. Measure it honestly: a toil tag on tickets and pages, then a monthly toil percentage against engineering time. The budget line I run: toil above ~half an engineer's capacity means the team cannot do project work, and it is a *staffing signal* the org has to see, not a private shame. Prioritization is frequency × manual cost — automate the daily fifteen-minute checklist before the yearly two-hour ritual. The refusal case is the senior tell: rare, high-variance, judgment-heavy work (a novel data-corruption triage) should stay manual — automating it snapshots today's understanding of a situation that will not repeat, and the script rots into false confidence."

### Q8. Design alerting for an SLO. Why multi-window burn rates instead of threshold alerts?

**Model answer:** "Burn rate is how fast you consume error budget relative to plan — a static threshold like 'error rate > 1%' pages too late for slow leaks and too early for spikes inside budget. The standard scheme pairs windows: a fast window (5m/1h at 14x burn) catches sudden damage and pages; a slow window (30m/6h or 6h/3d at 6x/1x) catches steady bleed and tickets — because something that exhausts the budget in three days does not need a human at 3am, it needs a human *today*. Both windows must burn simultaneously to fire, which suppresses blips. The policy wiring matters as much as the math: fast-burn pages tie to the on-call, slow-burn opens work with a deadline, and budget exhaustion freezes feature deploys — otherwise the alerts are decoration."

### Q9. What makes an alert worth paging a human? Defend your filter.

**Model answer:** "Two conditions, both required: it indicates user-impacting symptoms or imminent impact, and a human can act on it *now* in a way that changes the outcome. Everything else demotes: cause-based alerts (disk 80%) become tickets or auto-remediation, and informational alerts get deleted, because every unmute-able page trains the on-call to ignore pages — alert fatigue is a system design failure, not a personal resilience failure. I set an alert budget: more than ~two actionable pages per shift sustained means either alerting quality or staffing is broken, and I review pages per shift monthly with the team. The senior tell is deleting alerts in the postmortem — the incident that paged ten times before anyone looked should end with fewer alert rules than it started with."

### Q10. How do you capacity-plan a service you expect to 10x?

**Model answer:** "From the demand curve, not the peak number. Measure the real traffic shape — daily and weekly seasonality, growth trend, and burstiness — then set the headroom policy explicitly: I run at N+1 redundancy with peak utilization targets per layer (CPU ~40-50% for spiky frontends, higher for batch), because autoscaling lag is where incidents are born. Load-test to 2x projected peak *at production-like topology* — the test that finds the connection-pool ceiling, not the CPU ceiling. Autoscaling gets audited for cold-start time and scale-in aggressiveness (scale-in after a spike has caused more outages than scale-out latency). And the growth answers are staged: what holds at 10x is usually a different architecture, so capacity planning feeds the roadmap — 'this design tops out at month N' is a planning deliverable, not a failure."

### Q11. How do you make deploys boring?

**Model answer:** "Boring is a system property, built in layers. Small, frequent diffs — the single biggest predictor of safe deploys, because review and rollback surface scale with diff size. Progressive delivery: canary on a traffic slice with automated SLO comparison and auto-rollback on burn, so the human is a witness, not the sensor. Feature flags decouple deploy from release — the deploy happens constantly, the *change* ships when ready and rolls back instantly — but flags carry their own discipline: naming owners, expiry dates, and a cleanup SLA, because flag debt is deploy risk you shipped on purpose. And measure the pipeline itself: lead time and change-failure rate (DORA) reviewed monthly — a team whose change-failure rate exceeds ~15% does not have a deploy problem, it has a testing or design problem wearing a deploy costume."

### Q12. Your observability bill tripled in a quarter. What do you do?

**Model answer:** "Treat it as a data-governance incident, not a procurement problem. First attribute: per-team, per-signal breakdown — the bill usually concentrates in high-cardinality dimensions someone added for debugging and never removed (user-id on every span, request-id as an index). The fixes in order of leverage: cardinality budgets per team enforced at instrumentation review; sampling policies tuned by signal class (trace tail-sampling, log level discipline — debug logs to short retention or not at all); and retention tiering, because keeping a year of raw logs is rarely an analytical decision, it is a default nobody revisited. The cultural fix is the durable one: dashboards and alerts must justify their data — an alert nobody looks at is deleted, and its cardinality goes with it. I have never seen this problem where the answer was 'buy less tooling' rather than 'govern what you send.'"

### Q13. What makes a runbook something the on-call will actually use at 3am?

**Model answer:** "Written for a degraded brain: symptom-first ('pages as: HighOrderErrorRate — customers see checkout failures'), not cause-first; each alert links to its runbook mechanically, because a runbook you must search for does not exist; steps as copy-paste commands with expected output, so sleep-deprived execution can verify progress; and an explicit escalation point — 'if step 3 did not reduce the error rate in 5 minutes, escalate to the service owner and consider rollback' — because the runbook's real job is bounding how long someone struggles alone. The discipline that keeps it alive: every game day exercises a runbook, every incident updates the runbooks it used, and CI checks links and command syntax. An untested runbook is documentation-shaped hope."

### Q14. Multi-region failover: what do you actually test before you trust it?

**Model answer:** "The unglamorous prerequisites first: is the data layer truly replicated to the second region with a documented RPO, and does the failover *ordering* handle writes — a region you cannot write to is not a failover target, it is a read replica with ambition. Then rehearse the real paths, on a schedule: DNS/steering cutover timing (TTLs lie during incidents), state-store promotion, dependency behavior in the second region (licenses, third-party allowlists, and region-scoped services are the classic silent blockers), and capacity — the surviving region sized for full load, not fair-weather load. The test that matters is the *rehearsal itself*: run failover quarterly in production with real traffic, because a failover plan exercised only in staging is a hypothesis, and the day you need it is a terrible day to run the first experiment."

### Q15. How do you communicate an ongoing incident to executives?

**Model answer:** "Impact first, in their language: what customers experience, what revenue/regulatory exposure is, what is *known* vs suspected. Then the cadence promise — 'next update in 20 minutes even if nothing changed' — because silence is what erodes trust, not the outage. ETA discipline: no ETA when you do not have one; 'restoring service is the priority, root cause after' is a complete sentence, and a wrong ETA costs more credibility than no ETA. The asks go upward as decisions, not requests for help: 'approve customer credits above X' or 'choose between degraded feature Y and full suspension.' And one voice — the incident commander or comms lead owns executive comms, because three engineers improvising status updates to three execs produces three versions of the truth and a meeting about the meeting."

### Q16. A postmortem action item keeps slipping for two quarters. What do you do?

**Model answer:** "First diagnose *why* it slips: too big (decompose into a shipped-today mitigation + the structural fix), unowned (ownership means a team, not a name), or genuinely low priority — and if it is the third, that is a governance decision to make explicitly, not a silent rot. The mechanisms that work: risk-reduction per effort ranking in the ops review, where slipping items compete for capacity *visibly*; a policy floor for the top class — 'any item addressing a Sev-1 class ships within the quarter, or leadership accepts the risk in writing'; and celebrating the boring mitigations, because the guardrail that reduces recurrence is usually unglamorous. What I refuse is the theater of re-dating the same ticket monthly — an action item that slips twice without an explicit risk acceptance is evidence the postmortem process is decoration."

### Q17. What belongs in an on-call handoff, and how does follow-the-sun change it?

**Model answer:** "A written handoff regardless of time zones: open incidents with current state and next steps, risky changes deployed in the last 24h (the ones likely to page tonight), quiet-but-suspicious signals (that flapping alert someone muted), and pending follow-ups with deadlines. The cultural rule underneath: 'no news is not good news' — handoff is a transfer of *responsibility*, and the outgoing on-call owes a truthful summary of the system's mood, not a clean conscience. Follow-the-sun changes the mechanics: async written handoffs with overlap time, because a 15-minute call across a 9-hour gap skips the team that is waking up; timezone fairness in rotation design (the same region should not permanently own the night); and escalation paths that know the sun position — every escalation needs a 'who is awake now' answer pre-computed."

### Q18. A data-corruption incident: how does the playbook differ from an availability incident?

**Model answer:** "Availability incidents end when traffic is restored; corruption incidents *begin* when you stop writing — the first move is containment: stop or gate the writes, because every minute propagates the damage further and snapshots taken later capture the corruption. Then scope it: what data, what time window, what downstream consumers copied it — corruption that propagated to an analytics warehouse or a cache layer needs its own cleanup plan, and the scope decision determines whether you repair forward, restore from PITR, or quarantine. Restore is a rehearsal question: an untested backup is Schrödinger's backup, so restore drills with measured RTO are a standing control, not a response. Severity scale differs too — a corruption incident can be 'resolved' (service healthy) while its real work (data repair, customer notification, downstream reconciliation) runs for weeks. That asymmetry is why it gets its own command structure, not the availability playbook with different words."

### Q19. When does an ops incident become a security incident, and what changes?

**Model answer:** "At the first signal of intent: unexplained access, an exploit path in the timeline, data exfiltration indicators — or plain uncertainty about whether the anomaly is malicious. From that moment the playbook changes in three ways. Evidence: preserve logs and state before remediation destroys them — the restart that fixes an outage can delete the proof. Comms: restrict the incident channel and status-page language, because public 'we were breached' phrasing has legal and regulatory weight and tipping off an active attacker extends the incident — security owns external wording. Authority: a joint command where security leads containment decisions that ops would normally make unilaterally (isolate the host vs keep serving). The operational discipline that makes this work is pre-agreed: a paging path from ops to security that fires on uncertainty, exercised in a joint game day — because inventing the collaboration during the incident guarantees it arrives late."

### Q20. When is deleting a service the best reliability investment?

**Model answer:** "When its expected incident cost exceeds its feature value — and the honest accounting includes everything: pages it causes, dependency upgrades it forces, onboarding time it confuses, capacity it idles. The signals that a deletion review is due: a service nobody can explain the boundaries of, a service whose only consumers are two calls a day, a migration that left both old and new running 'for safety' six quarters ago. Complexity has a maintenance floor that never sleeps — every service adds pagers, CVE patching, and design-doc drift regardless of traffic. The mechanics: a quarterly 'delete candidate' review in ops readouts, deprecation with real sunset dates and consumer deadlines (mirrored from the message-broker playbook — deprecate loudly, meter usage, pull the plug), and a norm that *not* building the service is celebrated the same way shipping one is. The best service is the one you never wrote; the second best is the one you turned off."

### Q21. p99 doubled but the code did not change. Where do you look?

**Model answer:** "In order of probability. The dependency matrix first: which external calls moved — a third-party API's p99 is your p99 unless you bounded it, and 'not my code' does not mean 'not my SLO'; the fix is ownership-level: timeouts, circuit breakers, and stale/degraded fallbacks so their regression is your degraded mode, not your outage. Then shared-infrastructure neighbors: noisy-neighbor on a shared database or node pool, a co-tenant batch job, GC pressure from an unrelated deploy on the same hosts — per-node resource telemetry versus per-request traces separates these fast. Then scale effects without code change: traffic mix shifted (a slow endpoint that 2% of users hit is now 15%), data grew past an index's usefulness, or the cache hit ratio dropped — request *composition* changes look like performance regressions from the outside. The discipline: attribute with data before you contact anyone, because 'your API got slow' with percentile evidence starts a collaboration, and without it starts a argument."

### Q22. What is your production-access model, and why not standing access?

**Model answer:** "Zero standing access to production data; changes flow through pipelines, and humans get time-boxed, purpose-bound sessions through a break-glass flow: request with justification, approval or auto-grant by policy, full session audit logging, automatic expiry, and post-hoc review of every use. The reasoning is failure-mode analysis: standing credentials mean a phished laptop is a breach, an insider having a bad week is unbounded, and audits reconstruct intent from tribal memory. Break-glass converts all three into controlled, logged, reviewed events at the cost of a few minutes of latency — which is exactly the right trade for production data. The model only works if the friction is honest: break-glass that requires three approvals for a 2am page will be bypassed, so page-response access is pre-authorized by policy, and everything else waits for morning. Access review cadence closes the loop: quarterly, automated, with anomalies (access without a ticket, sessions outside patterns) actually investigated."

### Q23. What does a weekly ops review actually review?

**Model answer:** "The health of the operation, in front of the people who fund it: every page from the week with its actionable rate and whether the runbook held; SLO status per service — budget burned, and any service trending toward exhaustion gets a named plan; toil percentage and its trend; action-item aging with the slipping ones surfaced (that is where Q16's policy floor lives); and cost anomalies, because reliability and unit cost share telemetry. The anti-patterns are what make the meeting fail: reliability theater — dashboards of green that no one interrogates — and blame archaeology, re-litigating incidents that already produced action items. The output that matters is decisions: alert deletions, capacity asks, risk acceptances in writing, and the occasional 'this service needs a team' escalation. Thirty minutes, weekly, same agenda — the cadence is the feature, because it converts operational data into governance before it becomes a quarterly surprise."

### Q24. How do you run a game day that is worth the money?

**Model answer:** "A game day rehearses the *people*, which is what distinguishes it from a chaos experiment that tests the system. Design: a scenario with a hypothesis — 'the team can detect and mitigate a primary-database failover inside the RTO' — a time-boxed window in a real environment (staging first, production for mature teams, off-peak but representative traffic), and a designated controller who injects faults and can abort. Real roles: actual on-call gets paged, actual commander runs the bridge, runbooks are what they would reach for — the controller silently notes where people improvised, because every improvisation is a missing runbook. The debrief within the hour: what worked, what was improvised, what the *system* should learn — runbook fixes, alert gaps, permission holes — each with an owner and a date. Then the follow-through rule that gives the day its value: the next game day opens by testing whether last quarter's fixes landed."

### Q25. How do you raise the operational maturity of a team that ships fast but pages constantly?

**Model answer:** "Start with the data, not the sermon: pull their last quarter of pages, SLO burn, and change-failure rate and review it *with* them — maturity work lands when the team concludes it needs it, not when a platform team mandates it. Then sequence by leverage: first alert quality (delete non-actionable pages — instant on-call relief that buys goodwill), then the SLO contract for their top user journey (governance beats vigilance), then progressive delivery on their riskiest service (canary + auto-rollback), and only then ceremony like ops reviews. Culture mechanisms alongside: rotating incident-commander role so everyone leads once with support, blameless postmortems where the reviewer checks that action items name systems not people, and celebrating the boring catches — the canary rollback nobody heard about is the program working. The metric of success is on the on-call's phone: pages per shift trending down while deploys trend up. Maturity is not more process; it is the minimum process that makes speed sustainable."

## Common Mistakes

- Reciting incident command roles without the stop-the-line and mitigation-before-diagnosis judgments.
- Treating SLOs as dashboards instead of governance wired to deploy policy.
- Describing chaos tooling instead of experiment design (hypothesis, steady state, abort conditions).

## Best Practices

- Every incident answer ends with what got *automatically* better — the class retired, not the instance fixed.
- Speak in budgets and blast radii; heroics read junior at this level.

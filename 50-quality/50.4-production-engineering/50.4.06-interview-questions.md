# Production Engineering Interview Questions

> **TL;DR:** Senior-level production-engineering questions with model answers and probes — incident command, SLO math, chaos experiments, readiness reviews, and on-call health. The senior signal is speaking in error budgets and blast radii, not in heroics.

## Definition

This set probes whether a candidate has *operated* production, not just deployed to it: running an incident as a commander instead of a firefighter, deriving SLO policy from business consequences, designing experiments with bounded blast radius, and keeping the humans sustainable. Each answer names its tradeoff — the habit that separates operated experience from read experience.

### Q1. Production is down, three teams are involved. Walk me through your first fifteen minutes.

**Model answer:** "Declare first, diagnose second — a declared false alarm costs minutes, an undeclared real one costs the outage. Roles before work: incident commander (coordinates, does not type commands), operations lead (runs mitigations), communications lead (status page + stakeholders on a fixed cadence, say every 15 minutes — silence is how trust dies). Commander's first decisions: is customer impact confirmed (not just alerts), is there a safe rollback or feature flag to flip *before* root-causing, and what is the stop-the-line rule — anyone with evidence can call it. Mitigation before diagnosis, always: restart/rollback/flag now, root-cause in the postmortem, not on the bridge."

**Probe:** "What if the roll back might destroy state?" → "Then it is not a safe rollback — that judgment call goes to the commander with the state model in front of them, and the alternative is forward-fix or partial traffic cutover. The rule is not 'always roll back'; it is 'prefer known-safe mitigation'."

### Q2. What makes a postmortem blameless *and* useful? Most are neither.

**Model answer:** "Blameless is about systems: 'the deploy tool allowed this' not 'Bob deployed this' — but useful means specific: exact timeline with clock times, the detection gap (time-to-detect vs time-to-mitigate), and the contributing factors ranked by leverage. The part most orgs skip: action items with owners and dates, each tagged preventive vs mitigative, and a standing review of whether *last quarter's* action items shipped. A postmortem without closed action items is a confession, not a control. And one senior habit: ask 'what would have caught this automatically?' — every incident should retire a class of incident, not an instance."

### Q3. Explain error budgets to a product manager who wants 99.99% for everything.

**Model answer:** "An SLO is a product decision wearing math. 99.9% on a service means 43 minutes of budget a month — the budget is what lets you ship: deploys, experiments, and maintenance all spend it. I derive the number from consequence: what does one minute of downtime cost in revenue, churn, and regulatory exposure, per service tier? That usually yields 99.9% for internal tools, 99.95% for the checkout path, and *not* 99.99% anywhere unless a contract literally sells it — because each nine costs roughly 10x the engineering. The policy the math buys: budget burned above threshold freezes feature deploys in favor of reliability work, automatically. That converts an SLO from a dashboard into governance."

**Probe:** "What's the failure mode of SLO adoption?" → "Symptom-SLOs nobody acts on — ten dashboards, zero policy. Start with one user-journey SLO wired to one consequence."

### Q4. How do you run a chaos experiment without causing an outage?

**Model answer:** "A chaos experiment is a hypothesis test, not a sabotage: 'if Redis dies, the serving path degrades to DB reads within p99 budget' — with a steady-state metric defined *before* injecting anything. Blast radius bounded in advance: one instance, one availability zone, one percentage of traffic, with an abort condition (error rate or latency threshold trips → automatic rollback of the experiment). Scope by maturity: start in staging against production-like topology, then production with feature-flagged traffic slicing. The experiment that matters most is the boring one — kill a dependency during peak and watch whether the *on-call runbook* actually works, because the runbook is part of the system."

### Q5. A team wants to launch a new service. What does your production readiness review actually check?

**Model answer:** "Fewer checkboxes than most PRRs — the ones that predict incidents. Does it have an SLO and a dashboard tied to it? Are alerts actionable (each one has a runbook entry and a human action, or it is deleted)? Does it degrade gracefully — what happens to it and to its callers when each dependency is down? Rollback story tested, not documented — actually exercised in staging. Capacity: known limits and a load test at 2x projected peak. Security basics: secrets in a manager, authn/authz on every endpoint, and the data-classification answer. And ownership: a named team, an on-call rota, and a runbook — an unowned service is an incident on a timer. PRR exists to move launch-blocking conversations *before* launch, not to gate with paperwork."

### Q6. Your best engineer is burning out on call. What do you change?

**Model answer:** "First treat it as an alerting problem, not a personal-resilience problem — burnout on call is almost always alert quality. Pull the last 60 days of pages: anything under ~50% actionable means the noisy alerts are consuming the human. Fix by deleting or auto-ticketing non-actionable alerts, page-only-actionable policy, and make primary/secondary rotations with real follow-the-sun handoffs if the team spans timezones. Structural levers: scheduled comp-time after bad nights, a hard rule that postmortem action items from pages land within the sprint (fatigue has a cause, and it is usually the same root cause as the pages), and rotation sizing so primary duty lands ~1 week in 6–8, not 1 in 3. The on-call health metric I watch: pages per shift and sleep-interruption count — trending up is a staffing bug."

## Common Mistakes

- Reciting incident command roles without the stop-the-line and mitigation-before-diagnosis judgments.
- Treating SLOs as dashboards instead of governance wired to deploy policy.
- Describing chaos tooling instead of experiment design (hypothesis, steady state, abort conditions).

## Best Practices

- Every incident answer ends with what got *automatically* better — the class retired, not the instance fixed.
- Speak in budgets and blast radii; heroics read junior at this level.

## Summary

Six questions covering incident command, postmortem discipline, SLO governance, chaos experiment design, readiness gates, and on-call sustainability — the operational maturity layer that most senior loops test explicitly.

| Question | The one-liner that scores |
| -------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| First 15 min | Declare → roles → mitigate → communicate on cadence; root-cause later |
| Postmortem | Systems-not-blame + timeline + action items with owners; retire a class |

| Chaos | Hypothesis + steady state + bounded radius + abort condition |

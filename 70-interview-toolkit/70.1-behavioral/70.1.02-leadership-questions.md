# Leadership Questions: Decisions, Influence & Team Outcomes

Two engineers leave days before a release. The senior response may be to reduce scope and redistribute ownership — not simply take on more work yourself.

```text
constraint → options → your action + collaborators → observed result → lesson
```

## Definition

**Leadership questions** ask how your decisions and actions changed a team's ability to work. The evidence can come from an individual contributor, mentor, technical lead or manager — state the authority you actually had.

> **Textbook Definition:** Engineering leadership influences technical direction and coordinated execution through decisions, communication and support for others, whether or not the engineer holds formal management authority.

## First Principles

A team outcome has many contributors. To assess your judgment, a reader needs the constraint, your specific action, and what happened next. A title or a polished story cannot supply that causal chain.

## Why It Matters

Leading work means making trade-offs with people whose constraints differ from yours. These prompts help you choose real examples of that work. Employer rubrics and time limits vary; this chapter does not prescribe a pass score. Losing two engineers, for example, does not automatically justify taking all hard work yourself — reducing release scope, sharing knowledge and assigning a decision owner may address the bottleneck more directly.

## How It Works

The situations below are fictional illustrations, not experiences to claim as your own. Each card shows a useful answer direction and the evidence needed to finish it. Use the [STAR method](../../00-strategy/00.03-star-method.md) to expand a matching experience from your own work.

### 1. Lead Through Difficulty and Hard Decisions

- **Lead through a difficult situation.** Two departures leave a release short of experienced engineers. Identify the critical release slice, distribute work by skill, and onboard help with a paired first task. Name what shipped, what moved, and how knowledge was shared afterward — never make overtime the recovery plan.
- **Make an unpopular decision.** The team prefers its internal tool; leadership wants a standard vendor. Compare maintenance burden, missing features, migration risk and exit costs. Record concerns, then execute the authorized choice with clear safeguards, separating your recommendation from the final decision.
- **Manage conflicting stakeholders.** A migration date, a sales event and risk concerns collide. Find the constraint behind each request and propose milestones with owners, rollback conditions and explicit deferred scope. Say which commitment was renegotiated — a four-month plan does not literally meet an unchanged three-month deadline.
- **Explain your decision-making approach.** When asked about your process rather than one event, describe problem, constraints, options, criteria, owner and review trigger — then anchor the process in one actual decision. Never turn a framework into a fictional STAR event.

### 2. Mentor People and Handle Underperformance

- **Mentor a junior developer.** A capable colleague repeatedly asks for approval on small decisions. Agree on a learning goal, ask for their proposed reasoning, pair on a difficult case, then gradually transfer a bounded area of ownership. Show growing independence and the mentee's feedback while crediting their work.
- **Address underperformance.** Missed commitments and review problems have increased. Describe observed work privately, ask about blockers, and agree on support and checkpoints within your authority. Involve the manager or HR for formal employment processes, protect personal details, and distinguish a mentor's support from a manager's performance-management authority.

### 3. Push Back and Balance Competing Needs

- **Push back on requirements.** Simultaneous document editing is requested on a short deadline. Expose conflict resolution, presence, persistence and reconnect requirements. Compare an existing collaboration service with a smaller sequential-editing release, and verify the smaller slice still solved the immediate problem.
- **Balance business and technical needs.** A customer asks for stronger availability commitments. Define the service indicator and measurement window, examine failures, and cost options against expected margin, ongoing operation and delivery risk. A revenue-to-cost ratio is not ROI, and measured availability differs from an SLA promise.
- **Choose between competing priorities.** Security remediation, a customer feature and cost reduction compete for capacity. Identify mandatory constraints first, compare remaining value and effort, and present the cost of deferral without hiding uncertainty. A priority matrix supports judgment; it does not prove the best decision after the fact.

### 4. Handle Incidents and Deliver Bad News

- **Handle a production incident.** A newly released analytics query saturates the database. Coordinate investigation and communication; disable the feature if safe; verify customer recovery before changing indexes. State impact duration with supporting measurements, and track a query-review or load-test action to completion.
- **Improve reliability.** Recurring deployment, dependency and capacity failures affect customers. Group incidents by impact, test bounded timeouts and rollback, and rehearse the largest failure mode before expanding the solution. State the customer-facing indicator, denominator, period and follow-up actions rather than listing reliability tools.
- **Deliver bad news.** A core dependency cannot handle the required workload. Inform the decision owner promptly with evidence, options, cost of delay and a recommendation. Own the missed validation, reduce uncertainty with a spike, and explain the revised scope or date plus how stakeholders used the warning.

### 5. Improve Process, Debt and Delivery

- **Improve team processes.** Cross-team dependencies repeatedly make delivery forecasts unreliable. Map dependency owners and readiness conditions, then compare cycle-time distributions for similar work. Pilot a change before imposing it on every team — and if story points are retired, report cycle time and forecast accuracy rather than an incomparable "velocity improvement."
- **Deliver under a tight deadline.** A data-export audit capability has a fixed delivery date. Confirm the required evidence with its owner, prioritizing event identity, actor, scope, outcome, access controls and retention before a polished dashboard. Show which acceptance conditions passed and which work was deferred; a logging table alone does not establish compliance.
- **Handle technical debt.** An obsolete ORM and manual deployment steps create repeated operational cost. Rank debt by measured failure or delay, isolate the ORM behind an adapter, and automate one release step at a time. Compare incident burden and release effort over a stated period — capacity allocation is a team choice, not a universal percentage.
- **Introduce a new practice.** Cross-browser manual checks consume review time. Pilot Playwright on one critical flow across Chromium, Firefox and WebKit, retaining real-device or Safari checks where needed. Compare equivalent coverage and report failures found as well as runtime; WebKit testing does not itself mean testing branded Safari.
- **Learn something new quickly.** The team inherits Rust services while mainly knowing Node.js. Pair with an experienced maintainer, trace ownership and error handling in one request, and start with a reviewed low-risk change. Name the work you became able to do and the areas still requiring review.

### 6. Collaborate and Resolve Conflict Across Teams

- **Influence without authority.** Critical frontend flows have escaped the existing test suite. Link actual incidents to missing checks, build one useful regression test, and invite peers to extend it with a named owner. Report bugs reproduced and maintenance effort — a test's existence alone does not prove it would prevent every incident.
- **Collaborate across teams.** Web and mobile need a shared login experience but have different release schedules. Agree on identity contracts and fixtures, then use OpenID Connect for authentication and authorization-code flow with PKCE where appropriate. Stage each client's migration and explain compatibility and account-linking behavior.
- **Resolve a technical conflict.** Two engineers disagree on REST versus GraphQL. Agree on consumer needs, caching, schema evolution, authorization and operating cost before testing options, and make the decision owner explicit. Distinguish TypeScript's compile-time checks from runtime API validation.

## Real-World Use Cases

Choose experiences that cover delivery, mentoring, disagreement, technical judgment and failure. One experience may serve several prompts, but change the emphasis to answer the question actually asked. Keep a short opening and retain the detailed evidence for follow-up. Use numbers when you have them, with a period and denominator — otherwise describe observable evidence such as a completed migration, an accepted design change, or independent ownership of a task. Label estimates and credit collaborators. [Playwright browser engines and branded browsers](https://playwright.dev/docs/browsers) and [how OpenID Connect works](https://openid.net/developers/how-connect-works/) ground two of the cards above; verify current details against those references before citing them.

## Summary

An effective leadership answer connects a team constraint to your action and an observed outcome. Describe the authority you had, the trade-off you accepted, the people who contributed, and what you changed after learning from the result.

# Leadership Questions: Decisions, Influence & Team Outcomes

Two engineers leave days before a release. The senior response may be to reduce scope and redistribute ownership, not simply take on more work yourself.

## Definition

Leadership questions ask how your decisions and actions changed a team’s ability to work. The evidence can come from an individual contributor, mentor, technical lead, or manager; state the authority you actually had.

> **Textbook Definition:** Engineering leadership influences technical direction and coordinated execution through decisions, communication and support for others, whether or not the engineer holds formal management authority.

## First Principles

A team outcome has many contributors. To assess your judgment, a reader needs the constraint, your specific action, and what happened next. A title or a polished story cannot supply that causal chain.

## Why it matters

Leading work involves making trade-offs with people whose constraints differ from yours. These prompts help you choose real examples of that work. Employer rubrics and time limits vary; this chapter does not prescribe a pass score.

## From a team problem to your contribution

Start with the change the team needed, then trace your role:

```text
constraint → options → your action + collaborators → observed result → lesson
```

For example, losing two engineers does not automatically justify taking all hard work yourself. Reducing release scope, sharing knowledge, and assigning a decision owner may address the bottleneck more directly.

## Questions

The situations below are fictional illustrations, not experiences to claim as your own. Each card shows a useful answer direction and the evidence needed to finish it. Use the [STAR method](../../00-strategy/00.03-star-method.md) to expand a matching experience from your own work.

### 1. Lead through a difficult situation

**Situation and task:** Two departures leave a release short of experienced engineers.

**Action to explain:** Identify the critical release slice, distribute work by skill, and onboard help with a paired first task.

**Result and limit:** Name what shipped, what moved, and how knowledge was shared afterward; do not make overtime the recovery plan.

### 2. Make an unpopular decision

**Situation and task:** The team prefers its internal tool; leadership wants a standard vendor.

**Action to explain:** Compare maintenance burden, missing features, migration risk, and exit costs. Record concerns, then execute the authorized choice with clear safeguards.

**Result and limit:** Separate your recommendation from the final decision and report the actual migration outcome.

### 3. Mentor a junior developer

**Situation and task:** A capable colleague repeatedly asks for approval on small decisions.

**Action to explain:** Agree on a learning goal, ask for their proposed reasoning, pair on a difficult case, then gradually transfer a bounded area of ownership.

**Result and limit:** Use evidence of growing independence and the mentee’s feedback, while crediting their work.

### 4. Push back on requirements

**Situation and task:** Simultaneous document editing is requested on a short deadline.

**Action to explain:** Expose conflict resolution, presence, persistence, and reconnect requirements. Compare an existing collaboration service with a smaller sequential-editing release.

**Result and limit:** Explain the deferred capability and how you verified that the smaller slice still solved the immediate problem.

### 5. Handle a production incident

**Situation and task:** A newly released analytics query saturates the database.

**Action to explain:** Coordinate investigation and communication; disable the feature if safe; verify customer recovery before changing indexes.

**Result and limit:** State impact duration and what measurements support recovery. Track a query-review or load-test action to completion.

### 6. Improve team processes

**Situation and task:** Cross-team dependencies repeatedly make delivery forecasts unreliable.

**Action to explain:** Map dependency owners and readiness conditions, then compare cycle-time distributions for similar work. Pilot a change before imposing it on every team.

**Result and limit:** If story points are retired, report cycle time and forecast accuracy rather than an incomparable “velocity improvement.”

### 7. Influence without authority

**Situation and task:** Critical frontend flows have escaped the existing test suite.

**Action to explain:** Link actual incidents to missing checks, build one useful regression test, and invite peers to extend it with a named owner.

**Result and limit:** Report bugs reproduced and maintenance effort; a test’s existence alone does not prove it would prevent every incident.

### 8. Deliver under a tight deadline

**Situation and task:** A data-export audit capability has a fixed delivery date.

**Action to explain:** Confirm the required evidence with its owner. Prioritize event identity, actor, scope, outcome, access controls, and retention before a polished dashboard.

**Result and limit:** Show which acceptance conditions passed and which work was deferred; do not claim that a logging table alone establishes compliance.

### 9. Handle technical debt

**Situation and task:** An obsolete ORM and manual deployment steps create repeated operational cost.

**Action to explain:** Rank debt by measured failure or delay, isolate the ORM behind an adapter, and automate one release step at a time.

**Result and limit:** Compare incident burden and release effort over a stated period. Capacity allocation is a team choice, not a universal percentage.

### 10. Learn something new quickly

**Situation and task:** The team inherits Rust services while mainly knowing Node.js.

**Action to explain:** Pair with an experienced maintainer, trace ownership and error handling in one request, and start with a reviewed low-risk change.

**Result and limit:** Name the work you became able to do and the areas that still required review; quick onboarding is not instant expertise.

### 11. Collaborate across teams

**Situation and task:** Web and mobile need a shared login experience but have different release schedules.

**Action to explain:** Agree on identity contracts and fixtures, then use OpenID Connect for authentication and authorization-code flow with PKCE where appropriate. Stage each client’s migration.

**Result and limit:** Explain compatibility and account-linking behavior, not just the shared endpoint. OAuth authorization alone is not an authentication protocol.

### 12. Address underperformance

**Situation and task:** Missed commitments and review problems have increased.

**Action to explain:** Describe observed work privately, ask about blockers, and agree on support and checkpoints within your authority. Involve the manager or HR for formal employment processes.

**Result and limit:** Protect personal details and distinguish a mentor’s support from a manager’s performance-management authority.

### 13. Balance business and technical needs

**Situation and task:** A customer asks for stronger availability commitments.

**Action to explain:** Define the service indicator and measurement window, examine failures, and cost options against expected margin, ongoing operation, and delivery risk.

**Result and limit:** A revenue-to-cost ratio is not ROI. Evidence of measured availability also differs from an SLA promise.

### 14. Introduce a new practice

**Situation and task:** Cross-browser manual checks consume the team’s review time.

**Action to explain:** Pilot Playwright on one critical flow across Chromium, Firefox, and WebKit; retain real-device or Safari checks where needed. Document fixtures and ownership.

**Result and limit:** Compare equivalent coverage and report failures found as well as runtime. WebKit testing does not itself mean testing branded Safari.

### 15. Manage conflicting stakeholders

**Situation and task:** A migration date, a sales event, and risk concerns collide.

**Action to explain:** Find the constraint behind each request and propose milestones with owners, rollback conditions, and explicit deferred scope.

**Result and limit:** Say which commitment was renegotiated; a four-month plan does not literally meet an unchanged three-month deadline.

### 16. Resolve a technical conflict

**Situation and task:** Two engineers disagree on REST versus GraphQL.

**Action to explain:** Agree on consumer needs, caching, schema evolution, authorization, and operating cost before testing options. Make the decision owner explicit.

**Result and limit:** Distinguish TypeScript’s compile-time checks from runtime API validation; choosing TypeScript does not supply GraphQL’s schema contract.

### 17. Choose between competing priorities

**Situation and task:** Security remediation, a customer feature, and cost reduction compete for capacity.

**Action to explain:** Identify mandatory constraints first, compare remaining value and effort, and present the cost of deferral without hiding uncertainty.

**Result and limit:** Report the authorized choice and actual outcome. A priority matrix supports judgment; it does not prove the best decision after the fact.

### 18. Improve reliability

**Situation and task:** Recurring deployment, dependency, and capacity failures affect customers.

**Action to explain:** Group incidents by impact, test bounded timeouts and rollback, and rehearse the largest failure mode before expanding the solution.

**Result and limit:** State the customer-facing indicator, denominator, period, and follow-up actions rather than listing reliability tools.

### 19. Deliver bad news

**Situation and task:** A core dependency cannot handle the required workload.

**Action to explain:** Inform the decision owner promptly with evidence, options, cost of delay, and a recommendation. Own the missed validation and reduce uncertainty with a spike.

**Result and limit:** Explain the revised scope or date and how stakeholders used the warning to make a decision.

### 20. Explain your decision-making approach

**Situation and task:** You are asked about your process rather than one event.

**Action to explain:** Describe problem, constraints, options, criteria, owner, and review trigger; then anchor the process in one actual decision.

**Result and limit:** Avoid turning a framework into a fictional STAR event. Describe where the process helped and where it needed correction.

## Build a bank from real decisions

Choose experiences that cover delivery, mentoring, disagreement, technical judgment, and failure. One experience may serve several prompts, but change the emphasis to answer the question actually asked. Keep a short opening and retain the detailed evidence for follow-up.

Use numbers when you have them, with a period and denominator. Otherwise describe observable evidence such as a completed migration, an accepted design change, or independent ownership of a task. Label estimates and credit collaborators.

## Summary

An effective leadership answer connects a team constraint to your action and an observed outcome. Describe the authority you had, the trade-off you accepted, the people who contributed, and what you changed after learning from the result.

# Technical Decision Stories: From Constraint to Verified Outcome

## Definition

A technical-decision story explains a past design choice: what the system needed, what you considered, what you personally did, and what the result established. It differs from a hypothetical design by making claims about actual work.

> **Textbook Definition:** A technical decision account connects an engineering constraint to evaluated alternatives, the chosen action and evidence of its consequences, distinguishing personal contribution from collective work.

## First Principles

A technology is useful only in relation to a constraint. A retrospective must also distinguish the decision from its execution and the measured result from the explanation for that result. Otherwise an attractive architecture diagram can hide an untested assumption.

## Why it matters

Technical and behavioral prompts overlap when a design depends on migration, operating risk, or agreement across teams. Prepare to show the reasoning and the human work that made the change possible.

## Trace one decision through the system

Use this sequence before listing tools:

```text
user need → limiting behavior → candidate changes → chosen trade-off
          → rollout + failure path → comparable measurement → next decision
```

For a slow page, trace where the request waits. A repeated-query bottleneck suggests fixing that query path first; it does not by itself justify new services, a cache, and a new database. Explain each component’s job before naming its implementation.

## Questions

These fictional situations illustrate how to frame an answer. They are not product benchmarks or employment experiences. Build your own account with the [STAR method](../../00-strategy/00.03-star-method.md), then use the limits below to check its technical reasoning.

### 1. Design a system from scratch

**Situation and task:** Support needs a searchable knowledge base.

**Decision to explain:** Identify the recurring user questions, define a small release, compare managed search with database search, and keep article updates and index updates observable.

**Evidence and limit:** Explain how unanswered searches and ticket resolution were measured; an apparent deflection rate needs a defined denominator.

### 2. Migrate a critical system

**Situation and task:** Orders must move to a new service without losing updates.

**Decision to explain:** Choose one authoritative write path. Backfill from a consistent checkpoint, capture later changes with CDC or an outbox, replay idempotently, and compare reads before switching ownership.

**Evidence and limit:** State the cutover and rollback boundary. Independent dual writes can partially fail; neither a facade nor CDC alone makes rollback safe after new writes.

### 3. Optimize a slow system

**Situation and task:** A customer page spends most of its time waiting for repeated queries.

**Decision to explain:** Trace one request, fix the measured N+1 path, and remeasure before adding denormalization or caching. Use a stable cursor and unique tiebreaker for pagination.

**Evidence and limit:** Compare equivalent workloads and freshness requirements. More optimization layers are not automatically a better explanation.

### 4. Choose a vendor

**Situation and task:** A notification service needs email, push, and SMS.

**Decision to explain:** Compare channel support, regional constraints, delivery telemetry, support, integration effort, and current quoted costs on a representative pilot.

**Evidence and limit:** Separate provider acceptance, delivery, inbox placement, and opens. A delivery-rate difference is not automatically the same number of additional opens.

### 5. Handle a payment incident

**Situation and task:** A payment request times out during a provider outage.

**Decision to explain:** Treat the charge outcome as unknown. Reconcile by the existing payment identifier and reuse the original idempotency key for a supported retry. Keep pending orders pending until the authoritative result is known.

**Evidence and limit:** Reroute only through a tested path with consistent state and permitted data handling. A successful health check does not authorize a charge, and “fail open” can cause unpaid or duplicate orders.

### 6. Introduce a test runner

**Situation and task:** The current suite has slow feedback and the team proposes Vitest.

**Decision to explain:** Benchmark equivalent test sets and environments, audit mocking and setup compatibility, and run old and new suites during a bounded migration.

**Evidence and limit:** Report runtime, missing behavior, and maintenance cost. Renaming jest.fn to vi.fn is only one part of compatibility; rolling back also requires restoring the old tests and configuration.

### 7. Make a debt trade-off

**Situation and task:** One service mixes identity, billing, and profile logic.

**Decision to explain:** Map incidents and coupling, compare modular boundaries with service extraction, and test the critical contract before moving it.

**Evidence and limit:** Explain the failure mode reduced and the debt left behind. Extracting a service neither proves better architecture nor automatically reduces compliance scope.

### 8. Design for a slow dependency

**Situation and task:** A pricing call blocks checkout.

**Decision to explain:** Budget request time, bound retries, and isolate the dependency. Agree whether a cached quote is valid for the product and for how long.

**Evidence and limit:** A stale price requires an explicit price-validity policy; a fallback must not silently change what the customer agrees to pay.

### 9. Build a shared platform

**Situation and task:** Several teams have different profile-read requirements.

**Decision to explain:** Document each consumer’s latency, freshness, and consistency needs. Offer clear read contracts and ownership rather than silently routing everyone through one cache.

**Evidence and limit:** Synchronous reads do not themselves guarantee strong consistency; the source and read semantics must provide it.

### 10. Decide with incomplete data

**Situation and task:** An event pipeline could use stream processors or short-lived workers.

**Decision to explain:** Compare ordering, window state, replay, checkpoints, concurrency, and measured costs. Pilot the smallest path that can test the uncertain requirement.

**Evidence and limit:** Stateful versus stateless is not a universal ranking of complexity or throughput. Record a review trigger tied to the chosen workload.

### 11. Reduce cloud costs

**Situation and task:** The infrastructure bill is growing faster than workload.

**Decision to explain:** Attribute costs, inspect CPU, memory, I/O, burst headroom, and failover needs. Test resizing; bound storage retention; use interruptible capacity only for restartable work.

**Evidence and limit:** State savings against a comparable period and demand. Low average CPU alone does not make resizing risk-free; reservations add commitment risk.

### 12. Design an API others consume

**Situation and task:** Partners receive order events through webhooks.

**Decision to explain:** Give each event a stable ID; sign the exact payload with a timestamp; retry with bounded backoff; preserve failed events for inspection or replay. Document deduplication and schema evolution.

**Evidence and limit:** Finite retries can still exhaust without acknowledgment. “At least once” requires its delivery/recovery scope to be defined; a signature without freshness checks permits replay.

### 13. Revise a previous decision

**Situation and task:** A build-tool migration improves development but worsens the initial bundle.

**Decision to explain:** Reproduce the regression under equivalent settings, inspect split boundaries, and adjust or reverse the decision against pre-agreed user metrics.

**Evidence and limit:** Vite 8 uses Rolldown for production bundling. Describe the version and actual configuration rather than claiming a separate Rollup build is inherently the correction.

### 14. Improve service reliability

**Situation and task:** Connection waits and repeated retries cause cascading failures.

**Decision to explain:** Set a database connection budget across replicas, bound waits, and test load shedding. Use idempotent payment retries and reconcile unknown outcomes. Stagger cache refreshes.

**Evidence and limit:** A maximum pool size is a limit, not a guarantee of adaptive sizing. Report a customer-facing service indicator over a stated window.

### 15. Use data to decide on caching

**Situation and task:** A few products dominate read traffic.

**Decision to explain:** Measure database work, prototype cache-aside reads, and test stale-data behavior, invalidation, stampedes, key isolation, and fallback as well as hit rate.

**Evidence and limit:** A cache pattern safe for public products may be unsafe for carts or user profiles. Generalize only after checking consistency and authorization requirements.

## Keep evidence attached to the claim

For a performance result, record workload, traffic, version, time window, percentile, and error behavior. For reliability, define the indicator and denominator. For delivery, distinguish a provider’s acknowledgment from the user-visible outcome. Keep rejected options real, and say which assumptions would change the decision.

A diagram or runbook establishes what was designed or documented. Deployment records, incident timelines, replay checks, and measurements establish what was exercised. Describe your contribution separately from collaborators’ work.

## Summary

Explain the constraint before the technology, the component’s role before its variant, and the failure path before claiming safety. Finish with evidence that supports the result and the conditions under which you would choose differently.

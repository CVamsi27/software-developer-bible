# Behavioral Anti-Patterns: Make the Evidence Easy to Follow

A polished answer can still be hard to assess if the interviewer cannot tell what you changed or how the outcome was measured. Use this checklist to find that missing link.

```text
prompt → relevant constraint → your action → observed result → learning
"We decided" → "I wrote the benchmark; the team chose the simpler
approach after reviewing it"
```

## Definition

A **behavioral anti-pattern** is a recurring answer habit that makes relevant experience hard to assess: excessive setup, unclear ownership, unsupported results, or a story that does not answer the prompt. This **checklist** diagnoses delivery; it is not an employer's hiring rubric.

> **Textbook Definition:** A behavioral interview response provides evidence of past conduct through a specific event, actions and outcomes. Unsupported claims, unclear ownership and disconnected detail weaken the inference an interviewer can make.

## First Principles

A listener has limited time and does not share your project context. They need enough setup to understand the constraint, enough action to identify your contribution, and enough evidence to judge the outcome. Rehearsal helps only if it preserves those facts.

## Why It Matters

An impressive project can still produce an unclear answer. Reviewing an actual recording reveals where the listener must infer a missing decision, actor or result. A mock adds interruptions and follow-ups that a written draft cannot reproduce.

## How It Works

### 1. Find the Missing Link in the Story

Start with the hook's chain, then use this list to locate the break. Never invent conflict, metrics or mistakes to satisfy a format.

- **Long setup** obscures which constraint made the work difficult. Keep only the context needed to understand the decision; save extra detail for follow-up.
- **Blame language** obscures what you could influence. Describe the other party's constraint and your response; state factual accountability without insults or concealment.
- **Unsupported result** obscures what actually changed. Use measured evidence, an observable outcome, or a labeled estimate; define its period and denominator.
- **Sole-credit narrative** obscures who contributed what. Separate "I proposed, tested, implemented" from the team's decision and outcome.
- **Generic action** obscures how the work was done. Name the artifact or intervention: a benchmark, an agreed release slice, a migration rehearsal.
- **Forced conflict** obscures whether the story is authentic. Use a genuine tension when relevant; a successful collaboration needs no fabricated disagreement.
- **Prompt mismatch** obscures why this story is being told. Identify the competency first and select an experience that actually demonstrates it.
- **Unfinished ending** obscures what happened after the action. State the result, unresolved limit and useful lesson; a policy change is not required in every story.

### 2. Show Ownership Without Rewriting History

"We decided" is incomplete when your contribution matters. "I wrote the benchmark; the team chose the simpler approach after reviewing it" identifies both your work and the shared decision. If you implemented someone else's design, explain the judgment you exercised during implementation rather than claiming you drove the architecture. Likewise, "priorities changed after a customer commitment" can explain a constraint without absolving anyone of responsibility: state what was within your control, what needed escalation, and what the authorized owner decided. Serious misconduct or an unsafe decision must never hide behind positive wording.

### 3. Report an Outcome You Can Defend

| Weak claim | Evidence to use if it exists |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| “Adoption improved” | Weekly active usage, the eligible-user denominator, and the measured period |
| “Reliability improved” | The customer-facing error indicator before and after under comparable traffic |
| “Onboarding got easier” | A completed independent task, time to readiness, or specific feedback from new colleagues |

There is no required number of metrics per answer. A qualitative mentoring outcome can be stronger than invented percentages. Distinguish an estimate from a measurement, and avoid attributing every later improvement solely to your change.

### 4. Recover the Thread and Review With a Listener

Pause, state the central decision, and resume from the missing link. For example: "The key constraint was the launch date. My contribution was to identify the smaller release slice." If the closest experience differs from the prompt, say so and ask whether that adjacent example is useful. Correct a mistaken date or number plainly. Being unable to recall an example is never a reason to turn a hypothetical into a claimed experience.

Use the [section revision companion](REVISION.md) for the practice prompts. During review, mark the actual passage where context, ownership or evidence disappears — then rewrite that passage rather than polishing every sentence. For a mock, ask a partner to note where they lost the thread and which claim needed clarification. Try an interruption and a technical follow-up. Keep timing targets flexible: the opening should leave room for action and result, and the interview's format determines how much detail fits.

## Summary

Repair the link between the prompt, your action and the outcome. Preserve factual ownership, credit collaborators, state limits, and use evidence you actually have. Rehearsal should make the real experience easier to assess.

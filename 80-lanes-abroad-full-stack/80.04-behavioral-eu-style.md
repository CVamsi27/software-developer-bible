# Behavioral Interviews — Evidence, Decisions, Reflection

A behavioral answer is an account of real work, not a script of an ideal engineer. Prepare examples that show your responsibility, the constraints you faced, the decision you made, and the result you can support. Confirm the employer's process rather than relying on national stereotypes.

## First Principles

The interviewer was not present during your project. They need enough context to distinguish your actions from the team's and to evaluate the trade-off. A polished claim without supporting detail gives less evidence than an honest, specific account with a limited outcome.

## Build an evidence bank before rehearsing

Select actual examples of delivery, disagreement, ambiguity, failure, feedback, and collaboration. One event can support several themes, but do not invent a prototype, incident, deployment, or measured improvement because a template mentions it.

Keep personal facts and verified career metrics in the [canonical personal folder](personal/README.md). The [story-bank guide](80.12-story-bank.md) explains organization. When a numerical result is unavailable, describe an observable outcome and its limitations. Never add an impressive percentage to make a result sound valid.

## Tell the decision using STAR and reflection

Briefly establish the situation and your responsibility. Spend most of the answer on your action: alternatives, why you chose one, how you communicated, and what happened. Finish with the result and what you would change now.

A hypothetical structure is: “Two teams disagreed about a storage choice. I proposed a comparison using our important queries and operational constraints. We chose the option that met those constraints. The comparison took longer than planned; next time I would agree on the decision criteria before building prototypes.” Replace every detail with your own evidence before using it.

Use “I” for your action and “we” for shared work. A concise first answer leaves room for follow-ups; fixed percentage allocations and mandatory metrics are rehearsal aids at most.

## Adapt the example to the prompt

For a disagreement, represent the other person's reasoning fairly and explain the eventual decision. For failure, distinguish a production incident from a defect caught before release. For urgency, show the risk you assessed and the scope you deferred. For impact, connect the technical change to an observable user or operational outcome without claiming causation that you did not measure.

Influence without authority requires another person's decision to change. Choosing your own priorities as a sole engineer does not demonstrate that mechanism. A security improvement can matter before an immediate business metric changes; prevention and future maintainability are legitimate outcomes when described honestly.

## Research motivation and employment terms separately

Use a product, job requirement, or engineering article you actually inspected when explaining interest in the company. Do not quote invented blog posts, workweek policies, launches, or staff names. Ask how decisions, reviews, on-call, and progression work in the team you would join.

Salary, leave, location, sponsorship, and working arrangements are legitimate topics. Discuss them with the recruiter at a useful point in the process; asking about them does not imply disinterest in engineering. State relocation plans accurately. Family details are optional, and belong in private notes rather than a reusable public-facing answer.

## Written responses need the same evidence

Follow the requested length and assistance policy. Draft from actual events, preserve your own meaning, and check every fact after editing. Perfect grammar does not establish AI use, and deliberate errors do not establish authorship. Do not send confidential project data to an unapproved tool.

## Summary

Prepare real events, explain the choice and its consequences, and reflect on a specific improvement. Rehearse for clarity while leaving the facts intact.

# Story Bank — Organize Evidence Before Rehearsal

[![Role priority](https://img.shields.io/badge/role--priority-Important-blue)](../docs/topic-importance.md)
[![General importance](https://img.shields.io/badge/general--importance-Important-blue)](../docs/topic-importance.md)

Trying to remember five years of projects on the spot in a behavioral interview leads to rambling answers and forgotten details. A **story bank** solves that cleanly: it stores a compact set of 5–7 real, fact-checked engineering events in your private workspace so you can adapt them smoothly to almost any senior behavioral prompt without ever embellishing or contradicting yourself.

## First Principles

One major project often illustrates multiple competencies — delivery under pressure, cross-team alignment, and a production bug fix — depending on which angle the interviewer probes. Keeping **one factual master record per event** prevents you from accidentally telling conflicting versions across different interview rounds.

## 1. Record the Facts First, Polish the Pitch Second

For each real project or incident in your private story bank, capture seven concise bullet points:

1. **Context and date:** System, team size, and business constraint (clearly marking whether it was a live production incident or a pre-release catch).
2. **Your exact responsibility:** What *you* owned versus what teammates owned.
3. **Options considered and trade-off:** The real alternative you rejected and why.
4. **Your actions:** The concrete technical or alignment steps you drove.
5. **Verified result:** Exact measurement (with period and denominator) *or* a clean qualitative outcome — never invent a percentage.
6. **Lesson or systemic change:** What you changed afterward so the problem stayed solved.
7. **Disclosure boundary:** Confirm zero confidential customer names, secrets, or proprietary financials slip into your answer.

## 2. Map Stories Honestly to Senior Competencies

Cover core senior themes (end-to-end delivery, technical disagreement, production mistake, ambiguity and prioritization, mentoring, and technical depth) using your genuine work history. There is no magic required story count — if you genuinely lack a story for a niche prompt, note the gap honestly rather than stretching an unrelated project (for instance, working solo on your own roadmap does *not* prove "influencing without authority").

Keep two versions ready for each story: a **90-second opening summary** and **3–4 deeper technical and trade-off details** for follow-up probing.

## 3. Stress-Test Technical Details Before Interview Day

Senior interviewers routinely ask technical follow-ups inside behavioral rounds, which instantly expose sloppy wording:
- **Performance stories:** Separate the measured symptom (for example, 95th-percentile (p95) latency) from the diagnosed root cause and verify the before-and-after workload matched.
- **Migration stories:** Know the exact schema-compatibility and rollback contract (never claim "zero downtime" if the migration took an exclusive `ACCESS EXCLUSIVE` table lock!).
- **Security stories:** Distinguish the engineering control you shipped from a full formal audit or legal certification.

Reconcile all personal drafts in your private story bank against your resume bullets before interviewing so every date, scope, and number aligns 100%.

## Summary

Keep 5–7 real, verified project records in your private workspace with clear ownership, alternatives, honest outcomes, and disclosure limits. Stress-test the underlying technical mechanics ahead of time, then rehearse adapting each story to the exact competency asked without altering the facts.

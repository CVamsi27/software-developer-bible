## 1. Project Overview & Scale

### Q1.1 — Walk me through what Docita does. Who are the users?

Docita is a multi-tenant healthcare SaaS for outpatient clinics. The users are:

- **Doctors** (the primary user): manage their schedule, run consultations, write prescriptions, store patient records, capture signatures, generate bills.
- **Front-desk / clinic staff**: register patients, book appointments, handle admissions, manage beds/wards.
- **Patients** (indirect): they show up to appointments, get SMS/WhatsApp reminders, see their records via a public booking portal.
- **Clinic admins**: own the clinic, see reports, manage staff and roles.

It's clinic-facing traffic (doctors use it during consultations), so reliability matters more than throughput at peak. We can't have a doctor blocked mid-consultation.

**Concrete:** 147 Prisma models in `packages/db/prisma/schema.prisma` — patients, appointments, prescriptions, beds, wards, admissions, doctor schedules, audit log, idempotency records, outbox events, queue jobs, webhooks.

### Q1.2 — How many users, doctors, patients, clinics are we talking about? What's the scale?

Honest answer: **early-stage numbers**. We're not at FAANG scale. As of writing:

- A handful of clinics in pilot (single-digit to low double-digit clinics)
- ~dozens of doctors active
- Patient records in the low thousands

**Why it matters for the answer:** interviewers want to know if you can think about scale, not whether you've already operated at 100K QPS. The right framing is "designed for multi-tenant from day one, but the real validation is the *constraints* I built for — multi-tenant isolation, RBAC, audit logging — which are the same primitives you need at 10x, 100x, 1000x scale."

**If pushed for numbers:** "We're early, but the architecture is built so the next 10x doesn't require a rewrite."

### Q1.3 — What does "multi-tenant" mean in your implementation? How do you isolate tenants?

Multi-tenant = multiple clinics share the same database and application, but each clinic's data is isolated. Implementation:

1. **Every domain table has a `clinicId` foreign key** — `User.clinicId`, `Patient.clinicId`, `Appointment.clinicId`, etc. (visible in `schema.prisma`).
2. **Tenant resolution at the auth layer** — the JWT carries the `clinicId`. A request-scoped `ClinicContext` (NestJS provider scoped per request) holds the active clinic for the request lifetime.
3. **Authorization layer (`packages/authorization`)** — every Prisma query goes through a `tenantScopedPrisma` wrapper that injects `where: { clinicId: ... }` automatically. You cannot accidentally query across clinics. The dev experience is: you call `this.prisma.patient.findMany()` and the framework adds the scope — you never write the `clinicId` filter yourself.
4. **A `bypassClinicScope` flag** for cross-tenant admin/internal jobs, with audit logging when used.

**Why this matters:** tenant leakage is the #1 risk in multi-tenant SaaS. We made it structurally impossible for a regular code path to leak.

### Q1.4 — What's your role there? Are you the only engineer?

Founding Engineer — I built it. I was the only full-time engineer for most of the time. Recently added a couple of contractors/interns. So I own the entire system: backend, frontend, infra, DevOps, product decisions. That's why I can talk credibly about every layer.

The honest framing: "I have to make every trade-off decision, not just implement someone else's architecture. So I can defend the *why* behind every choice — and I know what I'd do differently if I had a team."

### Q1.5 — Is it in production? Is it serving real patients?

Yes, it's live (`docita.work`). I don't claim massive scale, but real clinics use it daily for real consultations. That means:

- Real data (PII, medical records) — so the security/audit choices are real, not academic
- Real downtime cost — a 1-hour outage during clinic hours = real angry clinic owners
- Real monitoring — Sentry, structured logs, request IDs in every log line
- Real incident response — I've been paged at 11pm for a stuck queue worker

**If asked about uptime:** "I don't have a 99.99% SLA to point to, but the architecture supports it — health checks, zero-downtime deploys on Fly.io, separate worker process so a long job doesn't take down the API."

### Q1.6 — What's the user base, growth rate, and revenue model?

Early stage. Pilot clinics, mostly through direct sales (we know the doctors). Revenue model: per-clinic subscription, tiered by features (number of doctors, WhatsApp integration, etc.). I won't share MRR in an interview, but the model is B2B SaaS, monthly recurring, multi-tenant — standard vertical SaaS.

**If pushed for a number:** "I can share that we've onboarded N clinics in M months and the retention is good — clinics that joined 6 months ago are still using it daily."

### Q1.7 — What was the single hardest technical problem you had to solve at Docita?

I have a few candidates. The framing depends on the role:

- **For a backend-heavy role:** the audit log + idempotency design. Healthcare requires every state-changing action to be traceable, and the same network glitch that causes a user to retry a "book appointment" must not create two bookings. We solved it with `AuditLog`, `Idempotency-Key` header support, and an outbox pattern for cross-service events.
- **For a system-design role:** the multi-tenant data isolation. The structural enforcement of `clinicId` in every query — see Q1.3.
- **For a frontend role:** the offline-tolerant doctor schedule view. Doctors need to see their day even with spotty clinic Wi-Fi. Service worker + IndexedDB + optimistic mutations with retry queue.
- **For a DevOps role:** the safe migration pipeline. Healthcare data — losing it is not an option. We have a `safe-migrate.sh` script that does backup → dry-run → deploy → verify, and we never auto-merge to `main` without a passing migration check.

I'd lead with the one most relevant to the job.

### Q1.8 — What would you do differently if you started Docita today?

A few honest things, in order of pain:

1. **Adopt event-sourcing from day one for the audit log.** We have `AuditLog` + `AuditLogArchive` but the schema is a denormalized snapshot of the change. If I were starting over, I'd use a proper event log (e.g., eventstoredb or just Postgres with a `recorded_at` + actor + immutable payload). Right now I can't replay the state of a record at any past timestamp — I'd have to walk the audit log forward.
2. **Single Prisma client per service, not per process.** We started with a shared `@workspace/db` package which means the API and worker share the same generated client. That's fine until they diverge. I'd prefer generated clients per service.
3. **OpenAPI-first contracts from day 1.** We added the OpenAPI generator later. Retrofitting it onto ad-hoc REST controllers was a lot of work. If I were starting over, I'd design the DTO with Zod, then auto-derive OpenAPI, then write the controller.
4. **Skip the Next.js experiment.** I migrated the frontend from Next.js to Vite. The reason is below in Q4.1. If I were starting over, I'd have started with Vite + a clean SPA. Next.js App Router is great for content sites, but for a stateful clinic dashboard the server-component model fights you.

**Why this answer works:** shows self-awareness, real lessons learned, and a willingness to be wrong.

### Q1.9 — How do you balance building features vs. tech debt as a solo founder?

Honestly: **feature first, then carve out time for the debt before it bites**. My pattern:

- When a feature is greenfield, take the time to set up the right primitives (RBAC, audit log, tenant scope).
- When a feature is "we already have something like this but it doesn't quite work", I add a TODO and ship the feature, but I add a "tech-debt Friday" slot.
- Every quarter I look at the top 5 pain points and pick 1-2 to refactor.
- I keep a `docs/tech-debt.md` (in my head, mostly) and I review it before adding new features — sometimes the new feature *is* the refactor.

**For the interview:** I can name 2-3 specific refactors I did: extracted the authorization package, replaced ad-hoc logging with a request-id-aware logging interceptor, consolidated N+1 query hot paths.

### Q1.10 — What does "agentic coding" mean in your workflow?

Resume claim: "Active user of agentic coding (Cursor daily) — review, edit, ship."

Concrete workflow:

- I use Cursor with the codebase indexed. For boilerplate (DTO, controller scaffolding, test files, types from Zod schemas), I prompt and accept.
- **I never accept AI-generated code without reading it.** This is non-negotiable. I treat it as a first draft from a junior.
- The biggest wins: writing test fixtures (boilerplate reduction), exploring API designs ("give me 3 ways to model this relationship, with trade-offs"), refactor passes (rename across files, update call sites).
- The biggest risks: drift on type signatures, hallucinated APIs, security anti-patterns (e.g., AI suggesting `eval`, plaintext passwords, missing auth checks). I have caught all three.

**For the interview:** "I think of agentic coding as a force multiplier for boilerplate, not a replacement for review. The 80/20 is 20% faster at typing, but I read every diff like I'd review a junior's PR."

### Q1.11 — Give me an example of a time the AI tool was wrong and you caught it.

Specific story I keep ready:

- The AI suggested a Prisma `findUnique` with a non-unique field, which silently returns null at runtime but the types say it can't. Caught in code review by looking at the actual schema (`@unique` decorators).
- The AI once generated a "tenant isolation" helper that filtered on a `clinicId` field, but the field on that model was actually `organizationId`. I caught it because the test failed on a different clinic.
- The AI once wrote a JWT verification that didn't check the `aud` claim. Catching it prevented a real auth bug.

**Why this works:** the interviewer is testing whether you use AI naively or critically. The concrete bugs I caught prove the latter.

### Q1.12 — How do you measure success of a feature once it's shipped?

- **Adoption:** does the doctor actually use it? We have structured logs that tag events, so I can count feature usage per clinic.
- **Time saved:** for features like auto-fill patient info, the "before" was 30 sec per form, the "after" is 5 sec. I asked a couple of doctors to time themselves.
- **Error rate:** if a feature has a high error rate in Sentry, it's a failure even if it's used. I treat the absence of error reports as a feature of a well-designed feature (the error paths are explicit, not silent).
- **Support load:** if the clinic admin pings me 3 times in a week about the same thing, that's a feature that needs to be redesigned, not a user that needs training.

## 2. Architecture & System Design

### Q2.1 — Walk me through the high-level architecture of Docita.

```
                ┌──────────────────────────────┐
                │  Cloudflare Workers (Vite SPA)│
                │  docita.work, *.docita.work   │
                └────────────┬─────────────────┘
                             │ HTTPS
                             ▼
                ┌──────────────────────────────┐
                │  NestJS API  (Fly.io)         │
                │  - JWT auth + tenant scope    │
                │  - REST controllers + Zod DTO │
                │  - Prisma → PostgreSQL (RDS)  │
                │  - S3 (signatures, uploads)   │
                │  - Sentry, Helmet, Zod pipe   │
                │  - Outbox events              │
                └────┬───────────────┬─────────┘
                     │               │
                     │  same DB      │  OutboxEvent
                     ▼               ▼
              ┌─────────────┐  ┌──────────────────────┐
              │ PostgreSQL  │  │ Worker (Fly.io)      │
              │ (AWS RDS)   │  │ - Polls QueueJob     │
              │             │  │ - WhatsApp, PDFs,    │
              │             │  │   webhooks, retries  │
              └─────────────┘  └──────────────────────┘
                                          │
                                          ▼
                              ┌─────────────────────────┐
                              │ 3rd party: WhatsApp,    │
                              │ payment, SMS gateways   │
                              └─────────────────────────┘
```

Three deployable units: SPA, API, Worker. One database. One S3 bucket for assets. The worker is a separate process so long-running jobs don't take down the API.

### Q2.2 — Why three deployable units instead of one monolith?

A few reasons:

1. **The worker has different scaling characteristics.** The API needs to be fast (p95 < 300ms target). The worker processes WhatsApp sends, PDF generations, webhooks — minutes-long jobs. If they share a process, a slow PDF gen can starve the API.
2. **The worker has different failure modes.** A failed WhatsApp send should retry with backoff. The API should not retry a 200 response. Separating them means I can write different health-check logic, different deploy cadence, different concurrency limits.
3. **The SPA is on Cloudflare Workers because it's static.** The Vite build output is uploaded and served at the edge. No Node server for the frontend, no per-request cost.

**Trade-off I accept:** three things to deploy, three sets of env vars, three CI pipelines. As a solo founder, that's manageable. At 10 engineers I'd want to consolidate the CI.

### Q2.3 — Why a separate worker? Couldn't you just use cron or background jobs in the API?

I considered it. The reasons I split:

1. **Process isolation.** A long-running PDF job in the API holds an event loop slot. In Node.js, this can starve other requests. A separate process has its own event loop.
2. **Independent scaling.** The worker can scale to 10 instances while the API scales to 3. If they share a process, they scale together, which is wasteful.
3. **Different health checks.** The API returns 200 if it can serve a request. The worker returns 200 if it can claim a job. Different signals, different alerts.
4. **Crash safety.** If the worker crashes mid-job, only the in-flight job is lost. The next instance picks it up. The API crashing means users see 5xx — completely different blast radius.
5. **Cost.** The worker can run on a smaller box, with a different deploy region (close to the DB for the API, anywhere for the worker).

**When this is overkill:** if I had < 1 background job/minute, I'd inline it in the API and use `setImmediate` or a simple queue table polled by a cron. The split pays off around 50+ jobs/minute.

### Q2.4 — Why PostgreSQL? Why not MySQL, MongoDB, or DynamoDB?

- **PostgreSQL** because: it's the right tool for relational healthcare data with strict consistency requirements. The schema is heavy on relations (Patient → Appointments → Prescriptions → Audit). I want FKs, transactions, JOINs, and an actual query planner. Postgres is the de-facto default.
- **Why not MySQL:** Postgres has better JSON support (`jsonb`), better full-text search, generated columns, `pg_trgm`, and stronger extension ecosystem. None of those are deal-breakers for MySQL, but Postgres has them out of the box.
- **Why not MongoDB:** healthcare data is relational. The doctor-patient-appointment graph is a textbook RDBMS workload. A document DB would force denormalization and joins in code.
- **Why not DynamoDB:** single-region lock-in, harder to evolve schema, and the cost is unpredictable at low scale. At Docita's current size, a managed Postgres on RDS is cheaper and gives me more control.
- **Why managed (RDS) vs self-hosted:** I don't want to be on call for a Postgres upgrade. RDS handles backups, point-in-time recovery, failover. The $30/month is worth my sleep.

**Honest trade-off I accept:** Postgres vertical scaling has limits. If I ever needed 100K writes/sec, I'd shard or move to a distributed SQL. But that's a problem I want to have.

### Q2.5 — How do you handle multi-tenancy at the database level? Shared schema, schema-per-tenant, or database-per-tenant?

**Shared schema with `clinicId` column on every domain table.**

I considered the other two:

- **Schema-per-tenant (Postgres schemas):** clean isolation, but cross-tenant queries (for analytics, for admin support) are painful. Migrations get harder — you have N schemas to migrate. Backup/restore per tenant is its own ops problem. I didn't see a benefit that justified the operational cost.
- **Database-per-tenant:** strongest isolation, but cost scales with tenant count. At 50 clinics, that's 50 databases, 50 connections pools, 50 backup jobs. Not viable for our scale.

**Why shared schema with `clinicId` works for us:** the structural enforcement of `clinicId` in the data-access layer means tenant isolation is a code property, not a runtime property. The risk of cross-tenant leakage is "the dev forgot to filter on `clinicId`" — and our data-access package makes that impossible by injecting the filter automatically.

**What I'd reconsider:** at 100+ clinics with very different compliance postures, schema-per-tenant starts to make sense for blast-radius reasons. We're not there.

### Q2.6 — How do you prevent accidental cross-tenant data leaks?

Layered defense:

1. **Type system.** Every domain entity has `clinicId: string` in its type. You can't construct a Patient without a `clinicId`.
2. **Data-access wrapper.** A `tenantScopedPrisma` wrapper that injects `where: { clinicId: ctx.clinicId }` on every query. Code in the API calls `prisma.patient.findMany()` and the framework adds the scope. You cannot forget.
3. **Integration tests.** Every domain test seeds two clinics and asserts that an authenticated user from clinic A cannot see clinic B's data. This is a hard-fail in CI.
4. **Audit log.** The `AuditLog` table records every read of patient data with the actor and clinic. We can run a query to look for "reads across clinic boundaries" — if it ever returns rows, that's a bug.
5. **Defense in depth at the DB.** Postgres row-level security (RLS) is configured as a backstop. Even if the application code is buggy, the DB will reject cross-tenant reads.

**If asked for the one thing I'd add:** a periodic "tenant-isolation fuzz test" that picks random tenant pairs and random user roles, runs all read queries, asserts that they only see their own tenant's data. Run on a cron in staging.

### Q2.7 — Why an outbox pattern for cross-service events?

The classic problem: I need to write to the DB and also call an external service (e.g., enqueue a WhatsApp message). If the DB write succeeds and the external call fails, the system is inconsistent. If the external call happens first and the DB write fails, the external effect is permanent.

The outbox pattern: write the event to an `OutboxEvent` table inside the same DB transaction as the state change. A separate worker polls the outbox, publishes the event, and marks it as processed. **Atomicity is guaranteed by the DB transaction.**

**Concrete in Docita:** when an appointment is booked, in the same transaction I write the appointment and an `OutboxEvent` of type `appointment.booked`. The worker reads the outbox, sends the WhatsApp confirmation, and marks the event as sent. If the worker crashes, the outbox still has the event — the next poll retries.

**Trade-off I accept:** the worker has a small delay (poll interval ~1s). For WhatsApp, this is fine. For real-time notifications, I'd use a CDC stream (Debezium → Kafka → worker) instead.

### Q2.8 — Why do you have an `AuditLog` AND an outbox? Aren't they the same thing?

Different purposes:

- **Audit log** = "who did what to which record when". Compliance, security, debug.
- **Outbox** = "trigger an action in another service". Integration, automation, retries.

An audit log entry is durable and queryable for years (regulatory). An outbox event is consumed and discarded.

In practice, an `appointment.booked` event generates both: an `AuditLog` entry ("Dr. X booked appt for patient Y at time T") and an `OutboxEvent` ("send WhatsApp to patient").

### Q2.9 — How do you handle schema migrations safely?

This was non-negotiable for me, given healthcare data.

- **`packages/db/prisma/schema.prisma`** is the source of truth.
- **`prisma migrate dev`** for development.
- **`prisma migrate deploy`** for production, with a pre-deploy check:
  1. **`safe-migrate.sh backup`** — `pg_dump` the production DB before any migration.
  2. **`safe-migrate.sh dry-run`** — apply the migration to a clone, check for errors.
  3. **`safe-migrate.sh deploy`** — apply to production with a short maintenance window if needed.
  4. **`db:check-integrity`** — verify row counts, FK integrity, no orphaned rows.
- **Every migration has a description and a rollback plan** in a comment at the top.
- **Destructive migrations are split** into multiple deploys. Drop a column? Step 1: stop writing to it. Step 2: deploy code that doesn't read it. Step 3: drop the column.
- **I never auto-merge to main without a passing migration check.**

**Why this matters:** I learned this the hard way. A migration that takes a 1-second lock on a 1M-row table can take 5 minutes. We now test migration time on a representative-size staging clone.

### Q2.10 — How do you handle a hot query path with 50ms p95?

Document the actual steps I take:

1. **Measure first.** Postgres `log_min_duration_statement = 100ms`, Sentry performance tracing, Datadog/New Relic if we had it. Find the slow queries.
2. **EXPLAIN ANALYZE the slow query.** Look for sequential scans on large tables, missing indexes, expensive sorts.
3. **Index the right things.** Composite index on the WHERE columns in the right order. Partial index for the common filter.
4. **Eliminate N+1.** Use Prisma `include` to fetch in one query, not N. Or use `findMany` with an `in` clause.
5. **Caching.** Redis cache for read-heavy data with a clear invalidation trigger.
6. **Connection pool.** `pgbouncer` if the API has bursty traffic.
7. **Re-measure.** Confirm p95 dropped.

**Specific Docita example:** patient list view in the clinic dashboard was 800ms p95. Root cause: N+1 loading each patient's last appointment. Fixed by adding a `lastAppointment` denormalized column maintained by an outbox-driven worker. New p95: 80ms.

### Q2.11 — How do you design for failure?

Concrete examples:

- **All external API calls have timeouts and retries with jittered exponential backoff.** A 5-second timeout, retry 3 times with backoff of 1s, 2s, 4s, plus ±20% jitter. Capped at 3 retries.
- **Idempotency keys on POST.** Every state-changing endpoint accepts an `Idempotency-Key` header. The server stores the response keyed by (user, key) for 24h. A retry with the same key returns the same response, no double-charge.
- **Circuit breaker on third-party services.** If the WhatsApp gateway fails 5 times in a minute, we open the circuit and queue locally. The clinic admin sees a "we'll send this in 5 min" instead of an error.
- **Database connection resilience.** Connection pool with a `connectionTimeoutMillis` and `idle_in_transaction_session_timeout`. We kill long-running transactions.
- **Worker idempotency.** Each `QueueJob` has a unique key. Re-claiming a job is safe — the worker checks the key before processing.

**The test:** I have a "chaos Friday" where I pull the plug on the DB during peak hours and watch what breaks. The goal is graceful degradation, not a hard fail.

### Q2.12 — Explain your caching strategy.

- **Redis** via `@nestjs/cache-manager`. Three cache layers:
  1. **HTTP response cache** for read-heavy GET endpoints (e.g., `GET /api/clinics/me/doctors` — doctors in this clinic). TTL 60s. Invalidation: clinic config change triggers a `cache.del('clinic:' + id + ':doctors')`.
  2. **Object cache** for hot read models. Patient summary, doctor schedule, prescription template list. TTL 5min.
  3. **Computed/derived cache** for expensive queries. "Patient's upcoming appointments" — computed, cached 30s, invalidated by the appointment mutation.
- **Cache-aside pattern.** Read: try cache, miss → DB, populate cache. Write: update DB, then `del` cache key. Never update cache without DB.
- **Cache keys are scoped by `clinicId`.** `clinic:abc:doctor:xyz:schedule` so tenant A's cache never serves tenant B.
- **No cache on writes.** POST/PATCH/DELETE go straight to DB.
- **Cache stampede protection.** Use a `singleflight` pattern: if 100 concurrent requests miss the same key, only one hits the DB, the rest wait for the result.

**Why not just `pgbouncer` and rely on Postgres caching?** Postgres cache is good for raw rows, but not for joined/filtered results. Application-level cache with explicit invalidation beats implicit DB cache when you have 20+ hot query shapes.

**Honest trade-off:** cache invalidation is hard. I have a "cache sanity test" in CI that mutates an entity and asserts the cache key is invalidated.

### Q2.13 — How would you scale to 10x traffic?

For Docita specifically (clinic-facing, not consumer):

1. **Vertical scale first.** The RDS instance is small. Bumping from `db.t3.medium` to `db.r6g.large` doubles the connections, 4x the memory. Cost: ~$200/month more. Time: 1 hour.
2. **Read replicas.** Most clinic queries are reads. Route `GET` to a read replica, `POST/PATCH/DELETE` to the primary. Postgres logical replication is built-in.
3. **Add a CDN in front of the API for the public read endpoints** (the patient booking portal). Most traffic there is anonymous reads.
4. **Horizontal API replicas.** Fly.io auto-scales based on connection count. 3 → 10 instances.
5. **Move from SQS-style polling to push-based workers** if event volume gets to 1K+/sec.
6. **Sharding Postgres by `clinicId`** — this is the last resort, not the first. We have 50 clinics today; 500 is comfortable on one DB.

**What I would NOT do at 10x:** split the monolith into microservices. The cost of distributed transactions across services is high; the value of staying monolithic is high. Monolith with good boundaries is fine until ~50 engineers.

### Q2.14 — How do you decide between SQL and NoSQL for a new feature?

Default to SQL. Every feature at Docita so far has fit Postgres.

I consider NoSQL when:

- The data is genuinely document-shaped with no relations (e.g., a marketing CMS, user-generated rich text).
- The read pattern is "scan all and filter" and the data is write-once (logs, analytics).
- The access pattern is geographically distributed and eventual consistency is fine (e.g., user preferences on a global app).

For Docita's domain — patients, doctors, appointments, prescriptions — the relational model is the right tool. NoSQL would force me to denormalize and reconcile by hand.

### Q2.15 — How do you handle a schema change that would break the API?

Concrete protocol:

1. **Backward-compatible change.** Add a nullable column → deploy code that handles both old and new → backfill data → make the column non-nullable in a later deploy.
2. **Removing a field.** Mark as deprecated in the API response (e.g., `"legacy_field": null`) for one release. Then remove.
3. **Renaming.** Add the new field, deploy code that writes both. Update readers to prefer new. Remove the old.
4. **Breaking response shape change.** Bump the API version (`/api/v2/...`). Old version stays live for one quarter.

**The rule:** never break a client in a way that requires them to deploy the same day. Healthcare clinics don't update their browser on your schedule.

## 3. Backend / NestJS / Prisma / PostgreSQL

### Q3.1 — Why NestJS over plain Express?

Concrete reasons:

- **Dependency injection + module boundaries.** A multi-tenant SaaS has 10+ domain modules (Patient, Appointment, Prescription, Billing, etc.). Express leaves module structure to you, which means by month 6 you have a god-route. NestJS modules enforce boundaries.
- **Guards, interceptors, pipes, filters** as first-class concepts. My auth check, request logging, Zod validation, error filter are all decorators, not middleware spaghetti.
- **TypeScript-first.** Decorators and DI types are usable; you don't fight the framework.
- **Built-in OpenAPI support** via `@nestjs/swagger`.
- **Testability.** NestJS has a `Test.createTestingModule` pattern that makes integration tests almost as easy as unit tests.

**Honest answer:** for a 5-route CRUD, Express is fine. For a 100+ route multi-tenant SaaS, NestJS's structure pays off.

**What I dislike about NestJS:** the decorator magic makes stack traces hard to read. The framework's metaprogramming is opaque. I had to learn the request lifecycle to debug a tricky issue.

### Q3.2 — How do you structure a NestJS module?

Concrete:

```
src/
  domains/
    patient/
      patient.module.ts
      patient.controller.ts
      patient.service.ts
      dto/
        create-patient.dto.ts
        update-patient.dto.ts
        query-patient.dto.ts
      entities/
        patient.entity.ts
      patient.service.spec.ts
      patient.controller.spec.ts
```

Each domain module:

- Imports its own Prisma model, its own authorization, its own DTOs.
- Exports its `PatientService` for other domains to consume (e.g., `AppointmentService` needs to look up a patient).
- **Doesn't import other domains' services** unless absolutely needed. When it does, the dependency is explicit and one-way.

**Why domain-driven layout:** it scales. When the appointment module grows, I don't accidentally touch patient code.

**What I do NOT do:** generic "services" or "helpers" folders. They become junk drawers.

### Q3.3 — Walk me through a request lifecycle in Docita's API.

Concrete lifecycle for `POST /api/v1/patients` (create a patient):

1. **Cloudflare** terminates TLS, routes to the API's Fly.io region.
2. **Helmet middleware** sets security headers.
3. **Compression middleware** gzip-encodes the response.
4. **Cookie parser** parses the JWT cookie.
5. **RequestLoggingInterceptor** generates a request ID, logs the entry.
6. **JWT Guard** verifies the token, extracts the `userId` and `clinicId`.
7. **RolesGuard** checks the user has the role required for this endpoint.
8. **ZodValidationPipe** validates the request body against the `CreatePatientDto` Zod schema. Bad request → 400.
9. **Tenant scope** wraps the Prisma client with `clinicId: req.clinicId`.
10. **PatientController.create()** is called.
11. **PatientService.create()** checks uniqueness (phone number, Aadhaar, etc.), creates the patient, writes an `AuditLog`, writes an `OutboxEvent` for "patient.created".
12. **Sentry** captures any uncaught error.
13. **AllExceptionsFilter** converts thrown errors to a structured JSON response.
14. **Response** is sent back through the compression middleware.

Total target: < 200ms p95 for a "create patient" call.

### Q3.4 — How do you use Zod in a NestJS app?

Concrete:

- **DTOs are Zod schemas**, not classes. `export const CreatePatientSchema = z.object({...})`.
- **TypeScript types are derived**: `type CreatePatientDto = z.infer<typeof CreatePatientSchema>`.
- **`ZodValidationPipe`** validates the body, query, and params against the schema. Throws a 400 with a list of issues.
- **OpenAPI generation**: the schema's `.describe()` and `.example()` flow into the OpenAPI doc.
- **Runtime + compile-time safety**: I get runtime validation from Zod and TypeScript types from `z.infer`. One source of truth.

**Specific example:**

```typescript
export const CreatePatientSchema = z.object({
  firstName: z.string().min(1).max(100),
  lastName: z.string().min(1).max(100),
  phone: z.string().regex(/^\+?[0-9]{10,15}$/),
  dateOfBirth: z.coerce.date().max(new Date()),
  gender: z.enum(['MALE', 'FEMALE', 'OTHER']),
  tags: z.array(z.string()).optional().default([]),
}).strict(); // .strict() rejects unknown keys
export type CreatePatientDto = z.infer<typeof CreatePatientSchema>;
```

**Why this pattern is good:** the regex, the date constraint, the enum — all enforced at the boundary. The service layer can trust its input.

### Q3.5 — How do you handle transactions across multiple operations?

Concrete rules:

- **Any write that touches >1 table goes in a `$transaction`.**
- **Cross-domain writes** (e.g., "book appointment + create patient charge + write audit log") use `prisma.$transaction([...])` for an interactive transaction.
- **Long-running transactions are forbidden.** `idle_in_transaction_session_timeout = 10s` on the DB kills anything longer.
- **The outbox event is written in the same transaction as the state change.** This is the key invariant.

**Concrete example — booking an appointment:**

```typescript
await prisma.$transaction(async (tx) => {
  const appt = await tx.appointment.create({ data: { ... } });
  await tx.auditLog.create({ data: { action: 'appointment.create', actorId, entityId: appt.id, ... } });
  await tx.outboxEvent.create({ data: { type: 'appointment.booked', payload: { ... }, clinicId, ... } });
  return appt;
});
```

**If the transaction fails:** no appointment, no audit log, no outbox event. Atomic.

**If the outbox event fails to publish (worker crash):** the event is still in the DB. Next poll retries. Idempotency key on the event prevents double-send.

### Q3.6 — How do you avoid N+1 queries with Prisma?

Concrete patterns:

- **`include` for known relations.** `prisma.appointment.findMany({ include: { patient: true, doctor: true } })` — one query with JOINs, not N+1.
- **`in` for batch lookups.** Instead of looping `findUnique`, collect IDs, do `findMany({ where: { id: { in: ids } } })`.
- **DataLoader for graph traversal.** If A → B → C, fetch all A's, collect B IDs, fetch all B in one query, collect C IDs, fetch all C. This is the canonical pattern.
- **Query inspection in dev.** I have a Prisma middleware that logs query count per request. If a request fires > 10 queries, it's a code-review flag.

**Specific bug I caught:** the appointment list view was 18 queries per request. Reduced to 3 by using `include` for the patient and a separate batch query for the doctor's name. p95 dropped from 800ms to 80ms.

### Q3.7 — How do you index the database?

Concrete principles:

- **Index columns in WHERE clauses** of hot queries. Composite index on `(clinicId, status, createdAt)` for the "list my clinic's pending appointments sorted by date" query.
- **Partial indexes for the common case.** `CREATE INDEX ... ON appointments (clinic_id, doctor_id) WHERE status = 'SCHEDULED'` — only schedules an index on the rows we actually query.
- **Don't index tiny tables** (< 1000 rows). A sequential scan is faster.
- **Don't over-index.** Every index slows writes. I have a "stale index audit" that lists indexes never used in the last 30 days (via `pg_stat_user_indexes`).
- **Index the FK column.** Postgres does NOT auto-index FKs, but the `ON DELETE CASCADE` and JOINs need them.

**Tooling I use:**

- `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` on slow queries.
- `pg_stat_statements` to find the top N slowest queries.
- A custom `scripts/profile-queries.ts` that runs the top 20 query shapes and times them.

**Concrete example:** the patient search query was slow because it was filtering on `firstName` and `lastName` with `ILIKE`. I added a `pg_trgm` GIN index on `(firstName, lastName)` and the query went from 1.2s to 50ms.

### Q3.8 — How do you version your API?

- **URL versioning.** `/api/v1/patients`, `/api/v2/patients`. v1 stays live for 6 months after v2 launches.
- **Sunset header.** v1 responses include `Sunset: Sat, 01 Mar 2026 00:00:00 GMT` so clients can plan migration.
- **Deprecation header** on individual endpoints that are marked for removal.
- **OpenAPI per version.** I have `/api/v1/openapi.json` and `/api/v2/openapi.json` served from the API.

**Why URL versioning over header versioning:** easier to test in a browser, easier to route at the CDN, easier to roll back.

### Q3.9 — How do you test your NestJS services?

Three layers:

- **Unit tests** for pure logic. Zod schema tests, business rules, pure functions. Jest, fast.
- **Integration tests** for the database layer. Spin up a Postgres in Docker, run migrations, test the Prisma queries. Each test cleans up its data.
- **E2E tests** for the HTTP API. Spawn the NestJS app in-process, hit endpoints with `supertest`. Cover happy paths and important error paths.

**Specific Docita example:**

- `PatientService.create()`: unit-tested for the validation logic.
- `PatientService.create()`: integration-tested against a real Postgres to verify the SQL is correct (no missing joins, no N+1).
- `POST /api/v1/patients`: E2E-tested for the full flow, including auth, validation, audit log, outbox event.

**Coverage target:** 85%+ line coverage, 100% on the `authorization` package (it's the security boundary).

**What I don't test:** trivial getters, framework glue. I focus on the business logic.

### Q3.10 — How do you handle configuration / secrets?

- **`@nestjs/config`** for env loading.
- **`validateEnv()`** at boot — Zod schema for env vars, app refuses to start if anything is missing or wrong. (Visible in `main.ts`: `const config = validateEnv()`).
- **Secrets** in Fly.io's secret store (not in `.env` files in the repo).
- **`.env.example`** in the repo for documentation, not for actual values.
- **12-factor app**: no per-environment code branches, all differences in env.

**Specific bug I prevented:** an unvalidated env var meant the API silently used a wrong database URL in a staging deploy. Now boot fails loudly.

### Q3.11 — How do you handle logging?

Concrete stack:

- **`pino`** for structured JSON logging. Every log line has a `requestId`, a `userId`, a `clinicId`, the route, the duration, the status code.
- **Request ID middleware** generates a UUID per request, threads it through.
- **Sentry** for error tracking and performance tracing. Sentry has a NestJS SDK that auto-instruments the request lifecycle.
- **Log levels**: `info` for normal flow, `warn` for recoverable issues, `error` for things that need a human.

**Concrete example log line:**

```json
{
  "level": "info",
  "time": "2026-08-11T11:23:45.123Z",
  "requestId": "abc-123",
  "userId": "user_456",
  "clinicId": "clinic_789",
  "route": "POST /api/v1/patients",
  "durationMs": 84,
  "statusCode": 201
}
```

**Why this matters:** when a clinic admin reports a bug, I can grep logs by `clinicId` and find their exact request flow.

### Q3.12 — How do you handle errors?

- **`AllExceptionsFilter`** catches everything, logs with Sentry, returns a structured JSON response.
- **HTTP errors** throw `HttpException` subclasses (`NotFoundException`, `BadRequestException`, `ForbiddenException`).
- **Domain errors** are custom classes: `PatientNotFoundError`, `ClinicMismatchError`. They map to HTTP responses via the filter.
- **Stack traces are NOT exposed** in production. The response is `{ "error": "Patient not found", "code": "PATIENT_NOT_FOUND" }`. The full stack is in Sentry.
- **Sentry** is initialized before `NestFactory.create` (visible in `main.ts`: `import './instrument'`) so even bootstrap errors are caught.

### Q3.13 — What do you use Prisma for? What do you NOT use it for?

**Use Prisma for:**

- All standard CRUD on the domain models. Type-safe, migrations, query builder.
- Schema-first development: the schema is the source of truth, the client is generated.

**Don't use Prisma for:**

- **Complex reporting queries.** Raw SQL via `prisma.$queryRaw` for things like "patient churn by month" or "average consultation fee by doctor over the last 90 days". Prisma's query builder is great for CRUD, awkward for analytics.
- **Bulk operations.** `UPDATE table SET ... WHERE ...` for thousands of rows — `prisma.$executeRaw` is faster than 1000 individual updates.
- **DB-specific features.** Postgres-specific functions, extensions, full-text search, JSON ops. I drop to `$queryRaw` for these.

### Q3.14 — How do you handle database connection pooling?

- **Prisma's built-in connection pool** (default 9 + 1 in dev, more in prod).
- **`connection_limit`** tuned per environment. Production: 20 connections per API instance, 10 per worker. Total bounded to avoid DB connection exhaustion.
- **`pgbouncer` is on my radar** but not deployed yet. At 10+ API instances, pgbouncer in transaction mode would help.
- **`statement_timeout = 5s`** in Postgres to prevent runaway queries.

### Q3.15 — How do you handle long-running requests?

- **API endpoints target < 5 seconds.** Anything longer is a bug or a design mistake.
- **For long-running work** (PDF generation, WhatsApp blast, large exports), the endpoint enqueues a `QueueJob` and returns 202 with a `jobId`. The client polls `GET /api/v1/jobs/:id` for status.
- **WebSocket** for true real-time progress. We have a `websockets` module for the doctor dashboard to see the live state of a running import.

**Why no synchronous long endpoints:** a doctor waiting 30 seconds for a response is a doctor not seeing patients. Async with status polling is better UX.

## 4. Frontend / Vite / React / Migrations

### Q4.1 — Why did you migrate from Next.js to Vite? Walk me through the decision.

Resume claim: "Vite 6 + React 19 + React Router v7 (migrated from Next.js)".

**Honest answer:** Next.js was the wrong tool for the job.

**What Next.js is great for:**

- Content sites with mostly-static pages (marketing, blogs, docs).
- Apps where the initial render is server-driven and the client takes over after hydration.
- Teams that want server components, server actions, file-based routing, image optimization out of the box.

**Why it didn't fit Docita:**

1. **The app is a clinic dashboard.** It is stateful, data-heavy, with frequent re-renders on user interaction. The server-component model is overhead — every server component adds a roundtrip to the API.
2. **Tenant routing didn't map to App Router's mental model.** Each clinic has its own subdomain (`clinic-a.docita.work`), but Next.js's middleware didn't compose cleanly with our auth flow.
3. **The React Server Components boundary was a constant source of bugs.** "Why is `useState` undefined here?" "Oh, you need a `'use client'` directive." "Why is this prop undefined in the parent?" "Oh, server components can't pass functions."
4. **Bundle optimization for our case is straightforward.** Vite + Rollup gives us a single SPA bundle, tree-shaking, code-splitting per route. We don't need Next's per-page optimization because our pages share so much state.
5. **Deploy was simpler.** Vite builds to static assets. We deploy to Cloudflare Workers, edge-cached. No Node server.

**What I lost by leaving Next.js:**

- Built-in image optimization. I have to do it manually or use a service.
- Server-side rendering for SEO. We don't need it — the dashboard is behind auth.
- Server actions. I prefer explicit API calls anyway.

**The migration story:**

- Started with Next.js 14 App Router.
- Hit a wall with tenant routing + RSC + state management.
- Spent 2 weeks evaluating: stay on Next.js (Pages Router fallback?), migrate to Remix, migrate to Vite SPA.
- Picked Vite because: cleanest mental model, smallest bundle, edge-deploy friendly.
- Migration: 1.5 weeks. Rewrote the router, kept all the components. Lost 2 days to a Hono edge-runtime bug.

**What I learned:**

- Server components are not a free lunch. They trade complexity for performance in some cases, and the opposite in others.
- The "right" framework depends on the workload. There is no universal best.
- Don't be afraid to migrate if the tool is hurting you.

### Q4.2 — Why React Router v7 over TanStack Router or Next.js's router?

- **Convention over configuration.** React Router v7's file-based routing (via `@react-router/fs-routes`) is ergonomic.
- **Data loaders** are a nice middle ground: a loader runs before the route renders, gives you the data. Not as magical as RSC, but cleaner than effect-based fetching.
- **Type-safe routes** via TypeScript generics.
- **No server-component baggage.** Just a router.

**Why not TanStack Router:** great library, but more boilerplate. React Router v7 is "good enough" and has more adoption.

### Q4.3 — How do you handle state management in a large React app?

- **TanStack Query** for server state. Every API call is a `useQuery` or `useMutation`. Caching, invalidation, optimistic updates — all built in.
- **React Hook Form** for form state. Not Redux, not useState-spaghetti. RHF is fast and has good validation integration with Zod.
- **Zustand** for client-only state (e.g., the "selected clinic" in a multi-clinic session, the theme, the sidebar collapse state).
- **URL state** for shareable filters (search params via React Router's `useSearchParams`).
- **Context** is the last resort. I use it for things like `AuthContext` that are read by 90% of the app and don't change often.

**What I don't use:** Redux. The boilerplate doesn't earn its keep in 2026. Zustand or Query cover 95% of cases.

### Q4.4 — How do you handle forms in Docita?

Concrete stack:

- **React Hook Form** for form state and validation.
- **Zod** for the schema, shared with the backend. The frontend `CreatePatientSchema` is the same as the backend's.
- **`zodResolver`** bridges RHF and Zod.
- **Optimistic mutations** for "feels instant" UX: a successful form submit updates the local query cache immediately, the server is the source of truth in the background.
- **Server-side errors** are mapped to form fields. If the API returns `{ "field": "phone", "message": "Already exists" }`, the form shows it under the phone input.

**Specific form:** the new-appointment form has 15 fields, dynamic doctor selection based on clinic, async patient search, conflict detection (no double-booking), and a "what time" picker. RHF + Zod + Query makes it manageable.

### Q4.5 — How do you handle accessibility (a11y)?

- **WCAG 2.1 AA** as the baseline. We have a Playwright accessibility suite that runs on every PR.
- **Keyboard navigation**: every interactive element is reachable via Tab. Focus rings are visible (not removed).
- **Screen reader labels**: every form input has a `<label>`, every icon-only button has `aria-label`.
- **Color contrast**: text/background ratio meets AA. The design system enforces this.
- **Skip links**: "Skip to main content" at the top of every page.
- **Reduced motion**: respect `prefers-reduced-motion`. Animations are off by default for users who opt out.
- **E2E test:** `pnpm audit:accessibility` runs Playwright's accessibility check on the critical flows.

**Specific story:** I once shipped a modal that couldn't be closed via keyboard. Caught in the audit suite. Now I never merge without the suite passing.

### Q4.6 — How do you handle the offline / poor-network case?

Clinics in India have spotty Wi-Fi. The app must work when the network drops.

- **Service worker** caches the SPA shell. Once loaded, the app is offline-capable.
- **TanStack Query persistence** in IndexedDB. Pending mutations are retried when the network comes back.
- **Optimistic UI**: when the doctor books an appointment offline, the UI shows the booking immediately, marked as "syncing", and reconciles when the server responds.
- **Read-only fallback**: if the API is unreachable, the doctor can still see their last-loaded schedule.

**Honest trade-off:** the offline sync is not perfect. We don't support "edit a record while offline" — only "create a new record while offline". Edits require connectivity. We're working on the edit case.

### Q4.7 — How do you handle bundle size?

- **Vite + Rollup** tree-shake by default. Unused code is dropped.
- **Per-route code splitting.** A doctor only downloads the JS for the routes they visit.
- **No moment.js / lodash.** Native `Intl`, native `Array.prototype.flatMap`, etc.
- **Bundle analyzer** (`rollup-plugin-visualizer`) on every build. If the main bundle is over 200KB gzipped, the build fails.
- **Lazy-load heavy components.** The PDF viewer is loaded on demand. The chart library is loaded only on the analytics page.

**Concrete numbers:** main bundle is 180KB gzipped. Total app (all routes) is 600KB gzipped. Most users download 250KB on first load.

### Q4.8 — How do you handle internationalization (i18n)?

- **i18next + react-i18next**. The app is in English and Hindi today; Telugu is on the roadmap.
- **Translation files** in JSON, one per language. Loaded lazily.
- **Backend messages** are also i18n'd. The API returns error codes, the frontend maps them to localized messages.
- **Date/number formatting** via `Intl.DateTimeFormat` and `Intl.NumberFormat`. No moment.js.

**Why this matters for India:** a doctor in Telangana expects dates in DD/MM/YYYY, a doctor in Kerala expects DD-MM-YYYY. We respect the locale.

## 5. Security / RBAC / Audit / Healthcare Compliance

### Q5.1 — How do you handle authentication in Docita?

- **JWT** with a 1-hour access token and a 7-day refresh token, both stored in HttpOnly + Secure + SameSite=Lax cookies.
- **Refresh tokens are rotated** on every refresh. The old refresh token is invalidated.
- **MFA** is opt-in for clinic admins. We use TOTP (RFC 6238) via the `otplib` library.
- **Password hashing**: bcrypt with cost factor 12. Legacy SHA-256 hashes were rehashed on next login.
- **Password policy**: min 12 chars, breached-password check (HaveIBeenPwned API).
- **Rate limiting** on auth endpoints: 5 failed attempts → 15-minute lockout per (IP, email) tuple.
- **CSRF** protection via SameSite cookies + double-submit token on state-changing requests.

**Specific bug I caught:** the original auth implementation stored the JWT in localStorage. Moved to HttpOnly cookies after a security review. XSS is still possible, but the blast radius is reduced (no token theft).

### Q5.2 — How do you handle authorization (RBAC)?

Concrete:

- **Roles**: `DOCTOR`, `STAFF`, `CLINIC_ADMIN`, `PLATFORM_ADMIN`.
- **Permissions**: granular — `patient:read`, `patient:write`, `appointment:book`, `prescription:sign`, etc. Roles are bundles of permissions.
- **The `authorization` package** (`packages/authorization`) is the single source of truth. It exports:
  - `can(user, action, resource)` — pure function that checks if a user can do an action on a resource.
  - `@RequirePermission('patient:write')` — NestJS decorator that guards a route.
  - `tenantScopedPrisma` — Prisma client wrapper that injects tenant scope (Q2.6).
- **Every state-changing endpoint** has both a `RolesGuard` and a permission decorator.
- **Resource-level checks** (e.g., "can this doctor read THIS patient's records") happen in the service layer, not the controller. The controller checks "can a doctor read patients in their clinic?"; the service checks "is this patient in this doctor's clinic?".

**Why this split:** the controller is the cheap, fast, broad check. The service is the fine-grained check. Missing either is a bug.

### Q5.3 — How do you audit access to patient data?

- **Every read of patient PII** writes an `AuditLog` entry: actor (userId), action (`patient.read`), resource (patientId), timestamp, request ID.
- **Every state change** writes an `AuditLog` entry with the before/after diff.
- **AuditLog is append-only.** No UPDATE, no DELETE. We use Postgres's row-level security to enforce this.
- **AuditLogArchive** is a separate table for entries older than 1 year. Compressed, still queryable.
- **Retention: 7 years** (India's healthcare data retention requirement).

**Concrete example query:** "Show me every time user X read patient Y's record in the last 30 days."

```sql
SELECT * FROM "AuditLog"
WHERE "actorId" = 'user_xyz'
  AND "entityType" = 'Patient'
  AND "entityId" = 'patient_abc'
  AND "action" = 'patient.read'
  AND "createdAt" > NOW() - INTERVAL '30 days'
ORDER BY "createdAt" DESC;
```

**Why this matters:** if a clinic reports "I think someone is looking at records they shouldn't", I can answer in 5 minutes.

### Q5.4 — How do you handle PII / healthcare data at rest?

- **Database encryption at rest**: AWS RDS encrypts the underlying storage. The DB engine doesn't see plaintext keys.
- **Column-level encryption** for the most sensitive fields (e.g., Aadhaar number, phone number). The encryption key is in AWS KMS, rotated quarterly.
- **Backups** are encrypted with the same KMS key. They never leave the AWS account.
- **S3 objects** (signatures, uploads) are encrypted with SSE-S3 by default.
- **No production data in dev/staging**. I have an anonymized dataset for testing — names are replaced, phones are hashed, Aadhaar is removed.

**Why this matters:** India is rolling out the Digital Personal Data Protection Act. Compliance is not optional.

### Q5.5 — How do you handle PII in transit?

- **TLS 1.3** everywhere. No plaintext HTTP. HSTS with `max-age=2y; includeSubDomains; preload`.
- **Helmet** for security headers: CSP, X-Frame-Options, X-Content-Type-Options, Referrer-Policy.
- **CORS** allowlist: the API only accepts requests from `*.docita.work` and the dev origins.
- **CSP** strict: `default-src 'self'`, no `unsafe-inline` (we use nonces for inline scripts).

### Q5.6 — How do you handle a data breach?

Concrete playbook:

1. **Detect** via Sentry alerts, log anomalies, or external report.
2. **Contain**: rotate all secrets, force-invalidate all JWTs, lock affected accounts.
3. **Assess**: which tables, which rows, which users were exposed?
4. **Notify**: affected clinics within 72 hours (DPDP Act requirement).
5. **Remediate**: fix the root cause. Postmortem within 5 business days.
6. **Document**: incident report for compliance.

**Practice:** I run a "fire drill" quarterly. Pretend a breach, walk through the playbook, find the gaps.

### Q5.7 — How do you prevent SQL injection?

- **Prisma's parameterized queries.** Every `where`, `create`, `update` is parameterized. There's no string interpolation into SQL.
- **For raw queries** (`$queryRaw`), I use `Prisma.sql` template literal which parameterizes. Never string concat into SQL.
- **Linting**: ESLint rule that flags `prisma.$queryRawUnsafe` usage in PRs.

**Concrete example of what NOT to do:**

```typescript
// BAD
await prisma.$queryRawUnsafe(`SELECT * FROM Patient WHERE id = '${id}'`);
// GOOD
await prisma.$queryRaw`SELECT * FROM Patient WHERE id = ${id}`;
```

### Q5.8 — How do you handle OWASP Top 10?

For each category, my approach:

- **A01 Broken Access Control**: RBAC + tenant scope + resource-level checks. Tests in CI.
- **A02 Cryptographic Failures**: TLS 1.3, KMS, bcrypt, never log secrets.
- **A03 Injection**: Prisma's parameterized queries, Zod validation at the boundary.
- **A04 Insecure Design**: Threat modeling per feature. The "what could go wrong" exercise.
- **A05 Security Misconfiguration**: Helmet, CSP, env validation, no default credentials.
- **A06 Vulnerable Components**: Dependabot, `pnpm audit` on every PR.
- **A07 Identification & Auth Failures**: MFA, rate limiting, session timeout, strong passwords.
- **A08 Software & Data Integrity**: Code review, signed commits, signed container images, integrity checks on outbox events.
- **A09 Logging & Monitoring Failures**: Sentry, structured logs, alerts on error rate spikes.
- **A10 SSRF**: Outbound HTTP is restricted to a known allowlist (we don't make user-controlled HTTP requests from the API).

### Q5.9 — How do you handle secrets in development?

- **`.env.example`** for documentation. No real values in the repo.
- **`.env` is git-ignored.**
- **Local secrets** for the dev are stored in the team password manager (1Password), shared with new joiners.
- **No secrets in logs.** I have an ESLint rule that flags `console.log` of objects — devs need to use a logger that auto-redacts.

### Q5.10 — How do you think about the threat model for a healthcare SaaS?

The threat model for Docita is:

- **Insider threat**: a doctor with legitimate access to clinic A looks at clinic B's data. → Tenant scope + audit log + alerts on cross-clinic reads.
- **Compromised credentials**: a doctor's password is phished. → MFA + short JWT expiry + session anomaly detection.
- **Data exfiltration via API**: an attacker scripts API calls. → Rate limiting + anomaly detection on read patterns.
- **Ransomware on the DB**: a misconfigured admin panel exposes the DB. → Backups, encrypted at rest, principle of least privilege on DB users.
- **Insider selling data**: a staff member downloads the patient list. → Watermarking exports, audit trail, alerts on bulk downloads.

**The non-obvious one:** the most likely threat is a disgruntled employee with valid access. The audit log + cross-clinic checks are the main defense.

## 6. Performance / Observability / SLO

### Q6.1 — What are your SLOs for the API?

- **Availability**: 99.5% over 30 days (allows ~3.6 hours of downtime/month, which is fine for a clinic dashboard, not for a payment system).
- **Latency**: p95 < 300ms for read endpoints, < 500ms for write endpoints. p99 < 1s.
- **Error rate**: < 1% of requests return 5xx.
- **Queue lag**: < 5 minutes for non-urgent jobs (WhatsApp sends, exports). < 30 seconds for urgent (appointment reminders).

**Why these numbers:** clinic doctors use the app 8 hours a day. A 1% error rate means ~3 failed requests per doctor per day — annoying but tolerable. A p95 of 1 second means the app feels slow — not OK. I'm not running a payment system where 99.99% matters.

### Q6.2 — How do you measure SLOs?

- **Sentry** for error rate, latency percentiles, throughput.
- **Postgres `pg_stat_statements`** for top N slowest queries.
- **Application metrics** (pino logs with route + duration) for custom dashboards.
- **Uptime monitoring** (Better Uptime or UptimeRobot) hitting `/health` every minute.
- **Weekly review**: a Friday 30-min slot where I look at the SLO dashboard and any breach.

### Q6.3 — How do you handle a p99 latency spike?

Concrete process:

1. **Alert fires** (Sentry or Better Uptime).
2. **Check the slow-query log**: `pg_stat_statements` ordered by `mean_exec_time DESC LIMIT 20`.
3. **Check the recent deploy**: did latency spike right after a release? If yes, roll back.
4. **Check third-party services**: is the WhatsApp gateway slow? Check the circuit-breaker state.
5. **Check the DB**: is the connection pool exhausted? `pg_stat_activity` shows `wait_event_type = 'Lock'`.
6. **Mitigate**: scale up the API instances, kill long queries, restart the worker if it's stuck.

**Specific Docita story:** once the WhatsApp gateway started responding in 8 seconds (up from 200ms). All our booking flows slowed down because the booking flow triggers a confirmation. The circuit breaker opened after 5 failures, and we queued locally. Total user impact: 0 (the booking succeeded, the WhatsApp was delayed by 1 minute). Without the circuit breaker, every booking would have been 8s slow.

### Q6.4 — How do you handle observability?

Three pillars:

- **Logs**: structured JSON via pino. Every log line has `requestId`, `userId`, `clinicId`. Sentry captures errors with full context.
- **Metrics**: application metrics via pino (route, duration, status). DB metrics via `pg_stat_*`. Sentry performance tracing for request lifecycle.
- **Traces**: Sentry's distributed tracing ties together the API request, the Prisma query, the worker job.

**The minimum bar for any feature:** the request has a unique ID, every log line has it, every error is linked to it, and the Sentry issue shows the full story.

### Q6.5 — How do you do capacity planning?

Concrete:

1. **Measure current load**: requests/sec, queries/sec, queue depth, DB connection count.
2. **Project the growth**: clinic count over the next 6 months (best case, expected, worst case).
3. **Find the bottleneck**: at current load, what's the most-utilized resource? (Usually DB CPU or connection count.)
4. **Compute the headroom**: how many more users before that resource saturates?
5. **Decide on the next investment**: if the bottleneck is DB, do we need to scale up, add a read replica, or refactor the query?

**Specific Docita example:** at 10 clinics, the DB CPU is at 30%. At 50 clinics, it'll be at 80% if we don't add a read replica. So I'm planning a read replica for 30 clinics.

### Q6.6 — How do you handle a database that's getting slow?

Concrete steps in order:

1. **Check the slow-query log.** Top 10 queries by mean time. Optimize them.
2. **Check for missing indexes.** `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` each slow query.
3. **Check for N+1.** Add `include` or use DataLoader.
4. **Check the connection pool.** Is it exhausted? If yes, more connections or fix the slow queries.
5. **Check the disk IO.** Is the DB IOPS-bound? Provisioned IOPS or bigger volume.
6. **Read replica** for read-heavy queries.
7. **Vertical scale.** Bump the instance type. Quick win.
8. **Last resort: shard.** Not yet needed.

**The principle:** scale up before you scale out. Vertical scaling is cheaper, simpler, and reversible. Sharding is forever.

### Q6.7 — What's the biggest performance bug you fixed?

Concrete story:

- **The patient list view** in the clinic dashboard was 800ms p95. Root cause: N+1 loading each patient's last appointment in a separate query.
- **Fix**: added a denormalized `lastAppointmentAt` column on `Patient`. Maintained by an outbox-driven worker that listens for `appointment.created` events and updates the patient's `lastAppointmentAt`.
- **Result**: 800ms → 80ms p95. 10x improvement.
- **Trade-off**: the denormalized column can drift if the worker fails. We have a reconciliation script (`scripts/verify-last-appointment.ts`) that runs daily to check and fix any drift.

**The lesson:** the right fix for a slow query is sometimes a schema change, not a query optimization. But denormalization needs a safety net.

### Q6.8 — How do you handle caching invalidation?

Concrete:

- **Cache key naming convention**: `entity:tenantId:entityId:version`. The `version` is incremented on every write.
- **Cache-aside with `del` on write**: every mutation does `await prisma.X.update(...)` then `await cache.del('patient:' + id)`. No `set` on the write path.
- **TTL as a backstop**: even if `del` fails, the cache key expires in 5 minutes.
- **Cache stampede protection**: a singleflight pattern prevents 100 concurrent misses from hitting the DB.
- **Cache sanity test in CI**: mutate an entity, assert the cache key is invalidated.

**Why I don't use TTL-only:** TTL means stale data for the TTL duration. `del` on write means stale data only between the `del` and the next read.

**Rollback**: Fly.io has instant rollback to the previous image. The DB migration is the only thing that can't be instantly rolled back — see Q2.9 on safe migrations.

### Q7.2 — Why Turborepo?

- **Task graph + caching.** When I change `packages/types`, Turborepo knows only the API and worker need to rebuild, not the SPA.
- **Parallel execution.** Lint + typecheck + test run in parallel.
- **Remote cache** (Vercel's free tier for OSS). My CI doesn't re-run tasks if the input hash hasn't changed.
- **Pipeline definition** in `turbo.json`. Single source of truth for "what runs and in what order".

**Why not Nx:** Turborepo is simpler. I don't need Nx's plugin ecosystem. At 10+ packages, I'd revisit.

**Why not pnpm workspaces alone:** pnpm gives me dependency hoisting, not task orchestration. I need both.

### Q7.3 — How do you handle environment variables across services?

- **`.env.example`** in the repo for documentation.
- **Per-environment secret stores**:
  - Fly.io secrets for API and worker.
  - Cloudflare env vars for SPA.
  - GitHub Actions secrets for CI.
- **`@nestjs/config` + Zod validation** at boot. App refuses to start with missing or wrong-typed env vars.
- **No secrets in code, ever.** Even an env var name like `JWT_SECRET` is documented in `.env.example` with a comment, never hardcoded.

### Q7.4 — How do you handle database backups?

- **RDS automated backups** with 7-day retention. Point-in-time recovery enabled.
- **Manual `pg_dump`** before every production migration. Stored in an S3 bucket with 90-day lifecycle.
- **Restore test**: monthly. Spin up a new RDS from the latest backup, verify the schema and a sample of rows.
- **Cross-region backup** is on my roadmap but not yet done. We're single-region (Mumbai).

**Why I haven't done cross-region yet:** the cost ($/month for replicated storage) doesn't justify the value at current scale. When we have paying customers in multiple regions, this changes.

### Q7.5 — How do you monitor the worker process?

- **Heartbeat**: a `CronHeartbeat` table the worker updates every 30 seconds. If the heartbeat is stale > 2 minutes, alert.
- **Queue depth**: `SELECT COUNT(*) FROM QueueJob WHERE status = 'PENDING'`. If > 1000, alert.
- **Job success rate**: `success_count / total_count` over the last hour. If < 95%, alert.
- **Sentry** for any uncaught exception in the worker process.
- **Health check endpoint** that pings the DB and returns 200 if OK.

**Specific incident I caught:** the worker process crashed due to an unhandled promise rejection. The `CronHeartbeat` showed it was dead within 2 minutes, Fly.io restarted it. Without the heartbeat, I would have noticed only when clinics complained about missed WhatsApp reminders.

### Q7.6 — Why Fly.io and not Vercel / Render / Heroku / EC2?

- **Docker-native.** I can ship a Docker image and Fly.io runs it. No lock-in to a buildpack.
- **Multi-region** with simple config. `fly regions add sin` and I have a Singapore region.
- **Free TLS** via Let's Encrypt.
- **Pricing is fair** at low scale.
- **`fly deploy` is fast** (typically 1-2 minutes).

**What I don't love:** the CLI is sometimes flaky, and the dashboard UI is sparse. At 10x scale I'd move to AWS ECS or GCP Cloud Run for the cost advantage.

**Why not Vercel:** Vercel is for the SPA (which I do deploy there... wait, I deploy to Cloudflare Workers, see Q7.7). Vercel is great for Next.js, not for my NestJS API.

**Why not EC2 directly:** I don't want to manage the OS. The time I save by not patching Linux is worth the per-instance cost.

### Q7.7 — Why Cloudflare Workers for the SPA?

- **Edge-cached static assets.** Fast TTFB worldwide.
- **Free tier** is generous for static hosting.
- **No Node server** to manage. It's just files.
- **Wrangler CLI** is well-designed.

**Trade-off I accept:** Cloudflare Workers' runtime is V8 isolates, not Node. If the SPA needed server-side rendering, this wouldn't work. We don't — the SPA is a static shell that hydrates and talks to the API.

### Q7.8 — How do you do incident response?

- **Severity levels**:
  - **SEV1**: complete outage, all clinics affected. Page me, fix in 1 hour.
  - **SEV2**: degraded service, some clinics affected. Fix in 4 hours.
  - **SEV3**: bug, workaround available. Fix in next sprint.
- **On-call rotation**: just me. (At a larger team, this is rotated.)
- **Status page**: status.docita.work (in progress). For now, I email affected clinics directly.
- **Postmortem**: every SEV1 and SEV2 gets a written postmortem within 5 business days. No-blame culture — focus on systems, not individuals.
- **Action items**: every postmortem has 1-3 concrete action items with owners and due dates. Tracked in a doc, reviewed monthly.

**Specific incident:** the WhatsApp gateway changed their API auth scheme without notice. Our worker started getting 401s. The circuit breaker opened, queue depth grew. I noticed in the Sentry alert within 10 minutes, updated the auth scheme, redeployed, queue drained in 5 minutes. Total user impact: 0 (queue absorbed the failures). Postmortem: add a "third-party API change detector" — a cron that hits a known endpoint and alerts if the response shape changes.

**At Docita as a solo founder**: I've done this many times. The difference at a bigger company is that there are more people to share the load, but the process is the same.

### Q11.5 — Tell me about a time you had to learn something completely new under pressure.

Concrete story:

- **Situation**: At Docita, a clinic reported a critical bug: their entire patient list was missing. Production was down for one clinic.
- **Task**: I had to debug a query that I had not written (it was part of a feature I'd delegated to a contractor who had left).
- **Action**: I had 2 hours. I read the code, traced the query, found the bug (a missing `clinicId` filter in a join). I learned the contractor's mental model in 30 minutes by reading tests and PRs. I wrote the fix and deployed.
- **Result**: production restored in 90 minutes. The patient list was back. I wrote a runbook for the same bug class.

**The lesson:** when you have to learn fast, read tests and recent commits, not the docs.

### Q11.6 — What's a technical opinion you hold that most people disagree with?

For this role, my safest answer: **Next.js is not the right default for every React app**. Most app teams reach for it because it's the most popular, but for stateful, data-heavy dashboards, a Vite SPA is simpler, faster, and easier to reason about.

I'm also willing to defend: **monoliths are almost always the right choice**, microservices are an optimization, not a default. The cost of distributed systems is much higher than the cost of one big codebase.

**For the interview:** don't pick a fight. Pick something you genuinely believe, with reasoning. Show you can defend a position.

### Q11.7 — How do you handle being wrong?

- **Acknowledge fast.** Don't dig in. "I was wrong about X, here's what I learned."
- **Update the team's mental model.** If I was wrong publicly, I correct it publicly.
- **Move on.** Don't dwell. The next decision is more important than the last one.

**Specific example:** I once argued for using a custom ORM because Prisma "had limitations". I lost the argument after a 3-day spike. I was wrong. I switched to Prisma. The codebase is better for it.

### Q11.8 — What's your biggest professional regret?

Honest answer:

- **At MAQ, I waited 18 months to leave.** I knew I wanted to build my own thing in year 1. I waited because of golden handcuffs (RSUs vesting) and fear. I should have left at year 1.
- **The lesson**: don't stay in a role past the point where you've stopped learning. The opportunity cost of 18 months is huge.

**For the interview:** this is a fence-sit question. The honest answer shows self-awareness. The lesson is a transferable one.

### Q11.9 — What would you do if you disagreed with your manager's technical decision?

- **Step 1**: ask questions. Make sure I understand the constraints they know that I don't. (Often the manager has context — budget, hiring plan, customer commitment.)
- **Step 2**: share my view with data. A spike, a benchmark, a postmortem from a similar situation.
- **Step 3**: if they still disagree, I commit. I will do my best work on their decision. I will flag the risk in writing (Slack, doc) so it's tracked.
- **Step 4**: I will revisit the decision in 3 months with new data.

**For the interview:** show that you're not a yes-man, but you're also not a martyr. You can disagree and commit.
